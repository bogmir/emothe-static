⸢ACT 3⸣

⸢Scene 1⸣

⸢With Titania still asleep onstage⸣, enter the Clowns, ⸢Bottom, Quince, Snout, Starveling, Snug, and Flute.⸣

BOTTOM

Are we all met?

QUINCE

Pat, pat. And here’s a marvels convenient place for our rehearsal. This green plot shall be our stage, this hawthorn brake our tiring-house, and we will do it in action as we will do it before the Duke.

BOTTOM

Peter Quince?

QUINCE

What sayest thou, bully Bottom?

BOTTOM

There are things in this comedy of Pyramus and Thisbe that will never please. First, Pyramus must draw a sword to kill himself, which the ladies cannot abide. How answer you that?

SNOUT

By ’r lakin, a parlous fear.

STARVELING

I believe we must leave the killing out, when all is done.

BOTTOM

Not a whit! I have a device to make all well. Write me a prologue, and let the prologue seem to say we will do no harm with our swords and that Pyramus is not killed indeed. And, for the more better assurance, tell them that I, Pyramus, am not Pyramus, but Bottom the weaver. This will put them out of fear.

QUINCE

Well, we will have such a prologue, and it shall be written in eight and six.

BOTTOM

No, make it two more. Let it be written in eight and eight.

SNOUT

Will not the ladies be afeard of the lion?

STARVELING

I fear it, I promise you.

BOTTOM

Masters, you ought to consider with yourself, to bring in (God shield us!) a lion among ladies is a most dreadful thing. For there is not a more fearful wildfowl than your lion living, and we ought to look to ’t.

SNOUT

Therefore another prologue must tell he is not a lion.

BOTTOM

Nay, you must name his name, and half his face must be seen through the lion’s neck, and he himself must speak through, saying thus, or to the same defect: “Ladies,” or “Fair ladies, I would wish you,” or “I would request you,” or “I would entreat you not to fear, not to tremble! My life for yours. If you think I come hither as a lion, it were pity of my life. No, I am no such thing. I am a man as other men are.” And there indeed let him name his name and tell them plainly he is Snug the joiner.

QUINCE

Well, it shall be so. But there is two hard things: that is, to bring the moonlight into a chamber, for you know Pyramus and Thisbe meet by moonlight.

SNOUT

Doth the moon shine that night we play our play?

BOTTOM

A calendar, a calendar! Look in the almanac. Find out moonshine, find out moonshine.

⸢Quince takes out a book⸣.

QUINCE

Yes, it doth shine that night.

BOTTOM

Why, then, may you leave a casement of the great chamber window, where we play, open, and the moon may shine in at the casement.

QUINCE

Ay, or else one must come in with a bush of thorns and a lantern and say he comes to disfigure or to present the person of Moonshine. Then there is another thing: we must have a wall in the great chamber, for Pyramus and Thisbe, says the story, did talk through the chink of a wall .

SNOUT

You can never bring in a wall . What say you, Bottom?

BOTTOM

Some man or other must present Wall . And let him have some plaster, or some loam, or some roughcast about him to signify wall, or let him hold his fingers thus, and through that cranny shall Pyramus and Thisbe whisper.

QUINCE

If that may be, then all is well. Come, sit down, every mother’s son, and rehearse your parts. Pyramus, you begin. When you have spoken your speech, enter into that brake, and so everyone according to his cue.

TITANIA

127Mine ear is much enamored of thy note,
128So is mine eye enthrallèd to thy shape,
129And thy fair virtue’s force perforce doth move me

BOTTOM

⸢Scene 2⸣

Enter ⸢Oberon,⸣ King of Fairies.

OBERON

1I wonder if Titania be awaked;
2Then what it was that next came in her eye,
3Which she must dote on in extremity.
4⸢Enter Robin Goodfellow.⸣ Here comes my messenger. How now, mad spirit?
5What night-rule now about this haunted grove?

ROBIN

6My mistress with a monster is in love.
7Near to her close and consecrated bower,
8While she was in her dull and sleeping hour,
9A crew of patches, rude mechanicals,
10That work for bread upon Athenian stalls,
11Were met together to rehearse a play
12Intended for great Theseus’ nuptial day.
13The shall owest thick-skin of that barren sort,
14Who Pyramus presented in their sport,
15Forsook his scene and entered in a brake.
16When I did him at this advantage take,
17An ass’s noll I fixèd on his head.
18Anon his Thisbe must be answerèd,
19And forth my ⸢mimic⸣comes. When they him spy,
20As wild geese that the creeping fowler eye,
21Or russet-pated choughs, many in sort,
22Rising and cawing at the gun’s report,
23Sever themselves and madly sweep the sky,
24So at his sight away his fellows fly,
25And, at our stamp, here o’er and o’er one falls.
26He “Murder” cries and help from Athens calls.
27Their sense thus weak, lost with their fears thus strong,
28Made senseless things begin to do them wrong;
29For briers and thorns at their apparel snatch,
30Some sleeves, some hats, from yielders all things catch.
31I led them on in this distracted fear
32And left sweet Pyramus translated there.
33When in that moment, so it came to pass,
34Titania waked and straightway loved an ass.

OBERON

35This falls out better than I could devise.
36But hast thou yet latched the Athenian’s eyes
37With the love juice, as I did bid thee do?

ROBIN

38I took him sleeping—that is finished, too—
39And the Athenian woman by his side,
40That, when he waked, of force she must be eyed.

HERMIA

LYSANDER

122Why should you think that I should woo in scorn?
123Scorn and derision never come in tears.
124Look when I vow, I weep; and vows so born,
125In their nativity all truth appears.
126How can these things in me seem scorn to you,
127Bearing the badge of faith to prove them true?

HELENA

128You do advance your cunning more and more.
129When truth kills truth, O devilish holy fray!
130These vows are Hermia’s. Will you give her o’er?
131Weigh oath with oath and you will nothing weigh.
132Your vows to her and me, put in two scales,

139Crystal is muddy. O, how ripe in show
140Thy lips, those kissing cherries, tempting grow!

291“Puppet”? Why so? Ay, that way goes the game.
292Now I perceive that she hath made compare
293Between our statures; she hath urged her height,
294And with her personage, her tall personage,
295Her height, forsooth, she hath prevailed with him.
296And are you grown so high in his esteem
297Because I am so dwarfish and so low?
298How low am I, thou painted maypole? Speak!
299How low am I? I am not yet so low
300But that my nails can reach unto thine eyes.

HELENA

301I pray you, though you mock me, ⸢gentlemen,⸣
302Let her not hurt me. I was never curst;
303I have no gift at all in shrewishness.
304I am a right maid for my cowardice.
305Let her not strike me. You perhaps may think,
306Because she is something lower than myself,
307That I can match her.

HERMIA

That I can match her. “Lower”? Hark, again!

HELENA

308Good Hermia, do not be so bitter with me.
309I evermore did love you, Hermia,
310Did ever keep your counsels, never wronged you—
311Save that, in love unto Demetrius,
312I told him of your stealth unto this wood.
313He followed you; for love, I followed him.
314But he hath chid me hence and threatened me
315To strike me, spurn me, nay, to kill me too.
316And now, so you will let me quiet go,
317To Athens will I bear my folly back
318And follow you no further. Let me go.
319You see how simple and how fond I am.

HERMIA

320Why, get you gone. Who is ’t that hinders you?

HELENA

321A foolish heart that I leave here behind.

HERMIA

322What, with Lysander?

HELENA

What, with Lysander? With Demetrius.

LYSANDER

323Be not afraid. She shall not harm thee, Helena.

DEMETRIUS

324No, sir, she shall not, though you take her part.

HELENA

325O, when she is angry, she is keen and shrewd.
326She was a vixen when she went to school,
327And though she be but little, she is fierce.

HERMIA

328“Little” again? Nothing ⸢but⸣ “low” and “little”?
329Why will you suffer her to flout me thus?
330Let me come to her.

LYSANDER

Let me come to her. Get you gone, you dwarf,
331You minimus of hind’ring knotgrass made,
332You bead, you acorn—

DEMETRIUS

You bead, you acorn— You are too officious
333In her behalf that scorns your services.
334Let her alone. Speak not of Helena.
335Take not her part. For if thou dost intend
336Never so little show of love to her,
337Thou shalt aby it.

LYSANDER

Thou shalt aby it. Now she holds me not.
338Now follow, if thou dar’st, to try whose right,
339Of thine or mine, is most in Helena.

DEMETRIUS

340“Follow”? Nay, I’ll go with thee, cheek by jowl.

⸢Demetrius and Lysander exit.⸣

HERMIA

341You, mistress, all this coil is long of you.

⸢Helena retreats.⸣

342Nay, go not back.

HELENA

Nay, go not back. I will not trust you, I,
343Nor longer stay in your curst company.
344Your hands than mine are quicker for a fray.
345My legs are longer though, to run away.

⸢She exits.⸣

HERMIA

346I am amazed and know not what to say.

⸢She exits.⸣

OBERON

347⸢to Robin⸣ This is thy negligence. Still thou mistak’st,
348Or else committ’st thy knaveries willfully.

ROBIN

349Believe me, king of shadows, I mistook.
350Did not you tell me I should know the man
351By the Athenian garments he had on?
352And so far blameless proves my enterprise
353That I have ’nointed an Athenian’s eyes;
354And so far am I glad it so did sort,
355As this their jangling I esteem a sport.

OBERON

356Thou seest these lovers seek a place to fight.
357Hie, therefore, Robin, overcast the night;
358The starry welkin cover thou anon
359With drooping fog as black as Acheron,
360And lead these testy rivals so astray
361As one come not within another’s way.
362Like to Lysander sometime frame thy tongue;
363Then stir Demetrius up with bitter wrong.
364And sometime rail thou like Demetrius.
365And from each other look thou lead them thus,
366Till o’er their brows death-counterfeiting sleep
367With leaden legs and batty wings doth creep.
368Then crush this herb into Lysander’s eye,

⸢He gives a flower to Robin.⸣

369Whose liquor hath this virtuous property,
370To take from thence all error with his might
371And make his eyeballs roll with wonted sight.
372When they next wake, all this derision
373Shall seem a dream and fruitless vision.
374And back to Athens shall the lovers wend,
375With league whose date till death shall never end.
376Whiles I in this affair do thee employ,
377I’ll to my queen and beg her Indian boy;
378And then I will her charmèd eye release
379From monster’s view, and all things shall be peace.

ROBIN

380My Fairy lord, this must be done with haste,
381For night’s swift dragons cut the clouds full fast,
382And yonder shines Aurora’s harbinger,
383At whose approach, ghosts wand’ring here and there
384Troop home to churchyards. Damnèd spirits all,
385That in crossways and floods have burial,
386Already to their wormy beds are gone.
387For fear lest day should look their shames upon,
388They willfully themselves exile from light
389And must for aye consort with black-browed night.

OBERON

390But we are spirits of another sort.
391I with the Morning’s love have oft made sport
392And, like a forester, the groves may tread
393Even till the eastern gate, all fiery red,
394Opening on Neptune with fair blessèd beams,
395Turns into yellow gold his salt-green streams.
396But notwithstanding, haste! Make no delay.
397We may effect this business yet ere day.

⸢He exits.⸣

ROBIN

398Up and down, up and down,
399I will lead them up and down.
400I am feared in field and town.
401Goblin, lead them up and down.
402Here comes one.

Enter Lysander.

LYSANDER

403Where art thou, proud Demetrius? Speak thou now.

ROBIN

404⸢in Demetrius’ voice⸣ Here, villain, drawn and ready. Where art thou?

LYSANDER

405I will be with thee straight.

ROBIN

406⸢in Demetrius’ voice⸣ Follow me, then, to plainer ground.

⸢Lysander exits.⸣

Enter Demetrius.

DEMETRIUS

407Lysander, speak again.
408Thou run away, thou coward, art thou fled?
409Speak! In some bush? Where dost thou hide thy head?

ROBIN

410⸢in Lysander’s voice⸣ Thou coward, art thou bragging to the stars,
411Telling the bushes that thou look’st for wars,
412And wilt not come? Come, recreant! Come, thou child!
413I’ll whip thee with a rod. He is defiled
414That draws a sword on thee.

DEMETRIUS

That draws a sword on thee. Yea, art thou there?

ROBIN

415⸢in Lysander’s voice⸣ Follow my voice. We’ll try no manhood here.

⸢They exit.⸣

⸢Enter Lysander.⸣

LYSANDER

416He goes before me and still dares me on.
417When I come where he calls, then he is gone.
418The villain is much lighter-heeled than I.
419I followed fast, but faster he did fly,
420That f ALL en am I in dark uneven way,
421And here will rest me. Come, thou gentle day,
422For if but once thou show me thy gray light,
423I’ll find Demetrius and revenge this spite.

⸢He lies down and sleeps.⸣

⸢Enter⸣ Robin and Demetrius.

ROBIN

424⸢in Lysander’s voice⸣ Ho, ho, ho! Coward, why com’st thou not?

DEMETRIUS

425Abide me, if thou dar’st, for well I wot
426Thou runn’st before me, shifting every place,
427And dar’st not stand nor look me in the face.
428Where art thou now?

ROBIN

Where art thou now? ⸢in Lysander’s voice⸣ Come hither. I am here.

DEMETRIUS

429Nay, then, thou mock’st me. Thou shalt buy this dear
430If ever I thy face by daylight see.
431Now go thy way. Faintness constraineth me
432To measure out my length on this cold bed.
433By day’s approach look to be visited.

⸢He lies down and sleeps.⸣

Enter Helena.

HELENA

434O weary night, O long and tedious night,
435Abate thy hours! Shine, comforts, from the east,
436That I may back to Athens by daylight
437From these that my poor company detest.
438And sleep, that sometimes shuts up sorrow’s eye,
439Steal me awhile from mine own company.

⸢She lies down and⸣sleeps.

ROBIN

440Yet but three? Come one more.
441Two of both kinds makes up four.
442Here she comes, curst and sad.
443Cupid is a knavish lad
444Thus to make poor females mad.

⸢Enter Hermia.⸣

HERMIA

445Never so weary, never so in woe,
446Bedabbled with the dew and torn with briers,
447I can no further crawl, no further go.
448My legs can keep no pace with my desires.
449Here will I rest me till the break of day.
450Heavens shield Lysander if they mean a fray!

⸢She lies down and sleeps.⸣

ROBIN

451On the ground
452Sleep sound.
453I’ll apply
454⸢To⸣ your eye,
455Gentle lover, remedy.
456⸢Robin applies the nectar to Lysander’s eyes.⸣ When thou wak’st,
457Thou tak’st
458True delight
459In the sight
460Of thy former lady’s eye.
461And the country proverb known,
462That every man should take his own,
463In your waking shall be shown.
464Jack shall have Jill;
465Naught shall go ill;
466The man shall have his mare again, and all shall be well

⸢He exits.⸣
