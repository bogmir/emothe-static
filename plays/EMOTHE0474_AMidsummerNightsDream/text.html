⸢Scene 1⸣

Enter Theseus, Hippolyta, ⸢and Philostrate,⸣ with others.

THESEUS

1Now, fair Hippolyta, our nuptial hour
2Draws on apace. Four happy days bring in
3Another moon. But, O, methinks how slow
4This old moon ⸢wanes!⸣ She lingers my desires
5Like to a stepdame or a dowager
6Long withering out a young man’s revenue.

HIPPOLYTA

7Four days will quickly steep themselves in night;
8Four nights will quickly dream away the time;
9And then the moon, like to a silver bow
10⸢New⸣-bent in heaven, shall behold the night
11Of our solemnities.

THESEUS

12Go, Philostrate,
13Stir up the Athenian youth to merriments.
14Awake the pert and nimble spirit of mirth.
15Turn melancholy forth to funerals;
16The pale companion is not for our pomp.

⸢Philostrate exits.⸣

17Hippolyta, I wooed thee with my sword
18And won thy love doing thee injuries,
19But I will wed thee in another key,
20With pomp, with triumph, and with reveling.

Enter Egeus and his daughter Hermia, and Lysander and Demetrius.

EGEUS

21Happy be Theseus, our renownèd duke!

THESEUS

22Thanks, good Egeus. What’s the news with thee?

EGEUS

23Full of vexation come I, with complaint
24Against my child, my daughter Hermia.—
25Stand forth, Demetrius.—My noble lord,
26This man hath my consent to marry her.—
27Stand forth, Lysander.—And, my gracious duke,
28This man hath bewitched the bosom of my child.—
29Thou, thou, Lysander, thou hast given her rhymes
30And interchanged love tokens with my child.
31Thou hast by moonlight at her window sung
32With feigning voice verses of feigning love
33And stol’n the impression of her fantasy
34With bracelets of thy hair, rings, gauds, conceits,
35Knacks, trifles, nosegays, sweetmeats—messengers
36Of strong prevailment in unhardened youth.
37With cunning hast thou filched my daughter’s heart,
38Turned her obedience (which is due to me)
39To stubborn harshness.—And, my gracious duke,
40Be it so she will not here before your Grace
41Consent to marry with Demetrius,
42I beg the ancient privilege of Athens:
43As she is mine, I may dispose of her,
44Which shall be either to this gentleman
45Or to her death, according to our law
46Immediately provided in that case.

THESEUS

47What say you, Hermia? Be advised, fair maid.
48To you, your father should be as a god,
49One that composed your beauties, yea, and one
50To whom you are but as a form in wax
51By him imprinted, and within his power
52To leave the figure or disfigure it.
53Demetrius is a worthy gentleman.

HERMIA

54So is Lysander.

THESEUS

So is Lysander. In himself he is,
55But in this kind, wanting your father’s voice,
56The other must be held the worthier.

HERMIA

57I would my father looked but with my eyes.

THESEUS

58Rather your eyes must with his judgment look.

HERMIA

59I do entreat your Grace to pardon me.
60I know not by what power I am made bold,
61Nor how it may concern my modesty
62In such a presence here to plead my thoughts;
63But I beseech your Grace that I may know
64The worst that may befall me in this case
65If I refuse to wed Demetrius.

THESEUS

66Either to die the death or to abjure
67Forever the society of men.
68Therefore, fair Hermia, question your desires,
69Know of your youth, examine well your blood,
70Whether (if you yield not to your father’s choice)
71You can endure the livery of a nun,
72For aye to be in shady cloister mewed,
73To live a barren sister all your life,
74Chanting faint hymns to the cold fruitless moon.
75Thrice-blessèd they that master so their blood
76To undergo such maiden pilgrimage,
77But earthlier happy is the rose distilled
78Than that which, withering on the virgin thorn,
79Grows, lives, and dies in single blessedness.

HERMIA

80So will I grow, so live, so die, my lord,
81Ere I will yield my virgin patent up
82Unto his Lordship whose unwishèd yoke
83My soul consents not to give sovereignty.

THESEUS

84Take time to pause, and by the next new moon
85(The sealing day betwixt my love and me
86For everlasting bond of fellowship),
87Upon that day either prepare to die
88For disobedience to your father’s will,
89Or else to wed Demetrius, as he would,
90Or on Diana’s altar to protest
91For aye austerity and single life.

DEMETRIUS

92Relent, sweet Hermia, and, Lysander, yield
93Thy crazèd title to my certain right.

LYSANDER

94You have her father’s love, Demetrius.
95Let me have Hermia’s. Do you marry him.

EGEUS

96Scornful Lysander, true, he hath my love;
97And what is mine my love shall render him.
98And she is mine, and all my right of her
99I do estate unto Demetrius.

LYSANDER

100⸢to Theseus⸣ I am, my lord, as well derived as he,
101As well possessed. My love is more than his;
102My fortunes every way as fairly ranked
103(If not with vantage) as Demetrius’;
104And (which is more than all these boasts can be)
105I am beloved of beauteous Hermia.
106Why should not I then prosecute my right?
107Demetrius, I’ll avouch it to his head,
108Made love to Nedar’s daughter, Helena,
109And won her soul; and she, sweet lady, dotes,
110Devoutly dotes, dotes in idolatry,
111Upon this spotted and inconstant man.

THESEUS

112I must confess that I have heard so much,
113And with Demetrius thought to have spoke thereof;
114But, being overfull of self-affairs,
115My mind did lose it.—But, Demetrius, come,
116And come, Egeus; you shall go with me.
117I have some private schooling for you both.—
118For you, fair Hermia, look you arm yourself
119To fit your fancies to your father’s will,
120Or else the law of Athens yields you up
121(Which by no means we may extenuate)
122To death or to a vow of single life.—
123Come, my Hippolyta. What cheer, my love?—
124Demetrius and Egeus, go along.
125I must employ you in some business
126Against our nuptial and confer with you
127Of something nearly that concerns yourselves.

EGEUS

128With duty and desire we follow you.

⸢All but Hermia and Lysander⸣ exit.

LYSANDER

129How now, my love? Why is your cheek so pale?
130How chance the roses there do fade so fast?

HERMIA

131Belike for want of rain, which I could well
132Beteem them from the tempest of my eyes.

LYSANDER

133Ay me! For aught that I could ever read,
134Could ever hear by tale or history,
135The course of true love never did run smooth.
136But either it was different in blood—

HERMIA

137O cross! Too high to be enthralled to ⸢low⸣.

LYSANDER

138Or else misgraffèd in respect of years—

HERMIA

139O spite! Too old to be engaged to young.

LYSANDER

140Or else it stood upon the choice of friends—

HERMIA

141O hell, to choose love by another’s eyes!

LYSANDER

142Or, if there were a sympathy in choice,
143War, death, or sickness did lay siege to it,
144Making it momentany as a sound,
145Swift as a shadow, short as any dream,
146Brief as the lightning in the collied night,
147That, in a spleen, unfolds both heaven and Earth,
148And, ere a man hath power to say “Behold!”
149The jaws of darkness do devour it up.
150So quick bright things come to confusion.

HERMIA

151If then true lovers have been ever crossed,
152It stands as an edict in destiny.
153Then let us teach our trial patience
154Because it is a customary cross,
155As due to love as thoughts and dreams and sighs,
156Wishes and tears, poor fancy’s followers.

LYSANDER

157A good persuasion. Therefore, hear me, Hermia:
158I have a widow aunt, a dowager
159Of great revenue, and she hath no child.
160From Athens is her house remote seven leagues,
161And she respects me as her only son.
162There, gentle Hermia, may I marry thee;
163And to that place the sharp Athenian law
164Cannot pursue us. If thou lovest me, then
165Steal forth thy father’s house tomorrow night,
166And in the wood a league without the town
167(Where I did meet thee once with Helena
168To do observance to a morn of May),
169There will I stay for thee.

HERMIA

There will I stay for thee. My good Lysander,
170I swear to thee by Cupid’s strongest bow,
171By his best arrow with the golden head,
172By the simplicity of Venus’ doves,
173By that which knitteth souls and prospers loves,
174And by that fire which burned the Carthage queen
175When the false Trojan under sail was seen,
176By all the vows that ever men have broke
177(In number more than ever women spoke),
178In that same place thou hast appointed me,
179Tomorrow truly will I meet with thee.

LYSANDER

180Keep promise, love. Look, here comes Helena.

Enter Helena.

HERMIA

181Godspeed, fair Helena. Whither away?

HELENA

182Call you me “fair”? That “fair” again unsay.
183Demetrius loves your fair. O happy fair!
184Your eyes are lodestars and your tongue’s sweet air
185More tunable than lark to shepherd’s ear
186When wheat is green, when hawthorn buds appear.
187Sickness is catching. O, were favor so!
188⸢Yours would⸣ I catch, fair Hermia, ere I go.
189My ear should catch your voice, my eye your eye;
190My tongue should catch your tongue’s sweet melody.
191Were the world mine, Demetrius being bated,
192The rest ⸢I’d⸣ give to be to you translated.
193O, teach me how you look and with what art
194You sway the motion of Demetrius heart!

HERMIA

195I frown upon him, yet he loves me still.

HELENA

196O, that your frowns would teach my smiles such skill!

HERMIA

197I give him curses, yet he gives me love.

HELENA

198O, that my prayers could such affection move!

HERMIA

199The more I hate, the more he follows me.

HELENA

200The more I love, the more he hateth me.

HERMIA

201His folly, Helena, is no fault of mine.

HELENA

202None but your beauty. Would that fault were mine!

HERMIA

203Take comfort: he no more shall see my face.
204Lysander and myself will fly this place.
205Before the time I did Lysander see
206Seemed Athens as a paradise to me.
207O, then, what graces in my love do dwell
208That he hath turned a heaven unto a hell!

LYSANDER

209Helen, to you our minds we will unfold.
210Tomorrow night when Phoebe doth behold
211Her silver visage in the wat’ry glass,
212Decking with liquid pearl the bladed grass
213(A time that lovers’ flights doth still conceal),
214Through Athens’ gates have we devised to steal.

HERMIA

215And in the wood where often you and I
216Upon faint primrose beds were wont to lie,
217Emptying our bosoms of their counsel ⸢sweet,⸣
218There my Lysander and myself shall meet
219And thence from Athens turn away our eyes
220To seek new friends and ⸢stranger companies.⸣
221Farewell, sweet playfellow. Pray thou for us,
222And good luck grant thee thy Demetrius.—
223Keep word, Lysander. We must starve our sight
224From lovers’ food till morrow deep midnight.

LYSANDER

225I will, my Hermia

(Hermia exits.)

I will, my Hermia Helena, a dieu.
226As you on him, Demetrius dote on you!

Lysander exits.

HELENA

227How happy some o’er other some can be!
228Through Athens I am thought as fair as she.
229But what of that? Demetrius thinks not so.
230He will not know what all but he do know.
231And, as he errs, doting on Hermia’s eyes,
232So I, admiring of his qualities.
233Things base and vile, holding no quantity,
234Love can transpose to form and dignity.
235Love looks not with the eyes but with the mind;
236And therefore is winged Cupid painted blind.
237Nor hath Love’s mind of any judgment taste.
238Wings, and no eyes, figure unheedy haste.
239And therefore is Love said to be a child
240Because in choice he is so oft beguiled.
241As waggish boys in game themselves forswear,
242So the boy Love is perjured everywhere.
243For, ere Demetrius looked on Hermia’s eyne,
244He hailed down oaths that he was only mine;
245And when this hail some heat from Hermia felt,
246So he dissolved, and show’rs of oaths did melt.
247I will go tell him of fair Hermia ‘s flight.
248Then to the wood will he tomorrow night
249Pursue her. And, for this intelligence
250If I have thanks, it is a dear expense.
251But herein mean I to enrich my pain,
252To have his sight thither and back again.

She exits.

Enter Quince the carpenter, and Snug the joiner, and Bottom the weaver, and Flute the bellows-mender, and Snout the tinker, and Starveling the tailor.

QUINCE

Is all our company here?

BOTTOM

You were best to call them generally, man by man, according to the scrip.

QUINCE

Here is the scroll of every man’s name which is thought fit, through all Athens, to play in our interlude before the Duke and the Duchess on his wedding day at night.

BOTTOM

First, good Peter Quince, say what the play treats on, then read the names of the actors, and so grow to a point.

QUINCE

Marry, our play is “The most lamentable comedy and most cruel death of Pyramus and Thisbe.”

BOTTOM

A very good piece of work, I assure you, and a merry. Now, good Peter Quince, call forth your actors by the scroll. Masters, spread yourselves.

QUINCE

Answer as I call you. Nick Bottom, the weaver.

BOTTOM

Ready. Name what part I am for, and proceed.

QUINCE

You, Nick Bottom, are set down for Pyramus.

BOTTOM

What is Pyramus—a lover or a tyrant?

QUINCE

BOTTOM

QUINCE

Robin Starveling, the tailor.

STARVELING

Here, Peter Quince.

QUINCE

Robin Starveling, you must play Thisbe’s mother.—Tom Snout, the tinker.

SNOUT

Here, Peter Quince.

QUINCE

You, Pyramus’ father.—Myself, Thisbe’s father.—Snug the joiner, you the lion’s part.— And I hope here is a play fitted.

SNUG

Have you the lion’s part written? Pray you, if it be, give it me, for I am slow of study.

QUINCE

You may do it extempore, for it is nothing but roaring.

BOTTOM

Let me play the lion too. I will roar that I will do any man’s heart good to hear me. I will roar that I will make the Duke say “Let him roar again. Let him roar again!”

QUINCE

An you should do it too terribly, you would fright the Duchess and the ladies that they would shriek, and that were enough to hang us all.

ALL

That would hang us, every mother’s son.

BOTTOM

I grant you, friends, if you should fright the ladies out of their wits, they would have no more discretion but to hang us. But I will aggravate my voice so that I will roar you as gently as any sucking dove. I will roar you an ’twere any nightingale.

QUINCE

You can play no part but Pyramus, for Pyramus is a sweet-faced man, a proper man as one shall see in a summer’s day, a most lovely gentlemanlike man. Therefore you must needs play Pyramus.

BOTTOM

Well, I will undertake it. What beard were I best to play it in?

QUINCE

Why, what you will.

BOTTOM

I will discharge it in either your straw-color beard, your orange-tawny beard, your purple-in-grain beard, or your French-crown-color beard, your perfit yellow.

QUINCE

Some of your French crowns have no hair at all, and then you will play barefaced. But, masters, here are your parts, ⸢giving out the parts ⸣, and I am to entreat you, request you, and desire you to con them by tomorrow night and meet me in the palace wood, a mile without the town, by moonlight. There will we rehearse, for if we meet in the city, we shall be dogged with company and our devices known. In the meantime I will draw a bill of properties such as our play wants. BOTTOM

QUINCE

At the Duke’s Oak we meet.

BOTTOM

Enough. Hold or cut bowstrings.

They exit.

ROBIN

1How now, spirit? Whither wander you?

FAIRY

2Over hill, over dale,
3Thorough bush, thorough brier,
4Over park, over pale,
5Thorough flood, thorough fire;
6I do wander everywhere,
7Swifter than the moon’s sphere.
8And I serve the Fairy Queen,
9To dew her orbs upon the green.
10The cowslips tall her pensioners be;
11In their gold coats spots you see;
12Those be rubies, Fairy favors;
13In those freckles live their savors.
14I must go seek some dewdrops here
15And hang a pearl in every cowslip’s ear.
16Farewell, thou lob of spirits. I’ll be gone.
17Our queen and all her elves come here anon.

ROBIN

18The King doth keep his revels here tonight.
19Take heed the Queen come not within his sight,
20For Oberon is passing fell and wrath
21Because that she, as her attendant, hath
22A lovely boy stolen from an Indian king;
23She never had so sweet a changeling.
24And jealous Oberon would have the child
25Knight of his train, to trace the forests wild.
26But she perforce withholds the lovèd boy,
27Crowns him with flowers and makes him all her joy.
28And now they never meet in grove or green,
29By fountain clear or spangled starlight sheen,
30But they do square, that all their elves for fear
31Creep into acorn cups and hide them there.

FAIRY

32Either I mistake your shape and making quite,
33Or else you are that shrewd and knavish sprite
34Called Robin Goodfellow. Are not you he
35That frights the maidens of the villagery,
36Skim milk, and sometimes labor in the quern
37And bootless make the breathless huswife churn,
38And sometime make the drink to bear no barm,
39Mislead night wanderers, laughing at their harm?
40Those that “Hobgoblin” call you and “sweet Puck,”
41You do their work, and they shall have good luck.
42Are not you he?

ROBIN

Are not you he? Thou speakest aright.
43I am that merry wanderer of the night.
44I jest to Oberon and make him smile
45When I a fat and bean-fed horse beguile,
46Neighing in likeness of a filly foal.
47And sometime lurk I in a gossip’s bowl
48In very likeness of a roasted crab,
49And, when she drinks, against her lips I bob
50And on her withered dewlap pour the ale.
51The wisest aunt, telling the saddest tale,
52Sometime for three-foot stool mistaketh me;
53Then slip I from her bum, down topples she

OBERON

82And never, since the middle summer’s spring,
83Met we on hill, in dale, forest, or mead,
84By pavèd fountain or by rushy brook,
85Or in the beachèd margent of the sea,
86To dance our ringlets to the whistling wind,
87But with thy brawls thou hast disturbed our sport.
88Therefore the winds, piping to us in vain,
89As in revenge have sucked up from the sea
90Contagious fogs, which, falling in the land,
91Hath every pelting river made so proud
92That they have overborne their continents.
93The ox hath therefore stretched his yoke in vain,
94The plowman lost his sweat, and the green corn
95Hath rotted ere his youth attained a beard.
96The fold stands empty in the drownèd field,
97And crows are fatted with the murrain flock.
98The nine-men’s-morris is filled up with mud,
99And the quaint mazes in the wanton green,
100For lack of tread, are undistinguishable.
101The human mortals want their winter here.
102No night is now with hymn or carol blessed.
103Therefore the moon, the governess of floods,
104Pale in her anger, washes all the air,
105That rheumatic diseases do abound.

TITANIA

OBERON

166It fell upon a little western flower,

170The juice of it on sleeping eyelids laid
171Will make or man or woman madly dote
172Upon the next live creature that it sees.
173Fetch me this herb, and be thou here again
174Ere the leviathan can swim a league.

ROBIN

175I’ll put a girdle round about the Earth

No epilogue, I pray you. For your play needs no excuse. Never excuse. For when the players are all dead, there need none to be blamed. Marry, if he that writ it had played Pyramus and hanged himself in Thisbe’s garter, it would have been a fine tragedy; and so it is, truly, and very notably discharged. But, come, your Bergomask.
