⸢ACT 5⸣

⸢Scene 1⸣

Enter Theseus, Hippolyta, and Philostrate, ⸢Lords, and Attendants.⸣

HIPPOLYTA

1’Tis strange, my Theseus, that these lovers speak of.

THESEUS

2More strange than true. I never may believe
3These antique fables nor these Fairy toys.
4Lovers and madmen have such seething brains,
5Such shaping fantasies, that apprehend
6More than cool reason ever comprehends.
7The lunatic, the lover, and the poet
8Are of imagination all compact.
9One sees more devils than vast hell can hold:
10That is the madman. The lover, all as frantic,
11Sees Helen’s beauty in a brow of Egypt.
12The poet’s eye, in a fine frenzy rolling,
13Doth glance from heaven to Earth, from Earth to heaven,
14And as imagination bodies forth
15The forms of things unknown, the poet’s pen
16Turns them to shapes and gives to airy nothing
17A local habitation and a name.
18Such tricks hath strong imagination
19That, if it would but apprehend some joy,
20It comprehends some bringer of that joy.
21Or in the night, imagining some fear,
22How easy is a bush supposed a bear!

HIPPOLYTA

23But all the story of the night told over,
24And all their minds transfigured so together,
25More witnesseth than fancy’s images
26And grows to something of great constancy,
27But, howsoever, strange and admirable.

Enter Lovers: LysanderDemetrius, Hermia, and Helena.

THESEUS

28Here come the lovers full of joy and mirth.—
29Joy, gentle friends! Joy and fresh days of love
30Accompany your hearts!

LYSANDER

Accompany your hearts! More than to us
31Wait in your royal walks, your board, your bed!

THESEUS

32Come now, what masques, what dances shall we have
33To wear away this long age of three hours
34Between our after-supper and bedtime?
35Where is ⸢our⸣ usual manager of mirth?
36What revels are in hand? Is there no play
37To ease the anguish of a torturing hour?
38Call Philostrate.

PHILOSTRATE

Call Philostrate. ⸢coming forward⸣ Here, mighty Theseus

THESEUS

39Say what abridgment have you for this evening,
40What masque, what music? How shall we beguile
41The lazy time if not with some delight?

PHILOSTRATE

42⸢giving Theseus a paper⸣ There is a brief how many sports are ripe.
43Make choice of which your Highness will see first.

THESEUS

44“The battle with the Centaurs, to be sung
45By an Athenian eunuch to the harp.”
46We’ll none of that. That have I told my love
47In glory of my kinsman Hercules.
48“The riot of the tipsy Bacchanals,
49Tearing the Thracian singer in their rage.”
50That is an old device, and it was played
51When I from Thebes came last a conqueror.
52“The thrice-three Muses mourning for the death
53Of learning, late deceased in beggary.”
54That is some satire, keen and critical,
55Not sorting with a nuptial ceremony.
56“A tedious brief scene of young Pyramus
57And his love Thisbe, very tragical mirth.”
58“Merry” and “tragical”? “Tedious” and “brief”?
59That is hot ice and wondrous strange snow!
60How shall we find the concord of this discord?

PHILOSTRATE

61A play there is, my lord, some ten words long
62(Which is as brief as I have known a play),
63But by ten words, my lord, it is too long,
64Which makes it tedious; for in all the play,
65There is not one word apt, one player fitted.
66And tragical, my noble lord, it is.
67For Pyramus therein doth kill himself,
68Which, when I saw rehearsed, I must confess,
69Made mine eyes water; but more merry tears
70The passion of loud laughter never shed.

THESEUS

71What are they that do play it?

PHILOSTRATE

72Hard-handed men that work in Athens here,
73Which never labored in their minds till now,
74And now have toiled their unbreathed memories
75With this same play, against your nuptial.

THESEUS

76And we will hear it.

PHILOSTRATE

And we will hear it. No, my noble lord,
77It is not for you. I have heard it over,
78And it is nothing, nothing in the world,
79Unless you can find sport in their intents,
80Extremely stretched and conned with cruel pain
81To do you service.

THESEUS

To do you service. I will hear that play,
82For never anything can be amiss
83When simpleness and duty tender it.
84Go, bring them in—and take your places, ladies.

⸢Philostrate exits⸣.

HIPPOLYTA

85I love not to see wretchedness o’ercharged,
86And duty in his service perishing.

THESEUS

87Why, gentle sweet, you shall see no such thing.

HIPPOLYTA

88He says they can do nothing in this kind.

THESEUS

89The kinder we, to give them thanks for nothing.
90Our sport shall be to take what they mistake;
91And what poor duty cannot do, noble respect
92Takes it in might, not merit.
93Where I have come, great clerks have purposèd
94To greet me with premeditated welcomes,
95Where I have seen them shiver and look pale,
96Make periods in the midst of sentences,
97Throttle their practiced accent in their fears,
98And in conclusion dumbly have broke off,
99Not paying me a welcome. Trust me, sweet,
100Out of this silence yet I picked a welcome,
101And in the modesty of fearful duty,
102I read as much as from the rattling tongue
103Of saucy and audacious eloquence.
104Love, therefore, and tongue-tied simplicity
105In least speak most, to my capacity.

⸢Enter Philostrate.⸣

PHILOSTRATE

106So please your Grace, the Prologue is addressed.

THESEUS

107Let him approach.

Enter the Prologue.

PROLOGUE

108If we offend, it is with our goodwill.
109That you should think we come not to offend,
110To show our simple skill,
111That is the true beginning of our end.
112Consider, then, we come but in despite.
113We do not come, as minding to content you,
114Our true intent is. All for your delight
115We are not here. That you should here repent you,
116The actors are at hand, and, by their show,
117You shall know all that you are like to know.

⸢Prologue exits.⸣

THESEUS

This fellow doth not stand upon points.

LYSANDER

He hath rid his prologue like a rough colt; he knows not the stop. A good moral, my lord: it is not enough to speak, but to speak true.

HIPPOLYTA

Indeed he hath played on this prologue like a child on a recorder—a sound, but not in government.

THESEUS

His speech was like a tangled chain—nothing impaired, but all disordered. Who is next?

Enter Pyramus ⸢(Bottom),⸣ and Thisbe ⸢(Flute),⸣ and Wall ⸢(Snout),⸣ and Moonshine ⸢(Starveling),⸣ and Lion ⸢(Snug),⸣ and Prologue ⸢(Quince)⸣.

QUINCE

126(as Prologue) Gentles, perchance you wonder at this show.
127But wonder on, till truth make all things plain.
128This man is Pyramus, if you would know.
129This beauteous lady Thisbe is certain.
130This man with lime and roughcast doth present
131“Wall,” that vile wall which did these lovers sunder;
132And through Wall’s chink, poor souls, they are content
133To whisper, at the which let no man wonder.
134This man, with lantern, dog, and bush of thorn,
135Presenteth “Moonshine,” for, if you will know,
136By moonshine did these lovers think no scorn
137To meet at Ninus’ tomb, there, there to woo.
138This grisly beast (which “Lion” hight by name)
139The trusty Thisbe coming first by night
140Did ⸢scare⸣away or rather did affright;
141And, as she fled, her mantle she did fall,
142Which Lion vile with bloody mouth did stain.
143Anon comes Pyramus, sweet youth and tall,
144And finds his trusty Thisbe’s mantle slain.
145Whereat, with blade, with bloody blameful blade,
146He bravely broached his boiling bloody breast.
147And Thisbe, tarrying in mulberry shade,
148His dagger drew, and died. For all the rest,
149Let Lion, Moonshine, Wall , and lovers twain
150At large discourse, while here they do remain.

THESEUS

I wonder if the lion be to speak.

DEMETRIUS

No wonder, my lord. One lion may when many asses do.

Lion, Thisbe, Moonshine, ⸢and Prologue⸣ exit.

SNOUT

153(as Wall) In this same interlude it doth befall
154That I, one ⸢Snout⸣ by name, present a wall ;
155And such a wall as I would have you think
156That had in it a crannied hole or chink,
157Through which the lovers, Pyramus and Thisbe,
158Did whisper often, very secretly.
159This loam, this roughcast, and this stone doth show
160That I am that same wall . The truth is so.
161And this the cranny is, right and sinister,
162Through which the fearful lovers are to whisper.

THESEUS

Would you desire lime and hair to speak better?

DEMETRIUS

It is the wittiest partition that ever I heard discourse, my lord.

THESEUS

Pyramus draws near the wall . Silence.

BOTTOM

166(as Pyramus) O grim-looked night! O night with hue so black!
167O night, which ever art when day is not!
168O night! O night! Alack, alack, alack!
169I fear my Thisbe’s promise is forgot.
170And thou, O wall , O sweet, O lovely wall ,
171That stand’st between her father’s ground and mine,
172Thou wall , O wall , O sweet and lovely wall,
173Show me thy chink to blink through with mine eyne.
174Thanks, courteous wall . Jove shield thee well for this.
175But what see I? No Thisbe do I see.
176O wicked wall , through whom I see no bliss,
177Cursed be thy stones for thus deceiving me!

THESEUS

The wall , methinks, being sensible, should curse again.

BOTTOM

No, in truth, sir, he should not. “Deceiving me” is Thisbe’s cue. She is to enter now, and I am to spy her through the wall . You shall see it will fall pat as I told you. Yonder she comes.

Enter Thisbe ⸢(Flute).⸣

FLUTE

183(as Thisbe) O wall , full often hast thou heard my moans
184For parting my fair Pyramus and me.
185My cherry lips have often kissed thy stones,
186Thy stones with lime and hair knit up in thee.

BOTTOM

187(as Pyramus) I see a voice! Now will I to the chink
188To spy an I can hear my Thisbe’s face.
189Thisbe?

FLUTE

Thisbe? (as Thisbe) My love! Thou art my love, I think.

BOTTOM

190(as Pyramus) Think what thou wilt, I am thy lover’s grace,
191And, like Limander, am I trusty still.

FLUTE

192(as Thisbe) And I like Helen, till the Fates me kill.

BOTTOM

193(as Pyramus) Not Shafalus to Procrus was so true.

FLUTE

194(as Thisbe) As Shafalus to Procrus, I to you.

BOTTOM

195(as Pyramus) O kiss me through the hole of this vile wall.

FLUTE

196(as Thisbe) I kiss the wall ’s hole, not your lips at all .

BOTTOM

197(as Pyramus) Wilt thou at Ninny’s tomb meet me straightway?

FLUTE

198(as Thisbe) ’Tide life, ’tide death, I come without delay.

⸢Bottom and Flute exit.⸣

SNOUT

199(as Wall) Thus have I, Wall , my part dischargèd so,
200And, being done, thus Wall away doth go.

⸢He exits.⸣

THESEUS

Now is the ⸢wall down⸣ between the two neighbors.

DEMETRIUS

No remedy, my lord, when walls are so willful to hear without warning.

HIPPOLYTA

This is the silliest stuff that ever I heard.

THESEUS

The best in this kind are but shadows, and the worst are no worse, if imagination amend them.

HIPPOLYTA

It must be your imagination, then, and not theirs.

THESEUS

If we imagine no worse of them than they of themselves, they may pass for excellent men. Here come two noble beasts in, a man and a lion.

Enter Lion ⸢(Snug)⸣ and Moonshine ⸢(Starveling)⸣.

SNUG

211⸢as Lion⸣ You ladies, you whose gentle hearts do fear
212The smallest monstrous mouse that creeps on floor,
213May now perchance both quake and tremble here,
214When lion rough in wildest rage doth roar.
215Then know that I, as Snug the joiner, am
216A lion fell, nor else no lion’s dam;
217For if I should as lion come in strife
218Into this place, ’twere pity on my life.

THESEUS

A very gentle beast, and of a good conscience.

DEMETRIUS

The very best at a beast, my lord, that e’er I saw.

LYSANDER

This lion is a very fox for his valor.

THESEUS

True, and a goose for his discretion.

DEMETRIUS

Not so, my lord, for his valor cannot carry his discretion, and the fox carries the goose.

THESEUS

His discretion, I am sure, cannot carry his valor, for the goose carries not the fox. It is well. Leave it to his discretion, and let us listen to the Moon.

STARVELING

(as Moonshine) This lanthorn doth the hornèd moon present.

DEMETRIUS

He should have worn the horns on his head.

THESEUS

He is no crescent, and his horns are invisible within the circumference.

STARVELING

(as Moonshine) This lanthorn doth the hornèd moon present. Myself the man i’ th’ moon do seem to be.

THESEUS

This is the greatest error of all the rest; the man should be put into the lanthorn. How is it else “the man i’ th’ moon”?

DEMETRIUS

He dares not come there for the candle, for you see, it is already in snuff.

HIPPOLYTA

I am aweary of this moon. Would he would change.

THESEUS

It appears by his small light of discretion that he is in the wane; but yet, in courtesy, in all reason, we must stay the time.

LYSANDER

Proceed, Moon.

STARVELING

(as Moonshine) All that I have to say is to tell you that the lanthorn is the moon, I the man i’ th’ moon, this thornbush my thornbush, and this dog my dog.

DEMETRIUS

Why, all these should be in the lanthorn, for all these are in the moon. But silence. Here comes Thisbe.

Enter Thisbe ⸢(Flute).⸣

FLUTE

248(as Thisbe) This is old Ninny’s tomb. Where is my love?

SNUG

(as Lion) O!

⸢The Lion roars. Thisbe runs off, dropping her mantle.⸣

DEMETRIUS

Well roared, Lion.

THESEUS

Well run, Thisbe.

HIPPOLYTA

Well shone, Moon. Truly, the Moon shines with a good grace.

⸢Lion worries the mantle.⸣

THESEUS

Well moused, Lion.

Enter Pyramus ⸢(Bottom).⸣

DEMETRIUS

And then came Pyramus.

⸢Lion exits.⸣

LYSANDER

And so the lion vanished.

BOTTOM

256(as Pyramus) Sweet Moon, I thank thee for thy sunny beams.
257I thank thee, Moon, for shining now so bright,
258For by thy gracious, golden, glittering ⸢gleams,⸣
259I trust to take of truest Thisbe sight.—
260But stay! O spite!
261But mark, poor knight,
262What dreadful dole is here!
263Eyes, do you see!
264How can it be!
265O dainty duck! O dear!
266Thy mantle good—
267What, stained with blood?
268Approach, ye Furies fell!
269O Fates, come, come,
270Cut thread and thrum,
271Quail, crush, conclude, and quell!

THESEUS

272This passion, and the death of a dear friend,
273would go near to make a man look sad.

HIPPOLYTA

274Beshrew my heart but I pity the man.

BOTTOM

275(as Pyramus) O, wherefore, Nature, didst thou lions frame,
276Since lion vile hath here deflowered my dear,
277Which is—no, no—which was the fairest dame
278That lived, that loved, that liked, that looked with cheer?
279Come, tears, confound!
280Out, sword, and wound
281The pap of Pyramus;
282Ay, that left pap,
283Where heart doth hop.

⸢Pyramus stabs himself.⸣

284Thus die I, thus, thus, thus.
285Now am I dead;
286Now am I fled;
287My soul is in the sky.
288Tongue, lose thy light!
289Moon, take thy flight!

⸢Moonshine exits.⸣

290Now die, die, die, die, die.

⸢Pyramus falls.⸣

DEMETRIUS

No die, but an ace for him, for he is but one.

LYSANDER

Less than an ace, man, for he is dead, he is nothing.

THESEUS

With the help of a surgeon he might yet recover and yet prove an ass.

HIPPOLYTA

How chance Moonshine is gone before Thisbe comes back and finds her lover?

THESEUS

She will find him by starlight. ⸢Enter Thisbe (Flute).⸣ Here she comes, and her passion ends the play.

HIPPOLYTA

Methinks she should not use a long one for such a Pyramus. I hope she will be brief.

DEMETRIUS

A mote will turn the balance, which Pyramus, which Thisbe, is the better: he for a man, God warrant us; she for a woman, God bless us.

LYSANDER

She hath spied him already with those sweet eyes.

DEMETRIUS

And thus she means, videlicet —

FLUTE

306(as Thisbe) Asleep, my love?
307What, dead, my dove?
308O Pyramus, arise!
309Speak, speak. Quite dumb?
310Dead? Dead? A tomb
311Must cover thy sweet eyes.
312These lily lips,
313This cherry nose,
314These yellow cowslip cheeks
315Are gone, are gone!
316Lovers, make moan;
317His eyes were green as leeks.
318O Sisters Three,
319Come, come to me
320With hands as pale as milk.
321Lay them in gore,
322Since you have shore
323With shears his thread of silk.
324Tongue, not a word!
325Come, trusty sword,
326Come, blade, my breast imbrue!

⸢Thisbe stabs herself.⸣

327And farewell, friends.
328Thus Thisbe ends.
329Adieu, adieu, adieu.

⸢Thisbe falls.⸣

THESEUS

Moonshine and Lion are left to bury the dead.

DEMETRIUS

Ay, and Wall too.

⸢Bottom and Flute arise.⸣

BOTTOM

No, I assure you, the wall is down that parted their fathers. Will it please you to see the Epilogue or to hear a Bergomask dance between two of our company?

THESEUS

No epilogue, I pray you. For your play needs no excuse. Never excuse. For when the players are all dead, there need none to be blamed. Marry, if he that writ it had played Pyramus and hanged himself in Thisbe’s garter, it would have been a fine tragedy; and so it is, truly, and very notably discharged. But, come, your Bergomask. Let your epilogue alone.

⸢Dance, and the players exit.⸣

344The iron tongue of midnight hath told twelve.
345Lovers, to bed! ’Tis almost Fairy time.
346I fear we shall outsleep the coming morn
347As much as we this night have overwatched.
348This palpable-gross play hath well beguiled
349The heavy gait of night. Sweet friends, to bed.
350A fortnight hold we this solemnity
351In nightly revels and new jollity.

They exit.

Enter ⸢Robin Goodfellow.⸣

ROBIN

352Now the hungry ⸢lion⸣ roars,
353And the wolf ⸢behowls⸣ the moon,
354Whilst the heavy plowman snores,
355All with weary task fordone.
356Now the wasted brands do glow,
357Whilst the screech-owl, screeching loud,
358Puts the wretch that lies in woe
359In remembrance of a shroud.
360Now it is the time of night
361That the graves, all gaping wide,
362Every one lets forth his sprite
363In the church-way paths to glide.
364And we fairies, that do run
365By the triple Hecate’s team
366From the presence of the sun,
367Following darkness like a dream,
368Now are frolic. Not a mouse
369Shall disturb this hall owed house.
370I am sent with broom before,
371To sweep the dust behind the door.

Enter ⸢Oberon and Titania,⸣ King and Queen of Fairies, with all their train.

OBERON

372Through the house give glimmering light,
373By the dead and drowsy fire.
374Every elf and Fairy sprite,
375Hop as light as bird from brier,
376And this ditty after me,
377Sing and dance it trippingly.

TITANIA

378First rehearse your song by rote,
379To each word a warbling note.
380Hand in hand, with Fairy grace,
381Will we sing and bless this place.

⸢Oberon leads the Fairies in song and dance.⸣

OBERON

382Now, until the break of day,
383Through this house each Fairy stray.
384To the best bride-bed will we,
385Which by us shall blessèd be,
386And the issue there create
387Ever shall be fortunate.
388So shall all the couples three
389Ever true in loving be,
390And the blots of Nature’s hand
391Shall not in their issue stand.
392Never mole, harelip, nor scar,
393Nor mark prodigious, such as are
394Despisèd in nativity,
395Shall upon their children be.
396With this field-dew consecrate
397Every Fairy take his gait,
398And each several chamber bless,
399Through this palace, with sweet peace.
400And the owner of it blest,
401Ever shall in safety rest.
402Trip away. Make no stay.
403Meet me all by break of day.

⸢All but Robin⸣ exit.

ROBIN

404If we shadows have offended,
405Think but this and all is mended:
406That you have but slumbered here
407While these visions did appear.
408And this weak and idle theme,
409No more yielding but a dream,
410Gentles, do not reprehend.
411If you pardon, we will mend.
412And, as I am an honest Puck,
413If we have unearnèd luck
414Now to ’scape the serpent’s tongue,
415We will make amends ere long.
416Else the Puck a liar call.
417So good night unto you all .
418Give me your hands, if we be friends,
419And Robin shall restore amends.

⸢He exits.⸣
