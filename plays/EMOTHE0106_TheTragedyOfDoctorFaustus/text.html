Christopher Marlowe

The Tragedy of Doctor Faustus

Act I

[Prologue]

Enter CHORUS.

Chorus

1Not marching in the fields of Trasimene
2Where Mars did mate the warlike Carthagens,
3Nor sporting in the dalliance of love
4In courts of kings where state is overturned,
5Nor in the pomp of proud audacious deeds,
6Intends our muse to vaunt his heavenly verse.
7Only this, gentles: we must now perform
8The form of Faustus' fortunes, good or bad.
9And now to patient judgements we appeal,
10And speak for Faustus in his infancy.
11Now is he born, of parents base of stock,
12In Germany, within a town called Rhode.
13At riper years to Wittenberg he went,
14Whereas his kinsmen chiefly brought him up.
15So much he profits in divinity
16That shortly he was graced with doctor's name,
17Excelling all, and sweetly can dispute
18In th'heavenly matters of theology;
19Till, swoll'n with cunning of a self-conceit,
20His waxen wings did mount above his reach,
21And, melting, heavens conspired his overthrow.
22For, falling to a devilish exercise,
23And glutted now with learning's golden gifts,
24He surfeits upon cursèd necromancy;
25Nothing so sweet as magic is to him,
26Which he prefers before his chiefest bliss.
27And this the man that in his study sits.

[Exit.]

[I.i]

FAUSTUS in his study.

Faustus

28Settle thy studies, Faustus, and begin
29To sound the depth of that thou wilt profess.
30Having commenced, be a divine in show,
31Yet level at the end of every art,
32And live and die in Aristotle's works.
33Sweet Analytics , 'tis thou hast ravished me!

[He reads.]

34Bene disserere est finis logices.
35Is to dispute well logic's chiefest end?
36Affords this art no greater miracle?
37Then read no more; thou hast attained that end.
38A greater subject fitteth Faustus's wit.
39Bid Oeconomy farewell, and Galen, come!
40Be a physician, Faustus. Heap up gold,
41And be eternised for some wondrous cure.

[He reads.]

42Summum bonum medicinae sanitas :
43The end of physic is our body's health.
44Why Faustus, hast thou not attained that end?
45Are not thy bills hung up as monuments,
46Whereby whole cities have escaped the plague
47And thousand desperate maladies been cured?
48Yet art thou still but Faustus, and a man.
49Couldst thou make men to live eternally,
50Or, being dead, raise them to life again,
51Then this profession were to be esteemed.
52Physic, farewell! Where is Justinian?

[He reads.]

53Si una eademque res legatur duobus,
54Alter rem, alter valorem rei, etc.
55A petty case of paltry legacies!

[He reads.]

56Exhaereditare filium non potest pater nisi —
57Such is the subject of the Institute
58And universal body of the law.
59This study fits a mercenary drudge
60Who aims at nothing but external trash—
61Too servile and illiberal for me.
62When all is done, divinity is best.
63Jerome's Bible, Faustus, view it well.

[He reads.]

64Stipendium peccati mors est . Ha!
65Stipendium, etc.
66The reward of sin is death? That's hard.

[He reads.]

67Si peccasse negamus, fallimur
68Et nulla est in nobis veritas.
69If we say that we have no sin,
70We deceive ourselves, and there is no truth in us.
71Why then belike we must sin,
72And so consequently die.
73Ay, we must die an everlasting death.
74What doctrine call you this? Che serà, serà :
75What will be, shall be. Divinity, adieu!

[He picks up a book of magic]

76These metaphysics of magicians
77And necromantic books are heavenly,
78Lines, circles, letters, characters—
79Ay, these are those that Faustus most desires.
80O, what a world of profit and delight,
81Of power, of honour, and omnipotence
82Is promised to the studious artisan!
83All things that move between the quiet poles
84Shall be at my command. Emperors and kings
85Are but obeyed in their several provinces,
86But his dominion that exceeds in this
87Stretcheth as far as doth the mind of man.
88A sound magician is a demigod.
89Here, tire my brains to get a deity.
90Wagner!

Enter WAGNER

Wagner! Commend me to my dearest friends,
91The German Valdes and Cornelius.
92Request them earnestly to visit me.

Wagner

93I will, sir.

Exit.

Faustus

94Their conference will be a greater help to me
95Than all my labours, plod I ne'er so fast.

Enter the [GOOD] ANGEL and SPIRIT, [the BAD ANGEL].

Good Angel

96O Faustus, lay that damnèd book aside
97And gaze not on it, lest it tempt thy soul
98And heap God's heavy wrath upon thy head!
99Read, read the Scriptures. That is blasphemy.

Bad Angel

100Go forward, Faustus, in that famous art
101Wherein all nature's treasure is contained.
102Be thou on earth as Jove is in the sky,
103Lord and commander of these elements.

Exeunt ANGELS.

Faustus

104How am I glutted with conceit of this!
105Shall I make spirits fetch me what I please?
106Resolve me of all ambiguities?
107Perform what desperate enterprise I will?
108I'll have them fly to India for gold,
109Ransack the ocean for orient pearl,
110And search all corners of the new-found world
111For pleasant fruits and princely delicates.
112I'll have them read me strange philosophy
113And tell the secrets of all foreign kings.
114I'll have them wall all Germany with brass
115And make swift Rhine circle fair Wittenberg.
116I'll have them fill the public schools with silk,
117Wherewith the students shall be bravely clad.
118I'll levy soldiers with the coin they bring
119And chase the Prince of Parma from our land,
120And reign sole king of all the provinces;
121Yea, stranger engines for the brunt of war
122Than was the fiery keel at Antwerp bridge
123I'll make my servile spirits to invent.
124Come, German Valdes and Cornelius,
125And make me blest with your sage conference!

Enter VALDES and CORNELIUS.

126Valdes, sweet Valdes, and Cornelius,
127Know that your words have won me at the last
128To practise magic and concealèd arts.
129Philosophy is odious and obscure;
130Both law and physic are for petty wits;
131'Tis magic, magic that hath ravished me.
132Then, gentle friends, aid me in this attempt,
133And I, that have with subtle syllogisms
134Gravelled the pastors of the German Church
135And made the flow'ring pride of Wittenberg
136Swarm to my problems as th'infernal spirits
137On sweet Musaeus when he came to hell,
138Will be as cunning as Agrippa was,
139Whose shadow made all Europe honour him.

Valdes

140Faustus, these books, thy wit, and our experience
141Shall make all nations to canonise us.
142As Indian Moors obey their Spanish lords,
143So shall the spirits of every element
144Be always serviceable to us three.
145Like lions shall they guard us when we please,
146Like Almaine rutters with their horsemen's staves,
147Or Lapland giants, trotting by our sides;
148Sometimes like women, or unwedded maids,
149Shadowing more beauty in their airy brows
150Than has the white breasts of the Queen of Love.
151From Venice shall they drag huge argosies,
152And from America the golden fleece
153That yearly stuffed old Philip's treasury,
154If learnèd Faustus will be resolute.

Faustus

155Valdes, as resolute am I in this
156As thou to live. Therefore object it not

Cornelius

157The miracles that magic will perform
158Will make thee vow to study nothing else.
159Faustus may try his cunning by himself.
160He that is grounded in astrology,
161Enriched with tongues, well seen in minerals,
162Hath all the principles magic doth require.
163Then doubt not, Faustus, but to be renowned
164And more frequented for this mystery
165Than heretofore the Delphian oracle.
166The spirits tell me they can dry the sea
167And fetch the treasure of all foreign wrecks—
168Yea, all the wealth that our forefathers hid
169Within the massy entrails of the earth.
170Then tell me, Faustus, what shall we three want?

Faustus

171Nothing, Cornelius. O, this cheers my soul!
172Come, show me some demonstrations magical,
173That I may conjure in some bushy grove
174And have these joys in full possession.

Valdes

175Then haste thee to some solitary grove,
176And bear wise Bacon's and Albanus' works,
177The Hebrew Psalter, and New Testament;
178And whatsoever else is requisite
179We will inform thee ere our conference cease.

Cornelius

180Valdes, first let him know the words of art,
181And then, all other ceremonies learned,

Valdes

182First I'll instruct thee in the rudiments,
183And then wilt thou be perfecter than I.

Faustus

184Then come and dine with me, and after meat
185We'll canvas every quiddity thereof,
186For ere I sleep I'll try what I can do.
187This night I'll conjure, though I die therefore.

Exeunt omnes.

[I.ii]

Enter two SCHOLARS.

First Scholar

I wonder what's become of Faustus, that

was wont to make our schools ring with 'sic probo'.

Enter WAGNER, [carrying wine].

Second Scholar

That shall we presently know. Here comes

his boy.

First Scholar

How now, sirrah, where's thy master?

Wagner

God in heaven knows.

Second Scholar

Why, dost not thou know, then?

Wagner

Yes, I know, but that follows not.

First Scholar

Go to, sirrah! Leave your jesting, and

tell us where he is.

Wagner

That follows not by force of argument, which

you, being licentiates, should stand upon. Therefore,

acknowledge your error, and be attentive.

Second Scholar

Then you will not tell us?

Wagner

You are deceived, for I will tell you. Yet

if you were not dunces, you would never ask me such a

question. For is not he corpus naturale ? And is

not that mobile ? Then, wherefore should you ask

me such a question? But that I am by nature

phlegmatic, slow to wrath, and prone to lechery—

to love, I would say—it were not for you to

come within forty foot of the place of execution,

although I do not doubt but to see you both hanged

the next sessions. Thus, having triumphed over

you, I will set my countenance like a precisian

and begin to speak thus: Truly, my dear brethren,

my master is within at dinner with Valdes and

Cornelius, as this wine, if it could speak,

would inform your worships. And so the Lord

bless you, preserve you, and keep you, my dear

brethren.

Exit.

First Scholar

188O Faustus,
189Then I fear that which I have long suspected,
190That thou art fall'n into that damnèd art
191For which they two are infamous through the world.

Second Scholar

192Were he a stranger, not allied to me,
193The danger of his soul would make me mourn.
194But come, let us go and inform the Rector.
195It may be his grave counsel may reclaim him.

First Scholar

196I fear me nothing will reclaim him now.

Second Scholar

197Yet let us see what we can do.

Exeunt.

[I.iii]

Thunder. Enter LUCIFER and four Devils [above], FAUSTUS to them with this speech. [He holds a book.]

Faustus

198Now that the gloomy shadow of the night,
199Longing to view Orion's drizzling look,
200Leaps from th'Antarctic world unto the sky
201And dims the welkin with her pitchy breath,
202Faustus, begin thine incantations,
203And try if devils will obey thy hest,
204Seeing thou has prayed and sacrificed to them.

[He draws a circle.]

205Within this circle is Jehovah's name
206Forward and backward anagrammatised,
207Th'abbreviated names of holy saints,
208Figures of every adjunct to the heavens,
209And characters of signs and erring stars,
210By which the spirits are enforced to rise.
211Then fear not, Faustus, to be resolute,
212And try the utmost magic can perform.

(Thunder.)

Sint mihi dei Acherontis propitii! Valeat

numen triplex Jehovae! Ignei, aerii, aquatici,

terreni, spiritus, salvete! Orientis princeps

Lucifer, Beelzebub, inferni ardentis monarcha, et

Demogorgon, propitiamus vos, ut appareat et

surgat, Mephistopheles! Quid tu moraris?

Per Jehovam, Gehennam, et consecratam aquam quam

nunc spargo, signumque crucis quod nunc facio, et per

vota nostra, ipse nunc surgat nobis dicatus

Mephistopheles!

[Faustus sprinkles holy water and makes a sign of the cross.]

Enter a Devil [MEPHISTOPHELES, in the shape of a] dragon.

213I charge thee to return and change thy shape.
214Thou art too ugly to attend on me.
215Go, and return an old Franciscan friar;
216That holy shape becomes a devil best.

Exit Devil [MEPHISTOPHELES].

217I see there's virtue in my heavenly words.
218Who would not be proficient in this art?
219How pliant is this Mephistopheles,
220Full of obedience and humility!
221Such is the force of magic and my spells.

Enter MEPHISTOPHELES [disguised as a friar].

Mephistopheles

222Now, Faustus, what wouldst thou have me do?

Faustus

223I charge thee wait upon me whilst I live,
224To do whatever Faustus shall command,
225Be it to make the moon drop from her sphere
226Or the ocean to overwhelm the world.

Mephistopheles

227I am a servant to great Lucifer
228And may not follow thee without his leave.
229No more than he commands must we perform.

Faustus

230Did not he charge thee to appear to me?

Mephistopheles

231No, I came now hither of mine own accord.

Faustus

232Did not my conjuring raise thee? Speak.

Mephistopheles

233That was the cause, but yet per accidens .
234For when we hear one rack the name of God,
235Abjure the Scriptures and his Saviour Christ,
236We fly in hope to get his glorious soul,
237Nor will we come unless he use such means
238Whereby he is in danger to be damned.
239Therefore, the shortest cut for conjuring
240Is stoutly to abjure all godliness
241And pray devoutly to the prince of hell.

Faustus

242So Faustus hath
243Already done, and holds this principle:
244There is no chief but only Beelzebub,
245To whom Faustus doth dedicate himself.
246This word 'damnation' terrifies not me,
247For I confound hell in Elysium.
248My ghost be with the old philosophers!
249But leaving these vain trifles of mens' souls,
250Tell me, what is that Lucifer, thy lord?

Mephistopheles

251Arch-regent and commander of all spirits.

Faustus

252Was not that Lucifer an angel once?

Mephistopheles

253Yes, Faustus, and most dearly loved of God.

Faustus

254How comes it then that he is prince of devils?

Mephistopheles

255O, by aspiring pride and insolence,
256For which God threw him from the face of heaven.

Faustus

257And what are you that live with Lucifer?

Mephistopheles

258Unhappy spirits that fell with Lucifer,
259Conspired against our God with Lucifer,
260And are for ever damned with Lucifer.

Faustus

261Where are you damned?

Mephistopheles

262In hell.

Faustus

263How comes it then that thou art out of hell?

Mephistopheles

264Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it.
265Think'st thou that I, that saw the face of God
266And tasted the eternal joys of heaven,
267Am not tormented with ten thousand hells
268In being deprived of everlasting bliss?
269O Faustus, leave these frivolous demands,
270Which strikes a terror to my fainting soul!

Faustus

271What, is great Mephistopheles so passionate
272For being deprivèd of the joys of heaven?
273Learn thou of Faustus manly fortitude,
274And scorn those joys thou never shalt possess.
275Go bear these tidings to great Lucifer:
276Seeing Faustus hath incurred eternal death
277By desperate thoughts against Jove's deity,
278Say he surrenders up to him his soul,
279So he will spare him four-and-twenty years,
280Letting him live in all voluptuousness,
281Having thee ever to attend on me,
282To give me whatsoever I shall ask,
283To tell me whatsoever I demand,
284To slay mine enemies and to aid my friends
285And always be obedient to my will.
286Go and return to mighty Lucifer,
287And meet me in my study at midnight,
288And then resolve me of thy master's mind.

Mephistopheles

289I will, Faustus.

Exit.

Faustus

290Had I as many souls as there be stars,
291I'd give them all for Mephistopheles.
292By him I'll be great emperor of the world
293And make a bridge through the moving air
294To pass the ocean; with a band of men
295I'll join the hills that bind the Afric shore
296And make that country continent to Spain,
297And both contributory to my crown.
298The Emperor shall not live but by my leave,
299Nor any potentate of Germany.
300Now that I have obtained what I desired,
301I'll live in speculation of this art
302Till Mephistopheles return again.

Exit [FAUSTUS below; exeunt LUCIFER and others Devils above].

[I.iv]

Enter WAGNER and [ROBIN,] the CLOWN.

Wagner

Come hither, sirrah boy.

Robin

'Boy'? O, disgrace to my person! Zounds, 'boy'

in your face! You have seen many boys with beards,

I am sure.

Wagner

Sirrah, hast thou no comings in?

Robin

Yes, and goings out too, you may see, sir.

Wagner

Alas, poor slave, see how poverty jests in his

nakedness! I know the villain's out of service,

and so hungry that I know he would give his soul to

the devil for a shoulder of mutton, though it were

blood raw.

Robin

Not so, neither. I had need to have it well roasted,

and good sauce to it, if I pay so dear, I can tell you.

Wagner

Sirrah, wilt thou be my man and wait on me? And

I will make thee go like Qui mihi discipulus .

Robin

What, in verse?

Wagner

No, slave, in beaten silk and stavesacre.

Robin

Stavesacre? That's good to kill vermin. Then

belike if I serve you, I shall be lousy.

Wagner

Why, so thou shalt be, whether thou dost it or no;

for, sirrah, if thou dost not presently bind thyself

to me for seven years, I'll turn all the lice about

thee into familiars and make them tear thee in pieces.

Robin

Nay, sir, you may save yourself a labour, for they

are as familiar with me as if they paid for their

meat and drink, I can tell you.

Wagner

Well, sirrah, leave your jesting, and take these

guilders.

[Offering money.]

Robin

Yes, marry, sir, and I thank you, too.

Wagner

So, now thou art to be at an hour's warning

whensoever and wheresoever the devil shall fetch thee.

Robin

Here, take your guilders. I'll none of 'em.

[He attempts to return the money.]

Wagner

Not I. Thou art pressed. Prepare thyself, for I

will presently raise up two devils to carry thee

away.— Banio! Belcher!

Robin

Belcher? An Belcher come here, I'll belch him.

I am not afraid of a devil.

Enter two Devils.

Wagner

[To Robin.] How now, sir, will you serve me now?

Robin

Ay, good Wagner. Take away the devil, then.

Wagner

Spirits, away!

[Exeunt Devils.]

Now, sirrah, follow me.

Robin

I will, sir. But hark you, master, will you teach

me this conjuring occupation?

Wagner

Ay, sirrah, I'll teach thee to turn thyself to a

dog, or a cat, or a mouse, or a rat, or anything.

Robin

A dog, or a cat, or a mouse, or a rat? O brave,

Wagner!

Wagner

Villain, call me Master Wagner, and see that you

walk attentively, and let your right eye be always

diametrally fixed upon my left heel, that thou mayst

quasi vestigias nostras insistere .

Robin

Well, sir, I warrant you.

Exeunt.

Act II

[II.i]

Enter FAUSTUS in his study.

Faustus

303Now, Faustus, must thou needs be damned?
304Canst thou not be saved?
305What boots it then to think on God or heaven?
306Away with such vain fancies, and despair!
307Despair in God and trust in Beelzebub.
308Now go not backward; Faustus, be resolute.
309Why waver'st thou? O, something soundeth in mine ear:
310' Abjure this magic, turn to God again!'
311Why, he loves thee not.
312The god thou serv'st is thine own appetite,
313Wherein is fixed the love of Beelzebub
314To him I'll build an altar and a church,
315And offer lukewarm blood of new-born babes.

Enter the two ANGELS.

Bad Angel

316Go forward, Faustus, in that famous art.

Good Angel

317Sweet Faustus, leave that execrable art.

Faustus

318Contrition, prayer, repentance—what of these?

Good Angel

319O, they are means to bring thee unto heaven.

Bad Angel

320Rather illusions, fruits of lunacy,
321That make them foolish that do use them most.

Good Angel

322Sweet Faustus, think of heaven and heavenly things.

Bad Angel

323No, Faustus, think of honour and of wealth.

Exeunt ANGELS.

Faustus

324Wealth?
325Why, the seigniory of Emden shall be mine.
326When Mephistopheles shall stand by me,
327What power can hurt me? Faustus, thou art safe;
328Cast no more doubts. Mephistopheles, come,
329And bring glad tidings from great Lucifer.
330Is't not midnight? Come, Mephistopheles!
331Veni, veni, Mephistophile!

Enter MEPHISTOPHELES.

332Now tell me what saith Lucifer thy lord.

Mephistopheles

333That I shall wait on Faustus whilst he lives,
334So he will buy my service with his soul.

Faustus

335Already Faustus hath hazarded that for thee.

Mephistopheles

336But now thou must bequeath it solemnly
337And write a deed of gift with thine own blood,
338For that security craves Lucifer.
339If thou deny it, I must back to hell.

Faustus

340Stay, Mephistopheles, and tell me,
341What good will my soul do thy lord?

Mephistopheles

342Enlarge his kingdom.

Faustus

343Is that the reason why he tempts us thus?

Mephistopheles

344Solamen miseris socios habuisse doloris.

Faustus

345Why, have you any pain, that torture other?

Mephistopheles

346As great as have the human souls of men.
347But tell me, Faustus, shall I have thy soul?
348And I will be thy slave, and wait on thee,
349And give thee more than thou hast wit to ask.

Faustus

350Ay, Mephistopheles, I'll give it him.

Mephistopheles

351Then, Faustus, stab thine arm courageously,
352And bind thy soul that at some certain day
353Great Lucifer may claim it as his own,
354And then be thou as great as Lucifer.

Faustus

355[Cutting his arm.] Lo, Mephistopheles, for love of thee
356Faustus hath cut his arm, and with his proper blood
357Assures his soul to be great Lucifer's,
358Chief lord and regent of perpetual night.
359View here this blood that trickles from mine arm,
360And let it be propitious for my wish.

Mephistopheles

361But Faustus,
362Write it in manner of a deed of gift.

Faustus

363Ay, so I do. [He writes.] But Mephistopheles,
364My blood congeals, and I can write no more.

Mephistopheles

365I'll fetch thee fire to dissolve it straight.

Exit.

Faustus

366What might the staying of my blood portend?
367Is it unwilling I should write this bill?
368Why streams it not, that I may write afresh?
369'Faustus gives to thee his soul'—O, there it stayed!
370Why shouldst thou not? Is not thy soul thine own?
371Then write again: 'Faustus gives to thee his soul.'

Enter MEPHISTOPHELES with the chafer of fire.

Mephistopheles

372See, Faustus, here is fire. Set it on.

Faustus

373So. Now the blood begins to clear again.
374Now will I make an end immediately.

[He writes.]

Mephistopheles

375What will not I do to obtain his soul?aparte

Faustus

376Consummatum est . This bill is ended,
377And Faustus hath bequeathed his soul to Lucifer.
378But what is this inscription on mine arm?
379' Homo, fuge! ' Whither should I fly?
380If unto heaven, he'll throw me down to hell.—
381My senses are deceived; here's nothing writ.—
382O, yes, I see it plain. Even here is writ
383' Homo, fuge! ' Yet shall not Faustus fly.

Mephistopheles

384I'll fetch him somewhat to delight his mind.aparte

Exit.

Enter Devils, giving crowns and rich apparel to Faustus, they dance, and then depart.

Enter MEPHISTOPHELES.

Faustus

385What means this show? Speak, Mephistopheles.

Mephistopheles

386Nothing, Faustus, but to delight thy mind
387And let thee see what magic can perform.

Faustus

388But may I raise such spirits when I please?

Mephistopheles

389Ay, Faustus, and do greater things than these.

Faustus

390Then Mephistopheles, receive this scroll,
391A deed of gift of body and of soul—
392But yet conditionally that thou perform
393All covenants and articles between us both.

Mephistopheles

394Faustus, I swear by hell and Lucifer
395To effect all promises between us both.

Faustus

396Then hear me read it, Mephistopheles.

'On these conditions following:

First, that Faustus may be a spirit in form and

substance.

Secondly, that Mephistopheles shall be his servant,

and be by him commanded.

Thirdly, that Mephistopheles shall do for him and

bring him whatsoever.

Fourthly, that he shall be in his chamber or house

invisible.

Lastly, that he shall appear to the said John Faustus

at all times in what shape and form soever he

please.

I, John Faustus of Wittenberg, Doctor, by these

presents, do give both body and soul to Lucifer,

Prince of the East, and his minister Mephistopheles;

and furthermore grant unto them that four-and-

twenty years being expired, and these articles

above written being inviolate, full power to fetch or

carry the said John Faustus, body and soul, flesh,

blood, into their habitation wheresoever.

By me, John Faustus.'

Mephistopheles

Speak, Faustus. Do you deliver this as your

deed?

Faustus

[Giving the deed.] Ay. Take it, and the devil

give thee good of it.

Mephistopheles

397So. Now, Faustus, ask me what thou wilt.

Faustus

398First I will question thee about hell.
399Tell me, where is the place that men call hell?

Mephistopheles

400Under the heavens.

Faustus

401Ay, so are all things else. But whereabouts?

Mephistopheles

402Within the bowels of these elements,
403Where we are tortured and remain for ever.

1283A year, a month, a week, a natural day,
1284That Faustus may repent and save his soul!
1285O lente, lente currite noctis equi!
1286The stars move still; time runs; the clock will strike;
1287The devil will come, and Faustus must be damned.
1288O, I'll leap up to heaven! Who pulls me down?
1289One drop of blood will save me. O, my Christ!
1290Rend not my heart for naming of my Christ!
1291Yet will I call on him. O, spare me, Lucifer!
1292Where is it now? 'Tis gone;
1293And see a threat'ning arm, an angry brow.
1294Mountains and hills, come, come and fall on me,
1295And hide me from the heavy wrath of heaven!
1296No? Then will I headlong run into the earth.
1297Gape, earth! O, no, it will not harbour me.
1298You stars that reigned at my nativity,
1299Whose influence hath allotted death and hell,
1300Now draw up Faustus like a foggy mist
1301Into the entrails of yon labouring cloud,
1302That when you vomit forth into the air,
1303My limbs may issue from your smoky mouths,
1304But let my soul mount and ascend to heaven.

The watch strikes.

1305O, half the hour is past! 'Twill all be past anon.
1306O, if my soul must suffer for my sin,
1307Impose some end to my incessant pain.
1308Let Faustus live in hell a thousand years,
1309A hundred thousand, and at last be saved.
1310No end is limited to damnèd souls.
1311Why wert thou not a creature wanting soul?
1312Or why is this immortal that thou hast?
1313O, Pythagoras' metempsychosis , were that true,
1314This soul should fly from me and I be changed
1315Into some brutish beast.
1316All beasts are happy, for, when they die,
1317Their souls are soon dissolved in elements;
1318But mine must live still to be plagued in hell.
1319Curst be the parents that engendered me!
1320No, Faustus, curse thyself. Curse Lucifer
1321That hath deprived thee of the joys of heaven.

The clock strikes twelve.

1322It strikes, it strikes! Now, body, turn to air,
1323Or Lucifer will bear thee quick to hell.
1324O soul, be changed into small waterdrops,
1325And fall into the ocean, ne'er be found!

Thunder, and enter the Devils.

1326O, mercy, heaven, look not so fierce on me!
1327Adders and serpents, let me breathe a while!
1328Ugly hell, gape not. Come not, Lucifer!
1329I'll burn my books. O, Mephistopheles!

Exeunt.

[V.iii]

Enter the SCHOLARS.

First Scholar

1330Come, gentlemen, let us go visit Faustus,
1331For such a dreadful night was never seen
1332Since first the world's creation did begin.
1333Such fearful shrieks and cries were never heard.
1334Pray heaven the doctor have escaped the danger.

Second Scholar

1335O, help us, heaven! See, here are Faustus' limbs,
1336All torn asunder by the hand of death.

Third Scholar

1337The devils whom Faustus served have torn him thus.
1338For, twixt the hours of twelve and one, methought
1339I heard him shriek and call aloud for help,
1340At which self time the house seemed all on fire
1341With dreadful horror of these damnèd fiends.

Second Scholar

1342Well, gentlemen, though Faustus' end be such
1343As every Christian heart laments to think on,
1344Yet, for he was a scholar, once admired
1345For wondrous knowledge in our German schools,
1346We'll give his mangled limbs due burial;
1347And all the students, clothed in mourning black,
1348Shall wait upon his heavy funeral.

Exeunt.

Enter CHORUS.

Chorus

1349Cut is the branch that might have grown full straight,
1350And burnèd is Apollo's laurel bough
1351That sometime grew within this learnèd man.
1352Faustus is gone. Regard his hellish fall,
1353Whose fiendful fortune may exhort the wise
1354Only to wonder at unlawful things,
1355Whose deepness doth entice such forward wits
1356To practise more than heavenly power permits.

[Exit.]

Terminat hora diem; terminat author opus.
