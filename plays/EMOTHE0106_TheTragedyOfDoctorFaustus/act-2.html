Act II

[II.i]

Enter FAUSTUS in his study.

Faustus

303Now, Faustus, must thou needs be damned?
304Canst thou not be saved?
305What boots it then to think on God or heaven?
306Away with such vain fancies, and despair!
307Despair in God and trust in Beelzebub.
308Now go not backward; Faustus, be resolute.
309Why waver'st thou? O, something soundeth in mine ear:
310' Abjure this magic, turn to God again!'
311Why, he loves thee not.
312The god thou serv'st is thine own appetite,
313Wherein is fixed the love of Beelzebub
314To him I'll build an altar and a church,
315And offer lukewarm blood of new-born babes.

Enter the two ANGELS.

Bad Angel

316Go forward, Faustus, in that famous art.

Good Angel

317Sweet Faustus, leave that execrable art.

Faustus

318Contrition, prayer, repentance—what of these?

Good Angel

319O, they are means to bring thee unto heaven.

Bad Angel

320Rather illusions, fruits of lunacy,
321That make them foolish that do use them most.

Good Angel

322Sweet Faustus, think of heaven and heavenly things.

Bad Angel

323No, Faustus, think of honour and of wealth.

Exeunt ANGELS.

Faustus

324Wealth?
325Why, the seigniory of Emden shall be mine.
326When Mephistopheles shall stand by me,
327What power can hurt me? Faustus, thou art safe;
328Cast no more doubts. Mephistopheles, come,
329And bring glad tidings from great Lucifer.
330Is't not midnight? Come, Mephistopheles!
331Veni, veni, Mephistophile!

Enter MEPHISTOPHELES.

332Now tell me what saith Lucifer thy lord.

Mephistopheles

333That I shall wait on Faustus whilst he lives,
334So he will buy my service with his soul.

Faustus

335Already Faustus hath hazarded that for thee.

Mephistopheles

336But now thou must bequeath it solemnly
337And write a deed of gift with thine own blood,
338For that security craves Lucifer.
339If thou deny it, I must back to hell.

Faustus

340Stay, Mephistopheles, and tell me,
341What good will my soul do thy lord?

Mephistopheles

342Enlarge his kingdom.

Faustus

343Is that the reason why he tempts us thus?

Mephistopheles

344Solamen miseris socios habuisse doloris.

Faustus

345Why, have you any pain, that torture other?

Mephistopheles

346As great as have the human souls of men.
347But tell me, Faustus, shall I have thy soul?
348And I will be thy slave, and wait on thee,
349And give thee more than thou hast wit to ask.

Faustus

350Ay, Mephistopheles, I'll give it him.

Mephistopheles

351Then, Faustus, stab thine arm courageously,
352And bind thy soul that at some certain day
353Great Lucifer may claim it as his own,
354And then be thou as great as Lucifer.

Faustus

355[Cutting his arm.] Lo, Mephistopheles, for love of thee
356Faustus hath cut his arm, and with his proper blood
357Assures his soul to be great Lucifer's,
358Chief lord and regent of perpetual night.
359View here this blood that trickles from mine arm,
360And let it be propitious for my wish.

Mephistopheles

361But Faustus,
362Write it in manner of a deed of gift.

Faustus

363Ay, so I do. [He writes.] But Mephistopheles,
364My blood congeals, and I can write no more.

Mephistopheles

365I'll fetch thee fire to dissolve it straight.

Exit.

Faustus

366What might the staying of my blood portend?
367Is it unwilling I should write this bill?
368Why streams it not, that I may write afresh?
369'Faustus gives to thee his soul'—O, there it stayed!
370Why shouldst thou not? Is not thy soul thine own?
371Then write again: 'Faustus gives to thee his soul.'

Enter MEPHISTOPHELES with the chafer of fire.

Mephistopheles

372See, Faustus, here is fire. Set it on.

Faustus

373So. Now the blood begins to clear again.
374Now will I make an end immediately.

[He writes.]

Mephistopheles

375What will not I do to obtain his soul?aparte

Faustus

376Consummatum est . This bill is ended,
377And Faustus hath bequeathed his soul to Lucifer.
378But what is this inscription on mine arm?
379' Homo, fuge! ' Whither should I fly?
380If unto heaven, he'll throw me down to hell.—
381My senses are deceived; here's nothing writ.—
382O, yes, I see it plain. Even here is writ
383' Homo, fuge! ' Yet shall not Faustus fly.

Mephistopheles

384I'll fetch him somewhat to delight his mind.aparte

Exit.

Enter Devils, giving crowns and rich apparel to Faustus, they dance, and then depart.

Enter MEPHISTOPHELES.

Faustus

385What means this show? Speak, Mephistopheles.

Mephistopheles

386Nothing, Faustus, but to delight thy mind
387And let thee see what magic can perform.

Faustus

388But may I raise such spirits when I please?

Mephistopheles

389Ay, Faustus, and do greater things than these.

Faustus

390Then Mephistopheles, receive this scroll,
391A deed of gift of body and of soul—
392But yet conditionally that thou perform
393All covenants and articles between us both.

Mephistopheles

394Faustus, I swear by hell and Lucifer
395To effect all promises between us both.

Faustus

396Then hear me read it, Mephistopheles.

'On these conditions following:

First, that Faustus may be a spirit in form and

substance.

Secondly, that Mephistopheles shall be his servant,

and be by him commanded.

Thirdly, that Mephistopheles shall do for him and

bring him whatsoever.

Fourthly, that he shall be in his chamber or house

invisible.

Lastly, that he shall appear to the said John Faustus

at all times in what shape and form soever he

please.

I, John Faustus of Wittenberg, Doctor, by these

presents, do give both body and soul to Lucifer,

Prince of the East, and his minister Mephistopheles;

and furthermore grant unto them that four-and-

twenty years being expired, and these articles

above written being inviolate, full power to fetch or

carry the said John Faustus, body and soul, flesh,

blood, into their habitation wheresoever.

By me, John Faustus.'

Mephistopheles

Speak, Faustus. Do you deliver this as your

deed?

Faustus

[Giving the deed.] Ay. Take it, and the devil

give thee good of it.

Mephistopheles

397So. Now, Faustus, ask me what thou wilt.

Faustus

398First I will question thee about hell.
399Tell me, where is the place that men call hell?

Mephistopheles

400Under the heavens.

Faustus

401Ay, so are all things else. But whereabouts?

Mephistopheles

402Within the bowels of these elements,
403Where we are tortured and remain for ever.
404Hell hath no limits, nor is circumscribed
405In one self place, but where we are is hell,
406And where hell is there must we ever be.
407And, to be short, when all the world dissolves,
408And every creature shall be purified,
409All places shall be hell that is not heaven.

Faustus

410I think hell's a fable.

Mephistopheles

411Ay, think so still, till experience change thy mind.

Faustus

412Why, dost thou think that Faustus shall be damned?

Mephistopheles

413Ay, of necessity, for here's the scroll
414In which thou hast given thy soul to Lucifer.

Faustus

415Ay, and body too. But what of that?
416Think'st thou that Faustus is so fond to imagine
417That after this life there is any pain?
418No, these are trifles and mere old wives' tales.

Mephistopheles

419But I am an instance to prove the contrary,
420For I tell thee I am damned and now in hell.

Faustus

Nay, an this be hell, I'll willingly be damned.

What? Sleeping, eating, walking, and disputing?

But leaving this, let me have a wife, the fairest

maid in Germany, for I am wanton and lascivious

and cannot live without a wife.

Mephistopheles

Well, Faustus, thou shalt have a wife.

He fetches in a woman devil.

Faustus

421What sight is this?

Mephistopheles

422Now, Faustus, wilt thou have a wife?

Faustus

423Here's a hot whore indeed! No, I'll no wife.

Mephistopheles

424Marriage is but a ceremonial toy.
425An if thou lovest me, think no more of it.

[Exit Devil.]

426I'll cull thee out the fairest courtesans
427And bring them every morning to thy bed.
428She whom thine eye shall like, thy heart shall have,
429Were she as chaste as was Penelope,
430As wise as Saba, or as beautiful
431As was bright Lucifer before his fall.

[Presenting a book.]

432Here, take this book and peruse it well.
433The iterating of these lines brings gold;
434The framing of this circle on the ground
435Brings thunder, whirlwinds, storm, and lightning.
436Pronounce this thrice devoutly to thyself,
437And men in harness shall appear to thee
438Ready to execute what thou command'st.

Faustus

439Thanks, Mephistopheles, for this sweet book.
440This will I keep as chary as my life.

Exeunt.

[II.ii]

Enter [ROBIN] the CLOWN [with a conjuring book].

Robin

[Calling offstage.] What, Dick, look to the horses

there till I come again.—I have gotten one of Doctor

Faustus's conjuring books, and now we'll have such

knavery as't passes.

Enter DICK.

Dick

What, Robin, you must come away and walk the horses.

Robin

I walk the horses? I scorn't, 'faith. I have

other matters in hand. Let the horses walk themselves

an they will. [He reads.] 'A' per se ; ’a’ 't', 'h',

'e', 'the'; 'o' per se 'o'; 'deny orgon, gorgon'.—

Keep further from me, O thou illiterate and unlearned

ostler.

Dick

'Snails, what hast thou got there, a book? Why,

thou canst not tell ne'er a word on't.

Robin

That thou shalt see presently.

[He draws a circle.]

Keep out of the circle, I say, lest I send you into

the hostry, with a vengeance.

Dick

That's like, 'faith! You had best leave your foolery,

for an my master come he'll conjure you, 'faith.

Robin

My master conjure me? I'll tell thee what: an my

master come here, I'll clap as fair a pair of horns

on's head as e'er thou sawest in thy life.

Dick

Thou need'st not do that, for my mistress hath done it.

Robin

Ay, there be of us here that have waded as deep into

matters as other men, if they were disposed to talk.

Dick

A plague take you! I thought you did not sneak up

and down after her for nothing. But I prithee tell me

in good sadness, Robin, is that a conjuring book?

Robin

Do but speak what thou'lt have me to do, and I'll

do't. If thou'lt dance naked, put off thy clothes,

and I'll conjure thee about presently. Or if thou'lt

go but to the tavern with me, I'll give thee white wine,

red wine, claret wine, sack, muscadine, malmsey, and

whippincrust, hold belly hold, and we'll not pay one

penny for it.

Dick

O brave! Prithee let's to it presently, for I am

as dry as a dog.

Robin

Come, then, let's away.

Exeunt.

[II.iii]

Enter FAUSTUS in his study, and MEPHISTOPHELES.

Faustus

441When I behold the heavens, then I repent
442And curse thee, wicked Mephistopheles,
443Because thou hast deprived me of those joys.

Mephistopheles

444'Twas thine own seeking, Faustus. Thank thyself.
445But think'st thou heaven is such a glorious thing?
446I tell thee, Faustus, it is not half so fair
447As thou or any man that breathes on earth.

Faustus

448How prov'st thou that?

Mephistopheles

449'Twas made for man; then he's more excellent.

Faustus

450If heaven was made for man, 'twas made for me.
451I will renounce this magic and repent.

Enter the two ANGELS.

Good Angel

452Faustus, repent! Yet God will pity thee.

Bad Angel

453Thou art a spirit. God cannot pity thee.

Faustus

454Who buzzeth in mine ears I am a spirit?
455Be I a devil, yet God may pity me;
456Yea, God will pity me if I repent.

Bad Angel

457Ay, but Faustus never shall repent.

Exeunt ANGELS.

Faustus

458My heart is hardened; I cannot repent.
459Scarce can I name salvation, faith, or heaven.
460Swords, poison, halters, and envenomed steel
461Are laid before me to dispatch myself;
462And long ere this I should have done the deed,
463Had not sweet pleasure conquered deep despair.
464Have not I made blind Homer sing to me
465Of Alexander's love and Oenone's death?
466And hath not he that built the walls of Thebes
467With ravishing sound of his melodious harp
468Made music with my Mephistopheles?
469Why should I die, then, or basely despair?
470I am resolved, Faustus shall not repent.
471Come, Mephistopheles, let us dispute again
472And reason of divine astrology.
473Speak. Are there many spheres above the moon?
474Are all celestial bodies but one globe,
475As is the substance of this centric earth?

Mephistopheles

476As are the elements, such are the heavens,
477Even from the moon unto the empyreal orb,
478Mutually folded in each others' spheres,
479And jointly move upon one axletree,
480Whose terminè is termed the world's wide pole.
481Nor are the names of Saturn, Mars, or Jupiter
482Feigned, but are erring stars.

Faustus

483But have they all one motion, both situ et tempore ?

Mephistopheles

All move from east to west in four-and-

twenty hours upon the poles of the world, but

differ in their motions upon the poles of the zodiac.

Faustus

484These slender questions Wagner can decide.
485Hath Mephistopheles no greater skill?
486Who knows not the double motion of the planets?
487That the first is finished in a natural day,
488The second thus: Saturn in thirty years,
489Jupiter in twelve, Mars in four, the Sun, Venus, and
490Mercury in a year, the moon in twenty-eight days.

These are freshmen's questions. But tell me, hath

every sphere a dominion or intelligentia ?

Mephistopheles

Ay.

Faustus

How many heavens or spheres are there?

Mephistopheles

Nine: the seven planets, the firmament,

and the empyreal heaven.

Faustus

But is there not coelum igneum et crystallinum ?

Mephistopheles

No, Faustus, they be but fables.

Faustus

Resolve me then in this one question: why are

not conjunctions, oppositions, aspects, eclipses

all at one time, but in some years we have more,

in some less?

Mephistopheles

Per inaequalem motum respectu totius .

Faustus

Well, I am answered. Now tell me who made the world.

Mephistopheles

I will not.

Faustus

Sweet Mephistopheles, tell me.

Mephistopheles

Move me not, Faustus.

Faustus

Villain, have not I bound thee to tell me anything?

Mephistopheles

Ay, that is not against our kingdom.

This is. Thou art damned. Think thou of hell.

Faustus

Think, Faustus, upon God, that made the world.

Mephistopheles

Remember this.

Exit.

Faustus

491Ay, go, accursèd spirit, to ugly hell!
492'Tis thou hast damned distressèd Faustus' soul.
493Is't not too late?

Enter the two ANGELS.

Bad Angel

494Too late.

Good Angel

495Never too late, if Faustus will repent.

Bad Angel

496If thou repent, devils will tear thee in pieces.

Good Angel

497Repent, and they shall never raze thy skin.

Exeunt ANGELS.

Faustus

498O Christ, my Saviour, my Saviour,
499Help to save distressèd Faustus' soul!

Enter LUCIFER, BEELZEBUB, and MEPHISTOPHELES.

Lucifer

500Christ cannot save thy soul, for he is just.
501There's none but I have int'rest in the same.

Faustus

502O, what art thou that look'st so terribly?

Lucifer

503I am Lucifer,
504And this is my companion prince in hell.

Faustus

505O, Faustus, they are come to fetch thy soul!

Beelzebub

506We are come to tell thee thou dost injure us.

Lucifer

507Thou call'st on Christ, contrary to thy promise.

Beelzebub

508Thou shouldst not think on God.

Lucifer

509Think on the devil.

Beelzebub

510And his dam, too.

Faustus

Nor will Faustus henceforth. Pardon him for this,

and Faustus vows never to look to heaven.

Lucifer

So shalt thou show thyself an obedient servant,

and we will highly gratify thee for it.

Beelzebub

Faustus, we are come from hell in person to show

thee some pastime. Sit down, and thou shalt behold the

Seven Deadly Sins appear to thee in their own proper shapes and likeness.

Faustus

That sight will be as pleasant to me as

paradise was to Adam the first day of his creation.

Lucifer

Talk not of paradise or creation, but mark

the show. Go, Mephistopheles, fetch them in.

[Faustus sits.]

[MEPHISTOPHELES fetches the SINS.]Enter the SEVEN DEADLY SINS.

Beelzebub

Now, Faustus, question them of their names and

dispositions.

Faustus

That shall I soon.—What art thou, the first?

Pride

I am Pride. I disdain to have any parents. I

am like to Ovid's flea: I can creep into every corner

of a wench. Sometimes like a periwig I sit upon her

brow; next, like a necklace I hang about her neck;

then, like a fan of feathers I kiss her, and then, turning

myself to a wrought smock, do what I list. But fie,

what a smell is here! I'll not speak a word more

for a king's ransom, unless the ground be perfumed

and covered with cloth of arras.

Faustus

Thou art a proud knave, indeed.—What art thou, the

second?

Covetousness

I am Covetousness, begotten of an old churl

in a leather bag; and might I now obtain my wish,

this house, you, and all should turn to gold, that I might

lock you safe into my chest. O my sweet gold!

Faustus

And what art thou, the third?

Envy

I am Envy, begotten of a chimney-sweeper and an oyster-

wife. I cannot read, and therefore wish all books

burnt. I am lean with seeing others eat. O,

that there would come a famine over all the world,

that all might die and I live alone! Then thou

shouldst see how fat I'd be. But must thou sit

and I stand? Come down, with a vengeance!

Faustus

Out, envious wretch!— But what are thou, the fourth?

Wrath

I am Wrath. I had neither father nor mother. I leaped

out of a lion's mouth when I was scarce an hour old,

and ever since have run up and down the world with these

case of rapiers, wounding myself when I could get

none to fight withal. I was born in hell, and look to

it, for some of you shall be my father.

Faustus

And what art thou, the fifth?

Gluttony

I am Gluttony. My parents are all dead, and the

devil a penny they have left me but a small pension,

and that buys me thirty meals a day, and ten bevers—

a small trifle to suffice nature. I come of a royal

pedigree. My father was a gammon of bacon, and my

mother was a hogshead of claret wine. My godfathers

were these: Peter Pickled-herring and Martin Martlemas-

beef. But my godmother, O, she was an ancient gentlewoman;

her name was Margery March-beer. Now, Faustus, thou

hast heard all my progeny, wilt thou bid me to supper?

Faustus

Not I.

Gluttony

Then the devil choke thee!

Faustus

Choke thyself, glutton!—What art thou, the sixth?

Sloth

Heigh-ho. I am Sloth. I was begotten on a sunny bank.

Heigh-ho. I'll not speak a word more for a king's ransom.

Faustus

And what are you, Mistress Minx, the seventh and last?

Lechery

Who, I? I, sir? I am one that loves an inch of raw

mutton better than an ell of fried stockfish, and the

first letter of my name begins with Lechery.

Lucifer

Away, to hell, away! On, piper!

Exeunt the SEVEN SINS.

Faustus

O, how this sight doth delight my soul!

Lucifer

But Faustus, in hell is all manner of delight.

Faustus

O, might I see hell and return again safe, how

happy were I then!

Lucifer

Faustus, thou shalt. At midnight I will send

for thee. [Presenting a book.] Meanwhile, peruse

this book, and view it throughly, and thou shalt

turn thyself into what shape thou wilt.

Faustus

[Taking the book.] Thanks, mighty Lucifer.

This will I keep as chary as my life.

Lucifer

Now, Faustus, farewell.

Faustus

Farewell, great Lucifer. Come, Mephistopheles.

Exeunt omnes, several ways.
