Act IV

[IV.i]

Enter MARTINO and FREDERICK [with other Officers and Gentlemen] at several doors.

Martino

844What ho, officers, gentlemen!
845Hie to the presence to attend the Emperor.
846Good Frederick, see the rooms be voided straight;
847His Majesty is coming to the hall.
848Go back, and see the state in readiness.

[Exeunt some.]

Frederick

849But where is Bruno, our elected pope,
850That on a Fury's back came post from Rome?
851Will not his Grace consort the Emperor?

Martino

852O, yes, and with him comes the German conjurer,
853The learnèd Faustus, fame of Wittenberg,
854The wonder of the world for magic art;
855And he intends to show great Carolus
856The race of all his stout progenitors,
857And bring in presence of his Majesty
858The royal shapes and warlike semblances
859Of Alexander and his beauteous paramour.

Frederick

860Where is Benvolio?

Martino

861Fast asleep, I warrant you.
862He took his rouse with stoups of Rhenish wine
863So kindly yesternight to Bruno's health
864That all this day the sluggard keeps his bed.

Frederick

865See, see, his window's ope. We'll call to him.

Martino

866What ho, Benvolio!

Enter BENVOLIO above at a window, in his nightcap, buttoning.

Benvolio

867What a devil ail you two?

Martino

868Speak softly, sir, lest the devil hear you;
869For Faustus at the court is late arrived,
870And at his heels a thousand Furies wait
871To accomplish whatsoever the doctor please.

Benvolio

872What of this?

Martino

873Come, leave thy chamber first, and thou shalt see
874This conjurer perform such rare exploits
875Before the Pope and royal Emperor
876As never yet was seen in Germany.

Benvolio

877Has not the Pope enough of conjuring yet?
878He was upon the devil's back late enough;
879An if he be so far in love with him,
880I would he would post with him to Rome again.

Frederick

881Speak, wilt thou come and see this sport?

Benvolio

Speak, wilt thou come and see this sport? Not I.

Martino

882Wilt thou stand in thy window and see it, then?

Benvolio

883Ay, an I fall not asleep i'th'meantime.

Martino

884The Emperor is at hand, who comes to see
885What wonders by black spells may compassed be.

Benvolio

Well, go you attend the Emperor. I am

content for this once to thrust my head out at a

window, for they say if a man be drunk overnight

the devil cannot hurt him in the morning. If

that be true, I have a charm in my head shall

control him as well as the conjurer, I warrant you.

Exeunt [FREDERICK and MARTINO BENVOLIO remains at his window].

A sennet. [Enter] CHARLES THE GERMAN EMPEROR, BRUNO, [THE DUKE OF] SAXONY, FAUSTUS, MEPHISTOPHELES, FREDERICK, MARTINO, and Attendants. [The Emperor sits in his throne.]

Emperor

886Wonder of men, renowned magician,
887Thrice-learnèd Faustus, welcome to our court.
888This deed of thine, in setting Bruno free
889From his and our professèd enemy,
890Shall add more excellence unto thine art
891Than if by powerful necromantic spells
892Thou couldst command the world's obedience.
893For ever be beloved of Carolus.
894And if this Bruno thou hast late redeemed
895In peace possess the triple diadem
896And sit in Peter's chair, despite of chance,
897Thou shalt be famous through all Italy
898And honoured of the German Emperor.

Faustus

899These gracious words, most royal Carolus,
900Shall make poor Faustus to his utmost power
901Both love and serve the German Emperor
902And lay his life at holy Bruno's feet.
903For proof whereof, if so your Grace be pleased,
904The doctor stands prepared by power of art
905To cast his magic charms, that shall pierce through
906The ebon gates of ever-burning hell
907And hale the stubborn Furies from their caves
908To compass whatsoe'er your Grace commands.

Benvolio

Blood, he speaks terribly.

But for all that, I do not greatly believe him. He

looks as like a conjurer as the Pope to a costermonger.

Emperor

909Then, Faustus, as thou late didst promise us,
910We would behold that famous conqueror
911Great Alexander and his paramour
912In their true shapes and state majestical,
913That we may wonder at their excellence.

Faustus

914Your Majesty shall see them presently.—
915Mephistopheles, away,aparte
916And with a solemn noise of trumpet's soundaparte
917Present before this royal Emperoraparte
918Great Alexander and his beauteous paramour.aparte

Mephistopheles

919Faustus, I will.aparte

[Exit.]

Benvolio

[At the window.] Well, Master Doctor, an your

devils come not away quickly, you shall have me asleep

presently. Zounds, I could eat myself for anger to

think I have been such an ass all this while, to stand

gaping after the devil's governor and can see nothing.

Faustus

I'll make you feel something anon, if my

art fail me not.—

[To Emperor]

920My lord, I must forewarn your Majesty
921That when my spirits present the royal shapes
922Of Alexander and his paramour,
923Your Grace demand no questions of the king,
924But in dumb silence let them come and go.

Emperor

925Be it as Faustus please. We are content.

Benvolio

[At the window.] Ay, ay, and I am content too.

An thou bring Alexander and his paramour before the

Emperor, I'll be Actaeon and turn myself to a stag.

Faustus

And I'll play Diana and send you the horns

presently.

[Enter MEPHISTOPHELES]

A sennet. Enter at one [door] the Emperor ALEXANDER, at the other DARIUS. They meet; Darius is thrown down. Alexander kills him, takes off his crown, and, offering to go out, his PARAMOUR meets him. He embraceth her and sets Darius' crown upon her head; and, coming back, both salute the [German] Emperor, who, leaving his state, offers to embrace them, which Faustus seeing suddenly stays him.

Then trumpets cease and music sounds.

926My gracious lord, you do forget yourself.
927These are but shadows, not substantial.

Emperor

928O, pardon me. My thoughts are so ravishèd
929With sight of this renownèd emperor
930That in mine arms I would have compassed him.
931But Faustus, since I may not speak to them
932To satisfy my longing thoughts at full,
933Let me this tell thee: I have heard it said
934That this fair lady, whilst she lived on earth,
935Had on her neck a little wart or mole.
936How may I prove that saying to be true?

Faustus

937Your Majesty may boldly go and see.

Emperor

938[Making an inspection.] Faustus, I see it plain,
939And in this sight thou better pleasest me
940Than if I gained another monarchy.

Faustus

941[To the spirits.] Away, begone!

Exit Show.

See, see, my gracious lord, what strange beast is

yon, that thrusts his head out at window.

[Benvolio is seen to have sprouted horns.]

Emperor

942O wondrous sight! See, Duke of Saxony,
943Two spreading horns most strangely fastenèd
944Upon the head of young Benvolio.

Saxony

What, is he asleep, or dead?

Faustus

He sleeps, my lord, but dreams not of his horns.

Emperor

This sport is excellent. We'll call and wake him.—

What ho, Benvolio!

Benvolio

A plague upon you! Let me sleep a while.

Emperor

I blame thee not to sleep much, having such a

head of thine own.

Saxony

Look up, Benvolio. 'Tis the Emperor calls.

Benvolio

The Emperor? Where? O, zounds, my head!

Emperor

Nay, an thy horns hold, 'tis no matter for thy

head, for that's armed sufficiently.

Faustus

Why, how now, sir knight? What, hanged by the

horns? This is most horrible. Fie, fie, pull in

your head, for shame. Let not all the world wonder

at you.

Benvolio

945Zounds, doctor, is this your villainy?

Faustus

946O, say not so, sir. The doctor has no skill,
947No art, no cunning to present these lords
948Or bring before this royal emperor
949The mighty monarch, warlike Alexander.
950If Faustus do it, you are straight resolved
951In bold Actaeon's shape to turn a stag.—
952And therefore, my lord, so please your Majesty,
953I’ll raise a kennel of hounds shall hunt him so
954As all his footmanship shall scarce prevail
955To keep his carcass from their bloody fangs.
956Ho, Belimoth, Argiron, Ashtaroth!

Benvolio

Hold, hold! Zounds, he'll raise up a kennel of

devils, I think, anon.—Good my lord, entreat for me.—

[Benvolio is attacked by devils.]

'Sblood, I am never able to endure these torments.

Emperor

957Then, good Master Doctor,
958Let me entreat you to remove his horns.
959He has done penance now sufficiently.

Faustus

My gracious lord, not so much for injury done to

me as to delight your Majesty with some mirth hath

Faustus justly requited this injurious knight; which

being all I desire, I am content to remove his horns.—

Mephistopheles, transform him.

[Mephistopheles removes the horns.]

And hereafter, sir, look you speak well of scholars.

Benvolio

Speak well of ye? 'Sblood, an scholars

be such cuckold-makers to clap horns of honest men's

heads o' this order, I'll ne'er trust smooth faces and

small ruffs more. But, an I be not revenged for this,

would I might be turned to a gaping oyster and drink

nothing but salt water.

[Exit from the window.]

Emperor

960Come, Faustus. While the Emperor lives,
961In recompense of this thy high desert
962Thou shalt command the state of Germany
963And live beloved of mighty Carolus.

Exeunt omnes.

[IV.ii]

Enter BENVOLIO, MARTINO, FREDERICK, and Soldiers.

Martino

964Nay, sweet Benvolio, let us sway thy thoughts
965From this attempt against the conjurer.

Benvolio

966Away! You love me not, to urge me thus.
967Shall I let slip so great an injury
968When every servile groom jests at my wrongs
969And in their rustic gambols proudly say,
970'Benvolio's head was graced with horns today'?
971O, may these eyelids never close again
972Till with my sword I have that conjurer slain!
973If you will aid me in this enterprise,
974then draw your weapons and be resolute.
975If not, depart. Here will Benvolio die
976But Faustus' death shall quit my infamy.

Frederick

977Nay, we will stay with thee, betide what may,
978And kill that doctor if he come this way.

Benvolio

979Then, gentle Frederick, hie thee to the grove,
980And place our servants and our followers
981Close in an ambush there behind the trees.
982By this, I know, the conjurer is near;
983I saw him kneel and kiss the Emperor's hand
984And take his leave, laden with rich rewards.
985Then, soldiers, boldly fight. If Faustus die,
986Take you the wealth; leave us the victory.

Frederick

987Come, soldiers, follow me unto the grove.
988Who kills him shall have gold and endless love.

Exit FREDERICK with the Soldiers.

Benvolio

989My head is lighter than it was by th'horns,
990But yet my heart's more ponderous than my head
991And pants until I see that conjurer dead.

Martino

992Where shall we place ourselves, Benvolio?

Benvolio

993Here will we stay to bide the first assault.
994O, were that damnèd hellhound but in place,
995Thou soon shouldst see me quit my foul disgrace.

Enter FREDERICK.

Frederick

996Close, close! The conjurer is at hand
997And all alone comes walking in his gown.
998Be ready, then, and strike the peasant down.

Benvolio

999Mine be that honour, then. Now, sword, strike home!
1000For horns he gave, I'll have his head anon.

Enter FAUSTUS, with the false head.

Martino

1001See, see, he comes.

Benvolio

See, see, he comes. No words. This blow ends all
1002Hell take his soul! His body thus must fall

[He strikes Faustus.]

Faustus

1003[Falling.] O!

Frederick

1004Groan you, Master Doctor?

Benvolio

1005Break may his heart with groans! Dear Frederick, see,
1006Thus will I end his griefs immediately.

Martino

1007Strike with a willing hand.

[Benvolio strikes off Faustus's false head.]

Strike with a willing hand. His head is off!

Benvolio

1008The devil's dead. The Furies now may laugh.

Frederick

1009Was this that stern aspect, that awful frown,
1010Made the grim monarch of infernal spirits
1011Tremble and quake at his commanding charms?

Martino

1012Was this that damnèd head whose heart conspired
1013Benvolio's shame before the Emperor?

Benvolio

1014Ay, that's the head, and here the body lies,
1015Justly rewarded for his villainies.

Frederick

1016Come, let's devise how we may add more shame
1017To the black scandal of his hated name.

Benvolio

1018First, on his head, in quittance of my wrongs,
1019I'll nail huge forkèd horns and let them hang
1020Within the window where he yoked me first,
1021That all the world may see my just revenge.

Martino

1022What use shall we put his beard to?

Benvolio

We'll sell it to a chimney-sweeper. It will

wear out ten birchen brooms, I warrant you.

Frederick

What shall his eyes do?

Benvolio

We'll put out his eyes, and they shall serve for

buttons to his lips to keep his tongue from catching cold.

Martino

An excellent policy. And now, sirs, having

divided him, what shall the body do?

[Faustus rises.]

Benvolio

1023Zounds, the devil's alive again!

Frederick

1024Give him his head, for God's sake!

Faustus

1025Nay, keep it. Faustus will have heads and hands,
1026Ay, all your hearts, to recompense this deed.
1027Knew you not, traitors, I was limited
1028For four-and-twenty years to breathe on earth?
1029And had you cut my body with your swords,
1030Or hewed this flesh and bones as small as sand,
1031Yet in a minute had my spirit returned,
1032And I had breathed a man made free from harm.
1033But wherefore do I dally my revenge?
1034Ashtaroth, Belimoth, Mephistopheles!

Enter MEPHISTOPHELES and other Devils [BELIMOTH and ASHTAROTH].

1035Go horse these traitors on your fiery backs,
1036And mount aloft with them as high as heaven;
1037Thence pitch them headlong to the lowest hell.
1038Yet stay. The world shall see their misery,
1039And hell shall after plague their treachery.
1040Go, Belimoth, and take this caitiff hence,
1041And hurl him is some lake of mud and dirt.

[To Ashtaroth.]

1042Take thou this other; drag him through the woods
1043Amongst the pricking thorns and sharpest briers,
1044Whilst with my gentle Mephistopheles
1045This traitor flies unto some steepy rock
1046That, rolling down, may break the villain's bones
1047As he intended to dismember me.
1048Fly hence. Dispatch my charge immediately.

Frederick

1049Pity us, gentle Faustus. Save our lives!

Faustus

1050Away!

Frederick

1051He must needs go that the devil drives.

Exeunt Spirits with the KNIGHTS [on their backs].

Enter the ambushed SOLDIERS.

First Soldier

1052Come, sirs. Prepare yourselves in readiness;
1053Make haste to help these noble gentlemen.
1054I heard them parley with the conjurer.

Second Soldier

1055See where he comes. Dispatch, and kill the slave.

Faustus

1056What's here? An ambush to betray my life?
1057Then, Faustus, try thy skill. Base peasants, stand!

[Trees come between Faustus and the Soldiers.]

1058For lo, these trees remove at my command
1059And stand as bulwarks 'twixt yourselves and me
1060To shield me from your hated treachery.
1061Yet to encounter this your weak attempt,
1062Behold an army comes incontinent.

FAUSTUS strikes the door, and enter a Devil playing on a drum, after him another bearing an ensign, and divers with weapons; MEPHISTOPHELES with fireworks. They set upon the SOLDIERS and drive them out.

[Exit FAUSTUS.]

[IV.iii]

Enter at several doors BENVOLIO, FREDERICK, and MARTINO, their heads and faces bloody and besmeared with mud and dirt, all having horns on their heads.

Martino

1063What ho, Benvolio!

Benvolio

What ho, Benvolio! Here. What, Frederick, ho!

Frederick

1064O, help me, gentle friend. Where is Martino?

Martino

1065Dear Frederick, here,
1066Half smothered in a lake of mud and dirt
1067Through which the Furies dragged me by the heels.

Frederick

1068Martino, see! Benvolio's horns again.

Martino

1069O misery! How now, Benvolio?

Benvolio

1070Defend me, heaven. Shall I be haunted still?

Martino

1071Nay, fear not, man. We have no power to kill.

Benvolio

1072My friends transformèd thus! O hellish spite!
1073Your heads are all set with horns.

Frederick

Your heads are all set with horns. You hit it right.
1074It is your own you mean. Feel on your head.

Benvolio

1075[Feeling his head.] Zounds, horns again!

Martino

1076Nay, chafe not, man, we all are sped.

Benvolio

1077What devil attends this damned magician,
1078That, spite of spite, our wrongs are doublèd?

Frederick

1079What may we do, that we may hide our shames?

Benvolio

1080If we should follow him to work revenge,
1081He'd join long asses' ears to these huge horns
1082And make us laughing-stocks to all the world.

Martino

1083What shall we then do, dear Benvolio?

Benvolio

1084I have a castle joining near these woods,
1085And thither we'll repair and live obscure
1086Till time shall alter this our brutish shapes.
1087Sith black disgrace hath thus eclipsed our fame,
1088We'll rather die with grief than live with shame.

Exeunt omnes.

[IV.iv]

Enter FAUSTUS, and the HORSE-CORSER, and MEPHISTOPHELES.

Horse-corser

[Offering money.] I beseech your worship, accept

of these forty dollars.

Faustus

Friend, thou canst not buy so good a horse for so

small a price. I have no great need to sell him, but if

thou likest him for ten dollars more, take him, because

I see thou hast a good mind to him.

Horse-corser

I beseech you, sir, accept of this. I am a

very poor man and have lost very much of late by

horseflesh, and this bargain will set me up again.

Faustus

Well, I will not stand with thee. Give me the

money. [He takes the money.] Now, sirrah, I must

tell you that you may ride him o'er hedge and ditch,

and spare him not. But do you hear? In any case ride

him not into the water.

Horse-corser

How, sir, not into the water? Why, will he

not drink of all waters?

Faustus

Yes, he will drink of all waters. But ride him

not into the water. O'er hedge and ditch, or where

thou wilt, but not into the water. Go bid the

ostler deliver him unto you, and remember what I say.

Horse-corser

I warrant you, sir. O, joyful day! Now am I

a made man for ever.

Exit.

Faustus

1089What art thou, Faustus, but a man condemned to die?
1090Thy fatal time draws to a final end.
1091Despair doth drive distrust into my thoughts.
1092Confound these passions with a quiet sleep.
1093Tush! Christ did call the thief upon the cross;
1094Then rest thee, Faustus, quiet in conceit.

He sits to sleep.

Enter the HORSE-CORSER, wet.

Horse-corser

O, what a cozening doctor was this! I,

riding my horse into the water, thinking some hidden

mystery had been in the horse, I had nothing under me

but a little straw and had much ado to escape drowning.

Well, I'll go rouse him and make him give me my forty

dollars again.—Ho, sirrah doctor, you cozening scab!

Master Doctor, awake, and rise, and give me my money

again, for your horse is turned to a bottle of hay.

Master Doctor! (He pulls off his leg.) Alas, I am

undone! What shall I do? I have pulled off his leg.

Faustus

O, help, help! The villain hath murdered me.

Horse-corser

Murder or not murder, now he has but one leg

I'll outrun him and cast this leg into some ditch or

other.

[Exit with the leg.]

Faustus

Stop him, stop him, stop him!— Ha, ha, ha! Faustus

hath his leg again, and the Horse-corser a bundle of hay

for his forty dollars.

Enter WAGNER

How now, Wagner, what news with thee?

Wagner

If it please you, the Duke of Vanholt doth earnestly

entreat your company and hath sent some of his men to

attend you with provision fit for your journey.

Faustus

The Duke of Vanholt's an honourable gentleman,

and one to whom I must be no niggard of my cunning.

Come away.

Exeunt.

[IV.v]

Enter CLOWN [ROBIN], DICK, HORSE-CORSER, and a CARTER

Carter

Come, my masters, I'll bring you to the best beer

in Europe.—What ho, Hostess!—Where be these whores?

Enter HOSTESS.

Hostess

How now, what lack you? What, my old guests,

welcome!

Robin

Sirrah Dick, dost thou know why I

stand so mute?

Dick

No, Robin, why is't?

Robin

I am eighteen pence on the score.

But say nothing. See if she have forgotten me.

Hostess

[Seeing Robin] Who's this that stands so solemnly

by himself? [To Robin.] What, my old guest?

Robin

O, Hostess, how do you? I hope my score stands still.

Hostess

Ay, there's no doubt of that, for methinks you make

no haste to wipe it out.

Dick

Why, Hostess, I say, fetch us some beer.

Hostess

You shall, presently.—Look up into th' hall

there, ho!

Exit.

Dick

Come, sirs, what shall we do now till mine Hostess

comes?

Carter

Marry, sir, I'll tell you the bravest tale how a

conjurer served me. You know Doctor Fauster?

Horse-corser

Ay, a plague take him! Here's some on's have

cause to know him. Did he conjure thee, too?

Carter

I'll tell you how he served me. As I was going to

Wittenberg t'other day with a load of hay, he met me

and asked me what he should give me for as much hay as

he could eat. Now, sir, I thinking that a little

would serve his turn, bade him take as much as he would

for three farthings. So he presently gave me my money

and fell to eating; and, as I am a cursen man, he never

left eating till he had eat up all my load of hay.

All

O monstrous! Eat a whole load of hay!

Robin

Yes, yes, that may be, for I have heard of one that

has eat a load of logs.

Horse-corser

Now, sirs, you shall hear how villainously he

served me. I went to him yesterday to buy a horse of

him, and he would by no means sell him under forty

dollars. So, sir, because I knew him to be such a

horse as would run over hedge and ditch and never tire,

I gave him his money. So when I had my horse, Doctor

Fauster bade me ride him night and day and spare him no 40

time. 'But', quoth he, 'in any case ride him not into

the water.' Now, sir, I, thinking the horse had had

some quality that he would not have me know of, what

did I but rid him into a great river? And when I came

just in the midst, my horse vanished away, and I sat

straddling upon a bottle of hay.

All

O brave doctor!

Horse-corser

But you shall hear how bravely I served him

for it. I went me home to his house, and there I found

him asleep. I kept a halloing and whooping in his ears,

but all could not wake him. I, seeing that, took him

by the leg and never rested pulling till I had pulled

me his leg quite off, and now 'tis at home in mine

hostry.

Robin

And has the doctor but one leg, then? That's

excellent, for one of his devils turned me into the

likeness of an ape's face.

Carter

Some more drink, Hostess!

Robin

Hark you, we'll into another room and drink a while,

and then we'll go seek out the doctor.

Exeunt omnes.

[IV.vi]

Enter the DUKE OF VANHOLT, his [pregnant] DUCHESS, FAUSTUS and MEPHISTOPHELES [and Servants].

Duke

Thanks, Master Doctor, for these pleasant sights. Nor

know I how sufficiently to recompense your great deserts

in erecting that enchanted castle in the air, the sight

whereof so delighted me as nothing in the world could

please me more.

Faustus

I do think myself, my good lord, highly recompensed

in that it pleaseth your Grace to think but well of that

which Faustus hath performed.—But, gracious lady, it may

be that you have taken no pleasure in those sights.

Therefore, I pray you tell me what is the thing you

most desire to have; be it in the world, it shall be yours.

I have heard that great-bellied women do long for things

are rare and dainty.

Duchess

True, Master Doctor, and, since I find you so kind,

I will make known unto you what my heart desires to

have. And were it now summer, as it is January, a dead

time of the winter, I would request no better meat than

a dish of ripe grapes.

Faustus

This is but a small matter.

Go, Mephistopheles, away!

Exit MEPHISTOPHELES.

Madam, I will do more than this for your content.

Enter MEPHISTOPHELES again with the grapes.

Here. Now taste ye these. They should be good, for

they come from a far country, I can tell you.

[The Duchess tastes the grapes.]

Duke

This makes me wonder more than all the rest, that at

this time of the year, when every tree is barren of

his fruit, from whence you had these ripe grapes.

Faustus

Please it your Grace, the year is divided into two

circles over the whole world, so that, when it is winter

with us, in the contrary circle it is likewise summer

with them, as in India, Saba, and such countries that

lie far east, where they have fruit twice a year. From

whence, by means of a swift spirit that I have, I had

these grapes brought, as you see.

Duchess

And, trust me, they are the sweetest grapes that

e'er I tasted.

The CLOWN[S] bounce at the gate, within.

Duke

1095What rude disturbers have we at the gate?
1096Go pacify their fury. Set it ope,
1097And then demand of them what they would have.

They knock again and call out to talk with Faustus.

[A Servant goes to the gate.]

Servant

Why, how now, masters, what a coil is there!

What is the reason you disturb the duke?

Dick

[Offstage.] We have no reason for it. Therefore, a

Servant
