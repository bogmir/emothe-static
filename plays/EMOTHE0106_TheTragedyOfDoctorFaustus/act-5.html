The Tragedy of Doctor Faustus

Act V

[V.i]

Thunder and lightning. Enter Devils with covered dishes. MEPHISTOPHELES leads them into Faustus's study.

Then enter WAGNER.

Wagner

1101I think my master means to die shortly.
1102He has made his will and given me his wealth:
1103His house, his goods, and store of golden plate,
1104Besides two thousand ducats ready coined.
1105I wonder what he means. If death were nigh,
1106He would not frolic thus. He's now at supper
1107With the scholars, where there's such belly-cheer
1108As Wagner in his life ne'er saw the like.
1109And see where they come. Belike the feast is done.

Exit.

Enter FAUSTUS, MEPHISTOPHELES, and two or three SCHOLARS.

First Scholar

Master Doctor Faustus, since our conference

about fair ladies—which was the beautifullest in all

the world—we have determined with ourselves that

Helen of Greece was the admirablest lady that ever

lived. Therefore, Master Doctor, if you will do us

so much favour as to let us see that peerless dame of

Greece, whom all the world admires for majesty, we

should think ourselves much beholding unto you.

Faustus

1110Gentlemen,
1111For that I know your friendship is unfeigned,
1112It is not Faustus' custom to deny
1113The just request of those that wish him well:
1114You shall behold that peerless dame of Greece,
1115No otherwise for pomp or majesty
1116Than when Sir Paris crossed the seas with her
1117And brought the spoils to rich Dardania.
1118Be silent then, for danger is in words.

[MEPHISTOPHELES goes to the door.]

Music sound. MEPHISTOPHELES brings in HELEN. She passeth over the stage.

Second Scholar

1119Was this fair Helen, whose admirèd worth
1120Made Greece with ten years' wars afflict poor Troy?

Third Scholar

1121Too simple is my wit to tell her worth,
1122Whom all the world admires for majesty.

First Scholar

1123Now we have seen the pride of nature's work,
1124We'll take our leaves, and for this blessèd sight
1125Happy and blest be Faustus evermore.

Faustus

1126Gentlemen, farewell. The same wish I to you.

Exeunt SCHOLARS.

Enter an OLD MAN.

Old Man

1127O gentle Faustus, leave this damnèd art,
1128This magic, that will charm thy soul to hell
1129And quite bereave thee of salvation!
1130Though thou hast now offended like a man,
1131Do not persever in it like a devil.
1132Yet, yet thou hast an amiable soul,
1133If sin by custom grow not into nature.
1134Then, Faustus, will repentance come too late;
1135Then thou art banished from the sight of heaven.
1136No mortal can express the pains of hell.
1137It may be this my exhortation
1138Seems harsh and all unpleasant. Let it not,
1139For, gentle son, I speak it not in wrath
1140Or envy of thee, but in tender love
1141And pity of thy future misery;
1142And so have hope that this my kind rebuke,
1143Checking thy body, may amend thy soul.

Faustus

1144Where art thou, Faustus? Wretch, what hast thou done?
1145Hell claims his right, and with a roaring voice
1146Says, 'Faustus, come! Thine hour is almost come.'

Mephistopheles gives him a dagger.

1147And Faustus now will come to do thee right.

[Faustus prepares to stab himself.]

Old Man

1148O, stay, good Faustus, stay thy desperate steps!
1149I see an angel hover o'er thy head,
1150And with a vial full of precious grace
1151Offers to pour the same into thy soul.
1152Then call for mercy and avoid despair.

Faustus

1153O friend, I feel thy words to comfort my distressèd soul.
1154Leave me a while to ponder on my sins.

Old Man

1155Faustus, I leave thee, but with grief of heart,
1156Fearing the enemy of thy hapless soul.

Exit.

Faustus

1157Accursèd Faustus, wretch, what hast thou done?
1158I do repent, and yet I do despair.
1159Hell strives with grace for conquest in my breast.
1160What shall I do to shun the snares of death?

Mephistopheles

1161Thou traitor, Faustus, I arrest thy soul
1162For disobedience to my sovereign lord.
1163Revolt, or I'll in piecemeal tear thy flesh.

Faustus

1164I do repent I e'er offended him.
1165Sweet Mephistopheles, entreat thy lord
1166To pardon my unjust presumption,
1167And with my blood again I will confirm
1168The former vow I made to Lucifer.

Mephistopheles

1169Do it then, Faustus, with unfeignèd heart,
1170Lest greater dangers do attend thy drift.

[Faustus cuts his arm and writes with his blood.]

Faustus

1171Torment, sweet friend, that base and agèd man
1172That durst dissuade me from thy Lucifer,
1173With greatest torment that our hell affords.

Mephistopheles

1174His faith is great. I cannot touch his soul.
1175But what I may afflict his body with
1176I will attempt, which is but little worth.

Faustus

1177One thing, good servant, let me crave of thee
1178To glut the longing of my heart's desire:
1179That I may have unto my paramour
1180That heavenly Helen, which I saw of late,
1181Whose sweet embraces may extinguish clear
1182These thoughts that do dissuade me from my vow,
1183And keep my vow I made to Lucifer.

Mephistopheles

1184This, or what else my Faustus shall desire,
1185Shall be performed in twinkling of an eye.

Enter HELEN again [brought in by MEPHISTOPHELES], passing over between two Cupids.

Faustus

1186Was this the face that launched a thousand ships
1187And burnt the topless towers of Ilium?
1188Sweet Helen, make me immortal with a kiss.

[They kiss.]

1189Her lips suck forth my soul. See where it flies!
1190Come, Helen, come, give me my soul again.

[They kiss again.]

1191Here will I dwell, for heaven is in these lips,
1192And all is dross that is not Helena.
1193I will be Paris, and for love of thee
1194Instead of Troy shall Wittenberg be sacked,
1195And I will combat with weak Menelaus,
1196And wear thy colours on my plumèd crest.
1197Yea, I will wound Achilles in the heel
1198And then return to Helen for a kiss.
1199O, thou art fairer than the evening's air,
1200Clad in the beauty of a thousand stars.
1201Brighter art thou than flaming Jupiter
1202When he appeared to hapless Semele,
1203More lovely than the monarch of the sky
1204In wanton Arethusa's azure arms;
1205And none but thou shalt be my paramour.

Exeunt.

[V.ii]

Thunder. Enter LUCIFER, BEELZEBUB, and MEPHISTOPHELES [above].

Lucifer

1206Thus from infernal Dis do we ascend
1207To view the subjects of our monarchy,
1208Those souls which sin seals the black sons of hell,
1209'Mong which as chief, Faustus, we come to thee,
1210Bringing with us lasting damnation
1211To wait upon thy soul. The time is come
1212Which makes it forfeit.

Mephistopheles

Which makes it forfeit. And this gloomy night
1213Here in this room will wretchèd Faustus be.

Beelzebub

1214And here we'll stay
1215To mark him how he doth demean himself.

Mephistopheles

1216How should he, but in desperate lunacy?
1217Fond worldling, now his heart-blood dries with grief;
1218His conscience kills it, and his labouring brain
1219Begets a world of idle fantasies
1220To overreach the devil. But all in vain.
1221His store of pleasures must be sauced with pain.
1222He and his servant Wagner are at hand,
1223Both come from drawing Faustus' latest will.
1224See where they come.

Enter FAUSTUS and WAGNER.

Faustus

1225Say, Wagner. Thou hast perused my will;
1226How dost thou like it?

Wagner

How dost thou like it? Sir, so wondrous well
1227As in all humble duty I do yield
1228My life and lasting service for your love.

Enter the SCHOLARS.

Faustus

1229Gramercies, Wagner.—Welcome, gentlemen.

[Exit WAGNER.]

First Scholar

Now, worthy Faustus, methinks your looks

are changed.

Faustus

O gentlemen!

Second Scholar

What ails Faustus?

Faustus

Ah, my sweet chamber-fellow! Had I lived with thee,

then had I lived still, but now must die eternally.

Look sirs, comes he not? Comes he not?

First Scholar

O my dear Faustus, what imports this fear?

Second Scholar

Is all our pleasure turned to melancholy?

Third Scholar

[To the other Scholars.] He is not well with

being over-solitary.

Second Scholar

If it be so, we'll have physicians, and

Faustus shall be cured.

Third Scholar

[To Faustus.] 'Tis but a surfeit, sir. Fear nothing.

Faustus

A surfeit of deadly sin, that hath damned both

body and soul.

Second Scholar

Yet, Faustus, look up to heaven, and remember

mercy is infinite.

Faustus

But Faustus's offence can ne'er be pardoned. The

serpent that tempted Eve may be saved, but not Faustus.

O gentlemen, hear with patience, and tremble not

at my speeches. Though my heart pant and quiver to

remember that I have been a student here these thirty

years, O, would I had never seen Wittenberg, never read

book! And what wonders I have done, all Germany can

witness, yea, all the world, for which Faustus hath

lost both Germany and the world, yea, heaven itself—

heaven, the seat of God, the throne of the blessed,

the kingdom of joy—and must remain in hell for ever.

Hell, O, hell for ever! Sweet friends, what shall

become of Faustus, being in hell for ever?

Second Scholar

Yet, Faustus, call on God.

Faustus

On God, whom Faustus hath abjured? On God, whom

Faustus hath blasphemed? O my God, I would weep,

but the devil draws in my tears. Gush forth blood

instead of tears, yea, life and soul. O, he stays

my tongue! I would lift up my hands, but see, they

hold 'em, they hold 'em.

All

Who, Faustus?

Faustus

Why, Lucifer and Mephistopheles. O gentlemen,

I gave them my soul for my cunning.

All

O, God forbid!

Faustus

God forbade it indeed, but Faustus hath done it.

For the vain pleasure of four-and-twenty years hath Faustus

lost eternal joy and felicity. I writ them a bill with

mine own blood. The date is expired. This is the

time, and he will fetch me.

First Scholar

Why did not Faustus tell us of this before,

that divines might have prayed for thee?

Faustus

Oft have I thought to have done so, but the devil

threatened to tear me in pieces if I named God, to

fetch me body and soul if I once gave ear to divinity.

And now 'tis too late. Gentlemen, away, lest you

perish with me.

Second Scholar

O, what may we do to save Faustus?

Faustus

Talk not of me, but save yourselves and depart.

Third Scholar

God will strengthen me. I will stay with

Faustus.

First Scholar

[To the Third Scholar] Tempt not God, sweet friend, but let us into the next room and pray for him.

Faustus

Ay, pray for me, pray for me! And what noise

soever you hear, come not unto me, for nothing can

rescue me.

Second Scholar

Pray thou, and we will pray that God may

have mercy upon thee.

Faustus

Gentlemen, farewell. If I live till morning,

I'll visit you; if not, Faustus is gone to hell.

All

Faustus, farewell.

Exeunt SCHOLARS.

Mephistopheles

1230Ay, Faustus, now thou hast no hope of heaven;
1231Therefore despair. Think only upon hell,
1232For that must be thy mansion, there to dwell.

Faustus

1233O thou bewitching fiend, 'twas thy temptation
1234Hath robbed me of eternal happiness.

Mephistopheles

1235I do confess it, Faustus, and rejoice.
1236'Twas I that, when thou wert i'the way to heaven,
1237Dammed up thy passage. When thou took'st the book
1238To view the Scriptures, then I turned the leaves
1239And led thine eye.
1240What, weep'st thou? 'Tis too late. Despair, farewell!
1241Fools that will laugh on earth must weep in hell.

Exit.

Enter the GOOD ANGEL and the BAD ANGEL at several doors.

Good Angel

1242O Faustus, if thou hadst given ear to me,
1243Innumerable joys had followed thee.
1244But thou didst love the world.

Bad Angel

But thou didst love the world. Gave ear to me,
1245And now must taste hell's pains perpetually.

Good Angel

1246O, what will all thy riches, pleasures, pomps
1247Avail thee now?

Bad Angel

Avail thee now? Nothing but vex thee more,
1248To want in hell, that had on earth such store.

Music while the throne descends.

Good Angel

1249O, thou hast lost celestial happiness,
1250Pleasures unspeakable, bliss without end.
1251Hadst thou affected sweet divinity,
1252Hell or the devil had had no power on thee.
1253Hadst thou kept on that way, Faustus, behold
1254In what resplendent glory thou hadst set
1255In yonder throne, like those bright shining saints,
1256And triumphed over hell. That hast thou lost.
1257And now, poor soul, must thy good angel leave thee.
1258The jaws of hell are open to receive thee.

[The throne ascends.] Exit [GOOD ANGEL].

Hell is discovered.

Bad Angel

1259Now, Faustus, let thine eyes with horror stare
1260Into that vast perpetual torture-house.
1261There are the Furies tossing damnèd souls
1262On burning forks; their bodies boil in lead.
1263There are live quarters broiling on the coals,
1264That ne'er can die. This ever-burning chair
1265Is for o'er-tortured souls to rest them in.
1266These that are fed with sops of flaming fire
1267Were gluttons, and loved only delicates,
1268And laughed to see the poor starve at their gates.
1269But yet all these are nothing. Thou shalt see
1270Ten thousand tortures that more horrid be.

Faustus

1271O, I have seen enough to torture me!

Bad Angel

1272Nay, thou must feel them, taste the smart of All.
1273He that loves pleasure must for pleasure of All.
1274And so I leave thee, Faustus, till anon;
1275Then wilt thou tumble in confusion.

Exit.

The clock strikes eleven.

Faustus

1276O Faustus,
1277Now hast thou but one bare hour to live,
1278And then thou must be damned perpetually.
1279Stand still, you ever-moving spheres of heaven,
1280That time may cease and midnight never come!
1281Fair nature's eye, rise, rise again, and make
1282Perpetual day; or let this hour be but
1283A year, a month, a week, a natural day,
1284That Faustus may repent and save his soul!
1285O lente, lente currite noctis equi!
1286The stars move still; time runs; the clock will strike;
1287The devil will come, and Faustus must be damned.
1288O, I'll leap up to heaven! Who pulls me down?
1289One drop of blood will save me. O, my Christ!
1290Rend not my heart for naming of my Christ!
1291Yet will I call on him. O, spare me, Lucifer!
1292Where is it now? 'Tis gone;
1293And see a threat'ning arm, an angry brow.
1294Mountains and hills, come, come and fall on me,
1295And hide me from the heavy wrath of heaven!
1296No? Then will I headlong run into the earth.
1297Gape, earth! O, no, it will not harbour me.
1298You stars that reigned at my nativity,
1299Whose influence hath allotted death and hell,
1300Now draw up Faustus like a foggy mist
1301Into the entrails of yon labouring cloud,
1302That when you vomit forth into the air,
1303My limbs may issue from your smoky mouths,
1304But let my soul mount and ascend to heaven.

The watch strikes.

1305O, half the hour is past! 'Twill all be past anon.
1306O, if my soul must suffer for my sin,
1307Impose some end to my incessant pain.
1308Let Faustus live in hell a thousand years,
1309A hundred thousand, and at last be saved.
1310No end is limited to damnèd souls.
1311Why wert thou not a creature wanting soul?
1312Or why is this immortal that thou hast?
1313O, Pythagoras' metempsychosis , were that true,
1314This soul should fly from me and I be changed
1315Into some brutish beast.
1316All beasts are happy, for, when they die,
1317Their souls are soon dissolved in elements;
1318But mine must live still to be plagued in hell.
1319Curst be the parents that engendered me!
1320No, Faustus, curse thyself. Curse Lucifer
1321That hath deprived thee of the joys of heaven.

The clock strikes twelve.

1322It strikes, it strikes! Now, body, turn to air,
1323Or Lucifer will bear thee quick to hell.
1324O soul, be changed into small waterdrops,
1325And fall into the ocean, ne'er be found!

Thunder, and enter the Devils.

1326O, mercy, heaven, look not so fierce on me!
1327Adders and serpents, let me breathe a while!
1328Ugly hell, gape not. Come not, Lucifer!
1329I'll burn my books. O, Mephistopheles!

Exeunt.

[V.iii]

Enter the SCHOLARS.

First Scholar

1330Come, gentlemen, let us go visit Faustus,
1331For such a dreadful night was never seen
1332Since first the world's creation did begin.
1333Such fearful shrieks and cries were never heard.
1334Pray heaven the doctor have escaped the danger.

Second Scholar

1335O, help us, heaven! See, here are Faustus' limbs,
1336All torn asunder by the hand of death.

Third Scholar

1337The devils whom Faustus served have torn him thus.
1338For, twixt the hours of twelve and one, methought
1339I heard him shriek and call aloud for help,
1340At which self time the house seemed all on fire
1341With dreadful horror of these damnèd fiends.

Second Scholar

1342Well, gentlemen, though Faustus' end be such
1343As every Christian heart laments to think on,
1344Yet, for he was a scholar, once admired
1345For wondrous knowledge in our German schools,
1346We'll give his mangled limbs due burial;
1347And all the students, clothed in mourning black,
1348Shall wait upon his heavy funeral.

Exeunt.

Enter CHORUS.

Chorus

1349Cut is the branch that might have grown full straight,
1350And burnèd is Apollo's laurel bough
1351That sometime grew within this learnèd man.
1352Faustus is gone. Regard his hellish fall,
1353Whose fiendful fortune may exhort the wise
1354Only to wonder at unlawful things,
1355Whose deepness doth entice such forward wits
1356To practise more than heavenly power permits.

[Exit.]

Terminat hora diem; terminat author opus.
