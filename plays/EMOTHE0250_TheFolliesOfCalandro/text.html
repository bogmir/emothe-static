The Follies of Calandro

Act I

Scene I

Fessenio, alone

Fessenio

1How true it is that man makes one plan and fortune makes another for him! Just when we thought we were quietly settled in Bologna, my master Lidio learned that his sister Santilla was alive and in Italy, and immediately his love for her was revived –a love stronger that ever brother felt toward sister, since, being twins, they were so alike appearance, in speech, and in manner that at Modone, if Lidio were dressed as a little girl and Santilla as a little boy, their own mother and nurse could not tell them apart, let alone strangers. God Himself could not have made them more alike, and it was only natural that each should love the other more than himself. Therefore Lidio, who had thought his sister dead, has now set out to find her. Four months ago, coming to Rome to look for her, he met the Roman lady Fulvia and fell in love whit her. So as to have his way with her, he had her husband Calandro hire me as a servant, and with her consent I immediately arranged it so that the lovers could meet whenever they wished by having Lidio visit her dressed as a woman answering to the name of Santilla. But lately, fearful that they should be discovered, he has shown himself increasingly neglectful of her, and pretends that he want to leave Rome. Fulvia is so upset about this that she can scarcely control herself, and has been consulting soothsayers and magicians; she has come to me about it, and has even sent her maid, Samia, who is in her confidence, to Lidio with prayers, with gifts, and with promises to give her son in marriage to his sister, should Lidio succeed in finding her. All of this has been done so openly that if her husband were not more of a sheep than a man he would have known about it long ago –in which case I would have had to take all the blame: it’s a good thing that I know how to take care of myself. I am in an impossible position: it’s bad enough to serve two masters, and I am trying to serve three –the husband, the wife, and my own master. I never have a moment’s rest. Oh, well, I don’t mind too much: a man who has nothing to do is as good as dead, and if it is true that a good servants is never idle, why then I am the best, for I never even have time to clean my ears. And speaking of Lidio, here he comes with Polinico, his teacher. When you see a dolphin, as the saying goes, you know a storm is brewing. I am going to step aside a moment and listen to what they are saying.

Scene II

Polinico, Lidio, Fessenio

Polinico

2I never would have thought, Lidio, that you would waste your time on idle love affairs. You are becoming an utterly worthless person, and I blame it all on that rascal Fessenio.

Fessenio

3(apart) Aha!

Lidio

4You shouldn’t say that, Polinico.

Polinico

5Ah, Lidio, I am wiser than you and that wretched servant of yours.

Fessenio

6(apart) We shall see about that.

Polinico

7A prudent man always thinks of what may happen in the future.

Fessenio

8(apart) Here we go with the lessons again.

Polinico

9Should this affair of yours become known, you would not only be in danger of your life but would lose the respect of everybody.

Fessenio

10(apart) Stupid pedagogue!

Polinico

11Nobody has any use for a playboy. And to think that you have been carrying on with one of the most respected women in our city! Take my advice, Lidio, and get out of it before it is too late.

Lidio

12Polinico, I am young, and young people have always been ruled by love; older people can afford to think of more serious things. I must do as love commands, and it has bidden me to love the lady Fulvia even more than I love myself. And I believe that many people, even if they knew, would not hold it against me, for, just as it is a sign of common sense in a woman not to love a man in a higher position, so it is a sign of merit in a man to love a lady of higher birth than himself.

Fessenio

13(approaching) Well said, my master.

Polinico

14These are arguments that you have learned from that miserable Fessenio.

Fessenio

15You are the miserable one.

Polinico

16(to Fessenio) Actually I’d be surprised if you didn’t try to spoil the good things that people try to do.

Fessenio

17I’m not planning to spoil anything of yours.

Polinico

18Nothing is worse than to see the lives of wise men governed by the saying of fools.

Fessenio

19I have always given him better advice than you.

Polinico

20No one can give good advice who has bad habits. If I had known you better, Fessenio, I would not have recommended you so highly to Lidio.

Fessenio

21Did I ever ask you for any favors?

Polinico

22Now I know that when a man praises another he is often deceived; when he criticizes another, he never is.

Fessenio

23The more fool you, for praising someone you did not know well. For my part, I know that when I speak of you I am never wrong.

Polinico

24Have you spoken evil of me?

Fessenio

25You have spoken evil of yourself.

Polinico

26I must be patient. I am not going to argue with you; it would be like shouting at the thunder.

Fessenio

27You won’t because you know you are in the wrong.

Polinico

28I am trying to avoid other means besides words.

Fessenio

29And what harm could you ever do to me in a hundred years?

Polinico

30Never mind, never mind. You would soon find out.

Fessenio

31My, my! I’d better be careful. Wild beasts can be dangerous.

Polinico

32I don’t want to argue with a servant.

Lidio

33That’s enough, Fessenio. Come off it.

Fessenio

34(to Polinico) Don’t threaten me. I may be just a servant, but even a fly has his rages, and even the smallest hair casts its shadow. Do I make myself clear?

Lidio

35Be quiet, Fessenio.

Polinico

36(to Fessenio) Let me continue my conversation with Lidio, if you please.

Fessenio

37Oh, he’ll do anything for the sake of peace.

Polinico

38(ignoring Fessenio) Lidio, God has given us two ears so that we can hear well.

Fessenio

39And only one mouth, so as not to talk too much.

Polinico

40(to Fessenio) I am not speaking to you. (To Lidio) A bad habit is easy to cure when it is new, but not when it has had time to grow old. Get rid of this passion, I tell you.

Lidio

41Why?

Polinico

42Ah, me, don’t you know that the companions of love are anger, hatred, enmity, disagreement, ruin, poverty, suspicion, worry, pernicious diseases in the souls of men? Avoid love, avoid it.

Lidio

43Ah, Polinico, I cannot.

Polinico

44And why?

Fessenio

45Damn you anyway, with all your whys and wherefores!

Lidio

46Everything is subject to the power of love, and there is no sweetness like that of fulfilling one’s desire: without love there is nothing perfect, nothing virtuous, nothing good.

Fessenio

47No one could have said it better.

Polinico

48There is no greater vice in a servant than flattery. And you, Lidio, do you listen to him? Listen to me instead.

Fessenio

49Yes, listen to him; he’s such a pretty piece of merchandise.

Polinico

50Love is like fire, when sulfur or some other vile substance is sprinkled over it, sends off evil odors that suffocate a man.

Fessenio

51And when incense, aloes, and amber are sprinkled over it, it makes a fragrance that will revive even the dead.

Lidio

52Aha, Polinico is caught in his own trap.

Polinico

53Return, Lidio, to what is worthwhile.

Fessenio

54What is worthwhile is adjusting to the times.

Polinico

55What is worthwhile is what is good and honest. I warn you that this affair is going to turn out badly.

Fessenio

56The prophet has spoken!

Polinico

57Remember that a virtuous soul is not moved by greed.

Fessenio

58Nor discouraged by fear.

Polinico

59(to Fessenio) You also do wrong when you know it is arrogance to ridicule the advice of wise men.

Fessenio

60At the same time that you call yourself a wise man you christen yourself a fool: you know there is no folly like that of attempting the impossible.

Polinico

61It is better to lose, speaking the truth, than to win telling lies.

Fessenio

62I tell the truth as well as you, but I don’t go around criticizing everything and everybody the way you: just because you know a few words of Latin you think everyone except yourself is an idiot. You are not Salomon, you know. You never stop to consider that is right for an old man may not be right for a young one; that what is right in time of danger may not be right at ordinary times. You, who are old, cling to the kind of life that old men remember, but you must allow Lidio, who is young, to lead the kind of life that is proper for young men nowadays, and adjust yourself to the idea.

Polinico

63It is certainly true that the more servants a man has, the more enemies. This fellow will lead you to the gallows, Lidio, and even if you had no other troubles you would always have a remorseful soul, for there is no torture so great as knowing the mistakes that you have made. Therefore leave this mistress of yours.

Lidio

64I can no more leave her than a form can leave its shadow.

Polinico

65Indeed, it might be better for you to hate her than to leave her.

Fessenio

66Oh, oh! He can’t even carry a calf and you want him to carry an ox.

Polinico

67She will leave you soon enough.

Lidio

68Oh, all women are not the same.

Polinico

69They may not look the same but they have the same nature.

Lidio

70You are wrong there.

Polinico

71Lidio, don’t be blind: they are all alike, and you cannot believe one even when she is dead.

Fessenio

72Now I am beginning to see the light.

Polinico

73What’s that?

Fessenio

74You are adjusting to the times.

Polinico

75I am merely speaking the truth to Lidio. What are you driving at?

Fessenio

76I mean that you have made an adjustment to the modern fashion.

Polinico

77How so?

Fessenio

78In disliking women, as almost everyone at this court does. But when you speak of them in this way, you do them an injustice.

Lidio

79Fessenio is right. I cannot approve of what you have said about women; they are the greatest source of goodness and comfort in the world, and without them we would be useless, inadequate, harsh, and like the beasts of the field.

Fessenio

80It is necessary to say all this? Everyone knows that women are so desirable that nowadays all the men go about imitating them and would willingly become women both in body and in soul.

Polinico

81Such comments do not deserve a reply.

Fessenio

82You mean you can’t think of one.

Polinico

83Remember Lidio, that you can still get out of this if you try. For your own good, I beg you once more to do so.

Lidio

84Polinico, there is nothing in the world that heeds advice less than love, whose nature is such that a man will allow himself to be consumed by it rather than give it up because someone tells him to. So that if you try to make me give up this woman it is like trying to catch a shadow or snare the wind in a net.

Polinico

85And this worries me very much. You used to be as easily molded as wax, and now you are headed for the worst kind of trouble. You don’t know what this woman is really like, and I warn you once more: This is all going to end very badly for you.

Lidio

86I don’t believe it, and even if it were true, haven’t you taught me in your lessons that the man who dies in love dies happy?

Polinico

87Well, I have warned you. Do as you please, and listen to this jackass instead of me: you will soon find out the damage that love can cause.

Fessenio

88You have said enough, Polinico. Do you know what damage love causes?

Polinico

89What, jackass?

Fessenio

90Like a truffle, it causes the fortunes of young men to rise, and old men to fart.

Lidio

91Ha, ha, ha!

Polinico

92Ah, Lidio, you laugh about it, and pay no attention to what I am trying to say. Well, I shall leave you on your own, and go about my business.

Exit

Fessenio

93What a pity. Did you notice how pious he pretends to be, as if we didn’t know he’s the world’s biggest hypocrite? I haven’t been able to get a word in edgewise, and I have good news about Calandro.

Lidio

94Let us hear it. It may take some of the sting out of Polinico’s words.

Scene III

Lidio, Fessenio

Lidio

95Well, now, tell me what has been going on.

Fessenio

96Fulvia’s husband, Calandro –that sheep whom I pretend is my master and whom you are cuckolding– seeing you dressed as a woman and calling yourself Santilla, has been completely taken in and has approached me to see if I could arrange for you to sleep with him: I have arranged it for this very evening.

Lidio

97Ho, ho! This is really something. And now that you mention it I remember that the other day, as I was returning from Fulvia dressed as a woman, he followed me for a short distance –I never guessed the reason. Let’s lead him on, shall we?

Fessenio

98Leave everything to me. I’ll make him think I’ve done miracles for him, and you may be sure he will believe everything I tell him. I tell him the wildest things in the world and he never once doubts me; he is the world’s biggest simpleton. I could give you a thousand examples, but to make a long story short I will simply say that there is so much nonsense in him that a single speck of it, found in Solomon, Aristotle, or Seneca, would be sufficient to discredit all the wisdom of those men. And the funniest thing of all is that he thinks himself so handsome and so charming that he imagines every woman who sees him falls in love with him, as if he were the most dashing young fellow alive. He is such an ox that it wouldn’t surprise me to see him eat hay, and for this reason it will be easy for us to lead him where we like.

Lidio

99Ha, ha, ha! I am going to die of laughter. But tell me, what’s going to happen when he comes up to me, thinking I am a woman?

Fessenio

100I’ll take care of that; leave it to me. But look, I see him now, coming our way. Go quickly: he mustn’t see us together.

Scene IV

Calandro, Fessenio

Calandro

101Fessenio.

Fessenio

102Who’s calling me? Oh, it’s my master.

Calandro

103Tell me, what about Santilla?

Fessenio

104You say, what about Santilla?

Calandro

105Yes.

Fessenio

106Well, let’s see... I don’t know for sure, but I think she is wearing a skirt, a blouse, an apron, a pair of gloves, and a pair of slippers.

Calandro

107You idiot, I don’t want to know what she is wearing, but how she is.

Fessenio

108Oh, I see. You want to know how she is.

Calandro

109If you please.

Fessenio

110Well, when I saw her a few minutes ago she was... wait, let’s see... she was sitting with her hand up to her chin, listening while I talked to her about you. Her eyes and mouth were wide open, and her little tongue hung out slightly, like this.

Calandro

111That’s more like it. So she listened willingly, eh?

Fessenio

112Indeed she did. And I have prepared her so that before long you will have your wish.

Calandro

113Good for you, dear Fessenio.

Fessenio

114I hope it will be.

Calandro

115Never doubt it. Oh! Fessenio, give me a hand: I am afraid I don’t feel very well.

Fessenio

116Why, what’s the matter, master? Do you have a fever?

Calandro

117No, of course not, stupid. It’s Santilla.

Fessenio

118Did she hit you?

Calandro

119Oh, what a fool you are! I mean I am so much in love that it hurts.

Fessenio

120Well, you will soon be with her.

Calandro

121What are we waiting for? Let’s go to her now.

Fessenio

122Not so fast. There are still a few details I have to attend to.

Calandro

123All right, but be as quick as you can.

Fessenio

124I will; don’t worry.

Calandro

125Don’t forget, now.

Fessenio

126(Exit Calandro.) I won’t. You’ll see. Ha, ha, ha! This is quite a situation: both husband and wife are in love with the same person. And now I see Fulvia’s servant, Samia, coming out of the house. Something is going on; I can tell by the look on her face. I’ll talk to her: she knows everything.

Scene V

Fessenio, Samia

Fessenio

127Samia, Samia, wait a minute!

Samia

128Oh, hello, Fessenio.

Fessenio

129What’s going on inside?

Samia

130Nothing that’s good for my mistress.

Fessenio

131How do you mean?

Samia

132She’s in a bad way.

Fessenio

133Why, what’s wrong?

Samia

134I’d better not say.

Fessenio

135Not say what?

Samia

136Well, frankly, she’s too anxious.

Fessenio

137To do what?

Samia

138Well... to enjoy herself with Lidio. Do I make myself clear?

Fessenio

139Oh, I knew that as well as you.

Samia

140But there is something else you don’t know.

Fessenio

141What?

Samia

142She is sending me to someone who will make Lidio do as she wishes.

Fessenio

143Oh? How will this “someone” make him do that?

Samia

144By means of songs.

Fessenio

145Songs?

Samia

146Yes, sir.

Fessenio

147And who is this musician?

Samia

148Who said anything about a musician? I said she was sending me to someone who would make Lidio go back to her, or die in the attempt.

Fessenio

149Who is this fellow?

Samia

150Ruffo the magician, who will do whatever needs to be done.

Fessenio

151How does he do that?

Samia

152He has a linguistic spirit.

Fessenio

153You mean a familiar spirit.

Samia

154I don’t understand all these words. All I’m supposed to do is tell the magician to come to my mistress. Good-bye, and don’t tell anyone what I’ve said.

Fessenio

155Don’t worry; I won’t. Good-bye.

Scene VI

Samia, Ruffo

Samia

156It’s too early to catch Ruffo at his dinner; I’d better look in the piazza to see if he’s there. Oh, what luck! I see him. Ruffo! Don’t you hear me, Ruffo?

Ruffo

157(turning around) I hear someone calling me.

Samia

158Ruffo, wait a minute!

Ruffo

159Who can this woman be?

Samia

160Oh, I am all out of breath.

Ruffo

161Well, what is it?

Samia

162My mistress begs you to go to her right away.

Ruffo

163Who is your mistress?

Samia

164The lady Fulvia.

Ruffo

165Calandro’s wife?

Samia

166Yes, sir.

Ruffo

167What does she want from me?

Samia

168She will tell you.

Ruffo

169That’s her house over there, isn’t it?

Samia

170Yes, it’s only a few steps. Shall we go?

Ruffo

171You go ahead and I’ll follow. (Aside) Could this be one of the many foolish women who think I am a magician and have a familiar spirit, as they call it? I think I know what she wants, so I’ll go to her house before this fellow who is coming this way gets here.

Scene VII

Fessenio, Calandro

Fessenio

172Now I see that the gods have their jokes as well as human beings: here is love, who is accustomed to dwell in noble hearts, sticking so firmly to this sheep Calandro that it’s impossible to dislodge him. He must not have much to do, to visit such a baboon.

Calandro

173Fessenio, oh, Fessenio!

Fessenio

174Who is it? Oh, it’s you, my master.

Calandro

175Have you seen Santilla?

Fessenio

176I have.

Calandro

177How do things look?

Fessenio

178You will have your way, and what a morsel! You certainly have good taste. You must do everything you can to win her.

Calandro

179I’ll get her, even if I have to go nude and barefoot.

Fessenio

180(aside) Just listen to this, all you lovers.

Calandro

181If I get her, I shall eat her up.

Fessenio

182Eat her up! Ah, Calandro, have a little pity. Wild beasts may eat one another, but men don’t eat women. You’re supposed to drink them, no eat them.

Calandro

183Drink them?

Fessenio

184Yes, drink them.

Calandro

185But how?

Fessenio

186Don’t you know?

Calandro

187I’m afraid I don’t.

Fessenio

188What a pity that a fine-looking fellow like you doesn’t know how to drink women.

Calandro

189Come, tell me how.

Fessenio

190All right. When you kiss one, don’t you suck her lips?

Calandro

191Yes.

Fessenio

192And when you drink something, don’t you suck it also?

Calandro

193Yes.

Fessenio

194Well, then: when you kiss a woman, you suck her lips, so you are drinking her.

Calandro

195It does seem so, and yet I have never drunk Fulvia, though I have kissed her many times.

Fessenio

196Ah, but that’s because she was kissing you, too. She was sucking your lips at the same time that you were sucking hers, which is why neither of you was drinking the other.

Calandro

197You are a clever man, Fessenio. It is true that I never once kissed Fulvia without her kissing me in return.

Fessenio

198There, you see?

Calandro

199But tell me. I used to know a Spanish girl who was always kissing my hand: did that mean she wanted to drink it?

Fessenio

200I’ll tell you a secret. When a Spanish girl kissed you, it isn’t because she loves you; it’s because she wants to drink your hands –or rather to suck the rings on your finger.

Calandro

201Fessenio, you know more about women’s secrets!

Fessenio

202(aside) Especially your woman’s.

Calandro

203That Spanish girl drank two rings of mine. I swear before God that from now on I will never let anybody drink me again.

Fessenio

204A wise decision.

Calandro

205And I’ll never let anyone kiss me unless I kiss her at the same time.

Fessenio

206Yes, do be careful, because if a girl should ever drink your nose, or one of your cheeks, or an eye, you would be the ugliest man in the world.

Calandro

207I’ll be careful, all right. Buy be sure to fix it so I can sleep with Santilla.

Fessenio

208Leave it to me. I’ll bring it off.

Calandro

209And be quiet about it, won’t you?

Fessenio

210I am going to see her now, and I’ll be back soon.

Scene VIII

Ruffo, alone

Ruffo

211A man should never give up hope: fortune sometimes comes when it is least expected. Fulvia, as I thought, believes I have a familiar spirit. She is passionately in love with a young man, and, every other means having failed, has come to me to see if I can arrange to have him go back to her dressed like a woman. She has promised me money if I can do this, and I think I can because the man she loves is Lidio, a friend of mine from my home town. I also know his servant Fannio, so I shouldn’t have too much trouble. But I haven’t promised her anything; I wanted to talk with Lidio first. The lady Fulvia can make both our fortunes if things go right. I’ll go up to Perillo’s house now, where Lidio is staying. It’s about dinnertime, so he should be there.

Act II

Scene I

Santilla as Lidio, Fannio, a nurse

Santilla

212Men are certainly more fortunate than women, and I can bear better witness to this than most women: ever since that day when Modone was burned by the Turks and I dressed as a boy and took my brother’s name, things have gone very well for me. Whereas if I had let it be known that I was a girl, the Turks would have kept me as a slave and not sold me to Perillo, and perhaps Perillo would not have bought me if he had known I was a girl. And now, because he has always been pleased with my behaviour, he wants me to marry his only daughter, whom he plans to leave everything to when he dies. He wants the marriage to take place right away, and therefore I have come to you, nurse, and to you, Fannio, to discuss this thing, which, as you can imagine, causes me some concern.

Fannio

213Shh, quiet! –or this woman I see coming toward us will hear.

Scene II

Samia, Santilla as Lidio, Fannio

Samia

214My mistress really has it bad. She says she saw Lidio from her window, so she sent me to talk to him. I’ll see if I can draw him aside. Good day, sir.

Santilla

215Good day.

Samia

216May I have a word alone with you, sir?

Santilla

217Who are you?

Samia

218You ask me who I am?

Santilla

219Naturally, since I don’t know.

Samia

220Well, you’ll find out soon enough.

Santilla

221What is it you want?

Samia

222My mistress begs you to love her as she loves you and says you are to feel free to come to her whenever you wish.

Santilla

223I don’t understand. Who is your mistress?

Samia

224Ah, Lidio, you want to make fun of me.

Santilla

225It is you who are making fun of me.

Samia

226It’s certainly odd that you pretend not to know either me or my mistress. Come, what shall I say to her?

Santilla

227I am not going to answer you until you explain yourself.

Samia

228So you don’t understand me, eh?

Santilla

229I don’t understand you; neither do I know you. Nor do I have any desire to. Please be on your way.

Samia

230You certainly are discreet. I’ll tell her that much.

Santilla

231Tell her what you like, as long as you leave me alone. I’m tired of looking at you.

Samia

232You are, are you? You miserable Greek, you’ll go see my mistress if it cost you your life, because she is sending me to the magician, and his spirit will make you do whatever he wants.

Exit

Santilla

233Ah, me, this only proves what I was saying: sad indeed is the fortune of us poor women! Things like this only serve to make me realize it.

Fannio

234I wish we had got more out of her; it couldn’t have done any harm.

Santilla

235My mind was on something more important. But is she had been more willing to talk, I would have shown myself more willing to listen.

Fannio

236I know who she is.

Santilla

237Who?

Fannio

238The servant of Fulvia, a noble Roman lady.

Santilla

239Oh, I know who you mean. And Fulvia is a good name for her.

Scene III

Ruffo, Santilla as Lidio, Tiresia, Fannio

Ruffo

240Hello there!

Santilla

241Whose voice is that?

Ruffo

242I have been looking everywhere for you.

Fannio

243Hello, Ruffo. What is it?

Ruffo

244Good news.

Fannio

245Let’s hear it.

Santilla

246Wait a minute. (To Tiresia) Tiresia, go to the house, see what Perillo is doing about this wedding of mine, and when Fannio comes there, send him back to me so I can find out what’s going on. Today I am going to keep out of sight, “As long as there is time, there is hope”. Go quickly now. (Exit Tiresia) Now, Ruffo, tell me your good news.

Ruffo
