Bernardo Dovizi da Bibbiena

The Follies of Calandro

← Act I Act III →

Act II

Scene I

Santilla as Lidio, Fannio, a nurse

Santilla

212Men are certainly more fortunate than women, and I can bear better witness to this than most women: ever since that day when Modone was burned by the Turks and I dressed as a boy and took my brother’s name, things have gone very well for me. Whereas if I had let it be known that I was a girl, the Turks would have kept me as a slave and not sold me to Perillo, and perhaps Perillo would not have bought me if he had known I was a girl. And now, because he has always been pleased with my behaviour, he wants me to marry his only daughter, whom he plans to leave everything to when he dies. He wants the marriage to take place right away, and therefore I have come to you, nurse, and to you, Fannio, to discuss this thing, which, as you can imagine, causes me some concern.

Fannio

213Shh, quiet! –or this woman I see coming toward us will hear.

Scene II

Samia, Santilla as Lidio, Fannio

Samia

214My mistress really has it bad. She says she saw Lidio from her window, so she sent me to talk to him. I’ll see if I can draw him aside. Good day, sir.

Santilla

215Good day.

Samia

216May I have a word alone with you, sir?

Santilla

217Who are you?

Samia

218You ask me who I am?

Santilla

219Naturally, since I don’t know.

Samia

220Well, you’ll find out soon enough.

Santilla

221What is it you want?

Samia

222My mistress begs you to love her as she loves you and says you are to feel free to come to her whenever you wish.

Santilla

223I don’t understand. Who is your mistress?

Samia

224Ah, Lidio, you want to make fun of me.

Santilla

225It is you who are making fun of me.

Samia

226It’s certainly odd that you pretend not to know either me or my mistress. Come, what shall I say to her?

Santilla

227I am not going to answer you until you explain yourself.

Samia

228So you don’t understand me, eh?

Santilla

229I don’t understand you; neither do I know you. Nor do I have any desire to. Please be on your way.

Samia

230You certainly are discreet. I’ll tell her that much.

Santilla

231Tell her what you like, as long as you leave me alone. I’m tired of looking at you.

Samia

232You are, are you? You miserable Greek, you’ll go see my mistress if it cost you your life, because she is sending me to the magician, and his spirit will make you do whatever he wants.

Exit

Santilla

233Ah, me, this only proves what I was saying: sad indeed is the fortune of us poor women! Things like this only serve to make me realize it.

Fannio

234I wish we had got more out of her; it couldn’t have done any harm.

Santilla

235My mind was on something more important. But is she had been more willing to talk, I would have shown myself more willing to listen.

Fannio

236I know who she is.

Santilla

237Who?

Fannio

238The servant of Fulvia, a noble Roman lady.

Santilla

239Oh, I know who you mean. And Fulvia is a good name for her.

Scene III

Ruffo, Santilla as Lidio, Tiresia, Fannio

Ruffo

240Hello there!

Santilla

241Whose voice is that?

Ruffo

242I have been looking everywhere for you.

Fannio

243Hello, Ruffo. What is it?

Ruffo

244Good news.

Fannio

245Let’s hear it.

Santilla

246Wait a minute. (To Tiresia) Tiresia, go to the house, see what Perillo is doing about this wedding of mine, and when Fannio comes there, send him back to me so I can find out what’s going on. Today I am going to keep out of sight, “As long as there is time, there is hope”. Go quickly now. (Exit Tiresia) Now, Ruffo, tell me your good news.

Ruffo

247Though I have known you and Fannio only a short time, I have grown quite fond of you. After all, we are from the same home town, aren’t we? And now something has come up that can work to our mutual advantage.

Santilla

248We are fond of you, too, Ruffo, and will be happy to help if we can. But what is it?

Ruffo

249I’ll be brief. Lidio, a certain woman has fallen in love with you and wants you to return her love. Other means having proved unsuccessful, she has come to me; this is because, since I know a few tricks and can read palms, I have the reputation among women –you know how credulous they are –of being a magician. They think I have a familiar spirit by means of which I can do or undo anything I want. I have allowed them to believe this because sometimes it has proved both useful and pleasant –as it will in the case of this woman, if you cooperate. She wants me to persuade you to go to bed with her, and I, thinking you would be agreeable to the idea, have given her some hope. This is all you have to do –it will make us both rich, and you will have the pleasure of sleeping with her as well.

Santilla

250Ruffo, this is a pretty tricky business, and as I have no experience in such things I could easily be made a fool of. I’ll think it over and discuss it with Fannio. But tell me: who is this woman?

Ruffo

251Her name is Fulvia. She’s rich, of noble family, and also beautiful.

Fannio

252Oh! The mistress of that woman we were just talking to.

Santilla

253Yes, she’s the one.

Ruffo

254Her maid spoke to you?

Santilla

255Just a few minutes ago.

Ruffo

256And what did you say?

Santilla

257I sent her away with strong words.

Ruffo

258Too bad. Well, the next time you talk to her you must be more agreeable, if we are to bring this off.

Santilla

259I will.

Fannio

260Tell me, Ruffo, when would Lidio have to go to her?

Ruffo

261The sooner the better.

Fannio

262By day or by night?

Ruffo

263By day.

Santilla

264Oh, someone would be sure to see me.

Ruffo

265She has thought of that. She wants me to have the spirit make him go in the form of a woman.

Fannio

266And what good would he be to the lady Fulvia, if your spirit changes him into a woman?

Ruffo

267I think she meant dressed like a woman. Anyway, that’s what she said.

Santilla

268How very extraordinary! Don’t you think so, Fannio?

Fannio

269I do indeed. As a matter of fact I like the idea.

Ruffo

270Are we agreed, then?

Santilla

271We’ll let you know in just a moment, after we’ve talked it over.

Ruffo

272Where shall we meet?

Fannio

273Right here.

Santilla

274And whoever comes first will wait for the other.

Ruffo

275Good enough. See you soon.

Scene IV

Fannio, Santilla as Lidio

Fannio

276This makes it easier for you to keep out of sight today, just as you wanted: no one would think of looking for you at Fulvia’s house. And knowing she is a whore, you can buy her silence as often as you please. But what I like best is the humor of the situation. You are a woman, and she wants you to go to her dressed like a woman: thus in satisfying her you will also be disappointing her.

Santilla

277Shall we do it?

Fannio

278By all means.

Santilla

279Very well. Now go home, find out what’s going on, get me the necessary clothes, and meet me at Franzino’s shop. We’ll tell Ruffo we’ll do as he says.

Fannio

280You’d better go, too. Here comes someone. Maybe Perillo has sent him to look for you.

Santilla

281He’s not one of our men. But you’re right; I mustn’t show myself. Good-bye for now.

Scene V

Fessenio, Fulvia

Fessenio

282There’s the lady Fulvia over there. I’ll go and tell her Lidio is determined to go away, just to see how she takes it.

Fulvia

283Welcome, dear Fessenio! Tell me, what news of Lidio?

Fessenio

284He doesn’t seem to be himself today.

Fulvia

285Oh? What’s the matter with him?

Fessenio

286He’s taken it into his head to go look for his sister Santilla.

Fulvia

287Ah, me. He wants to leave Rome?

Fessenio

288He seems set on it.

Fulvia

289Dear Fessenio, if you are interested in your own welfare, or in Lidio’s, and if mine means anything to you, you must urge him, persuade him, beg him not to leave on this account. I will search all Italy for this girl, and if we succeed in finding her, I give you my word that I will give her in marriage to Flaminio, my only son.

Fessenio

290Do you want me to promise him this?

Fulvia

291I have given my word, and I will keep it.

Fessenio

292I’m sure he’ll listen willingly to this; it’s a thing that’s certain to please him.

Fulvia

293Unless you can help me I am desperate. Plead with him to save my life, for it belongs to him.

Fessenio

294I’ll see what I can do. For your sake.

Fulvia

295For yours, too, dear Fessenio! Remember that.

Exit

Fessenio

296Well, well, may God have pity on her. It’s a good thing that Lidio is planning to pay her a visit today, dressed like a woman as he always does at such times. Though she doesn’t know it, he’s as hot for it as she is. But first I have to attend to Calandro, and there he is now. I’ll tell him everything is ready.

Scene VI

Fessenio, Calandro

Fessenio

297Greetings, master! You are in luck, for I bring good news. Give me your hand.

Calandro

298My hand –and my foot.

Fessenio

299(aside) My, how witty we are today!

Calandro

300(grumpily) What’s this news of yours that’s supposed to be so good?

Fessenio

301What is it? Why, man, the world is your oyster.

Calandro

302What do you mean?

Fessenio

303I mean that Santilla loves you even more than you love her, because I told her how generous you are, how good looking, and how wise. And now she is as eager for the bed as you are. I no sooner mentioned your name than she got all excited: you’re going to be the happiest man in all the world.

Calandro

304Are you telling me the truth? Ah, I can’t wait to suck those red lips and lovely cheeks, the color of wine and cottage cheese.

Fessenio

305(aside) Blood and milk –his favorite diet.

Calandro

306Dear Fessenio, you shall name your own reward.

Fessenio

307(aside) Such kindness, all of a sudden!

Calandro

308Let’s go to her now.

Fessenio

309Oh, no, you can’t do that! Do you think she’s a common whore? You will have to do this thing properly.

Calandro

310Well then, how am I to go to her?

Fessenio

311On your feet.

Calandro

312I know that, but in what manner?

Fessenio

313If you went there openly, someone would be sure to see you, so we have decided that you must hide in a trunk and let yourself be carried into her room: in that way you can best fulfill your mutual desire.

Calandro

314Ah, so I don’t go on my feet after all.

Fessenio

315I can see you are a shrewd one. That’s right.

Calandro

316It won’t be too uncomfortable, will it, Fessenio?

Fessenio

317No, no.

Calandro

318Will the trunk be big enough that I can get all of me inside?

Fessenio

319What difference does that make? If we can’t get you inside in one piece, we can do it in little pieces.

Calandro

320Little pieces? How do you mean?

Fessenio

321Just so. Little pieces.

Calandro

322But how?

Fessenio

323Why, don’t you know?

Calandro

324I do not.

Fessenio

325If you had ever sailed on a ship, you would. Because when there are hundreds of people that have to be fitted into a boat, the only way it can be done is to take a leg from one, an arm from another, and so on until they are all netly stowed away like any other kind of cargo. That way they don’t take up so much room.

Calandro

326But what happens after that?

Fessenio

327Why, when they get into port, each person claims the part of him that has been taken away, and sometimes, whether through carelessness or malice, a man will take a limb that belongs to someone else: that’s bad, because it’s either too big or too small, which makes him look crippled and lopsided. Now do you understand?

Calandro

328I do, and I shall take care that my member is not exchanged for someone else’s.

Fessenio

329If you don’t exchange it yourself, no one else is going to: that’s for certain. Anyway, you’ll be all alone in there. Of course you may have to be divided up before we can fit you in.

Calandro

330Where are the best places to divide a man?

Fessenio

331Why, wherever you see a bulge or a prominence of some kind, like up here, or down here. Would you like to see how it’s done?

Calandro

332If you please.

Fessenio

333There’s nothing to it, just a bit of magic. You have to repeat what I say, but in a low voice, because if you should shout, everything would be spoiled.

Calandro

334All right.

Fessenio

335First we’ll start with your hand. Give it to me, and repeat after me: Ambracullac.

Calandro

336Anculabrac.

Fessenio

337(tugging his arm) You said it wrong. Try again: Ambracullac.

Calandro

338Alabrucuc.

Fessenio

339(again tugging his arm) That’s even worse. Ambracullac.

Calandro

340Alucumbrac.

Fessenio

341No, no, no! look, say: Am.

Calandro

342Am.

Fessenio

343Bra.

Calandro

344Bra.

Fessenio

345Cul.

Calandro

346Cul.

Fessenio

347Lac.

Calandro

348Lac.

Fessenio

349Stupid.

Calandro

350Stupid.

Fessenio

351Ox.

Calandro

352Ox.

Fessenio

353Now.

Calandro

354Now.

Fessenio

355You’re.

Calandro

356Yo’re.

Fessenio

357Going.

Calandro

358Going.

Fessenio

359To.

Calandro

360To.

Fessenio

361(Twisting his arm violently.) Get it!

Calandro

362Ouch! Oh, my poor arm.

Fessenio

363Didn’t I tell you not to raise your voice? I never saw anyone with such a short memory and so little patience. Now you have broken the spell.

Calandro

364And you have broken my arm.

Fessenio

365It’s no use. We won’t be able to divide you up.

Calandro

366How shall we manage, then?

Fessenio

367I’ll have to find a truck big enough to hold you as you are.

Calandro

368I hope you can, because I don’t like all this dividing. My arm is killing me.

Fessenio

369I’ll go look for one now.

Calandro

370And I’ll go to the marketplace and come right back.

Exit

Fessenio

371Now I must find Lidio and tell him about this: it’s so funny we’ll be supplied with laughs the rest of the year. I’m going now because I don’t want to have to talk with Samia, whom I see over there muttering to herself.

Scene VII

Samia, Fulvia

Samia

372Ah, me, such is life! It hasn’t been more than a month since Lidio wanted to spend every minute with my mistress, and now, when he sees how much she is in love with him, he pays her no attention. If we don’t find some way out of this, I don’t know what Fulvia will do; she might cause a scandal, and I have a feeling that Calandro’s brother already suspect something . Heaven knows it’s obvious enough: all she thinks about is Lidio. How true it is that he who has love in his heart has spurs in his side!

Fulvia

373Samia!

Samia

374Coming!

Scene VIII

Santilla as Lidio, Fannio

Santilla

375Tiresia told you this?

Fannio

376Yes.

Santilla

377They are taking the wedding for granted?

Fannio

378They are.

Santilla

379Does Virginia seem happy about it?

Fannio

380She can hardly wait.

Santilla

381They are getting everything ready, then?

Fannio

382Yes, indeed; they have turned the house upside down.

Santilla

383They think I am pleased?

Fannio

384Evidently.

Santilla

385Ah, me! What causes others to be happy causes me nothing but pain. The affection that Perillo and his wife have for me hurts like a sharp arrow: I can’t do what they want, but neither can I do what would be best for myself. Oh, if God had only given me death instead of life, and a grave instead of a cradle when I came out of my mother’s womb, then this situation would never have arisen: my fortune would have ended where it began. Oh, my poor brother, you who met with such an untimely death in our fatherland (or so at least I believe), if only you were here to advise me! What is poor Santilla going to do now –since henceforth I must call myself Santilla and not Lidio? I am a woman, yet I must also be a bridegroom! If I marry Virginia, they will find out I am a woman and not a man: they will turn me out and might even have me put to death. And if I refuse to marry her, they will also turn me out, with their curses. If I reveal myself now to be a woman, I would only bring harm upon myself. I can’t stand much more of this, though. A cliff on one side, wolves on the other!

Fannio

386Don’t give up hope; something may yet turn up. I think you are right to keep out of Perillo’s sight, and this idea of Ruffo’s for you to go to Fulvia in women’s clothes is a good one. I have them all ready for you. We must do one thing at a time, as they come.

Santilla

387I wonder what is keeping Ruffo.

Fannio

388Remember what we said: whoever came first was to wait for the others.

Santilla

389We’d better not stay here, or that fellow over there might see us. He may have been sent by Perillo, though I doubt it.

Scene IX

Fessenio, Calandro

Fessenio

390Things couldn’t possibly work out better. Lidio is dressing up as a woman, and Calandro is waiting for him in a room on the first floor of his house. Lidio, looking simply irresistible, will visit him there, and then when the old fool’s back is turned, we’ll slip in a Prostitute through the back door to take his place. He’s such a fool that he can’t tell a nightingale from a jackass, and will never know the difference. Here he comes now; see how happy he looks. God bless you, master.

Calandro

391And you, too, dear Fessenio. Is the trunk ready?

Fessenio

392Yes, and it’s so big you won’t even get your hair mussed.

Calandro

393That’s fine. But tell me something.

Fessenio

394Yes?

Calandro

395Should I stay awake in the trunk, or go to sleep?

Fessenio

396What do you mean, awake or asleep? Don’t you know that when you are on a horse you ride, when you are in the streets you walk, when at table you eat, when in bed you sleep, and when you are in a trunk you die?

Calandro

397Die? What do you mean?

Fessenio

398Just that. Die, Why?

Calandro

399Damn! That’s not so good.

Fessenio

400Didn’t you ever die before?

Calandro

401Not that I know of.

Fessenio

402Then if you’ve never been dead, how do you know it’s so bad?

Calandro

403Did you ever die?

Fessenio

404Oh, yes! Thousands of times.

Calandro

405Does it hurt?

Fessenio

406No more than sleeping.

Calandro

407Do I absolutely have to die?

Fessenio

408Absolutely, when you get in the trunk.

Calandro

409But who will kill me?

Fessenio

410You will have to do it yourself.

Calandro

411And how do I go about it?

Fessenio

412There’s nothing to it. But since you don’t know, I’ll tell you.

Calandro

413If you please.

Fessenio

414You close your eyes, clench your hands together, twist your arms, and then keep very still and very quiet. Finally you neither see nor hear anything.

Calandro

415I see. But how do I come back to life?

Fessenio

416That’s one of the greatest secrets in the whole world; hardly anybody knows it. I wouldn’t tell it to anyone but you, and you must swear not to repeat it to a single soul.

Calandro

417I swear I won’t tell it to anybody, not even to myself.

Fessenio

418Oh, it’s all right to tell it to yourself –but only to one ear, not to both.

Calandro

419Promised. Now tell it to me.

Fessenio

420Well, Calandro, you know that the only difference between the dead and the living is that the living move around and the dead do not. And if you do exactly as I say, you will come back to life all right.

Calandro

421Tell me how.

Fessenio

422First you must raise your head and spit straight up in the air, then you give your whole body a shake. Next you open your eyes, say a few words, and move your arms and legs. That always chases death away, and you may be sure, Calandro, that whoever is able to do this is never dead. Now you are in possession of one of the world’s greatest secrets.

Calandro

423I appreciate your confidence. Now I die and come back to life whenever I like.

Fessenio

424That you can, master.

Calandro

425And I’ll be careful to do it exactly right.

Fessenio

426I’m sure you will.

Calandro

427Shall I try it once, just to make sure?

Fessenio

428A good idea. Are you ready?

Calandro

429Yes.

Fessenio

430Make an ugly face. Uglier still. There! A fine corpse if ever I saw one. There’s nothing like a little know-how. Who would ever have thought this man would learn to die so well? At least he looks dead on the outside: if he’s as dead on the inside as he is on the outside, it’s a perfect job. We’ll see. (Makes magical motions) Calandro! Oh, Calandro!

Calandro

431(weakly) I’m dead. I am dead.

Fessenio

432Come back to life, Calandro. Spit in the air.

Calandro

433(sighing) Ah, ah. You should have let me alone.

Fessenio

434Why?

Calandro

435I was just beginning to see the next world.

Fessenio

436You’ll have plenty of time to see it in the trunk.

Calandro

437I can hardly wait.

Fessenio

438That’s the right spirit. Now that you know how to die and come back to life so well, we mustn’t waste time.

Calandro

439That’s right. Are we ready?

Fessenio

440Not quite. We have to make sure that your wife doesn’t suspect anything. You tell her you are going to the country, and go instead to Menicuccio’s house. I’ll have everything ready and meet you there.

Calandro

441All right. I’ll do it now, since the beast is ready.

Fessenio

442(Inspects Calandro’s lower parts) It’s ready, is it? Let’s have a look.

Calandro

443Ha, ha! I mean my mule is ready.

Fessenio

444I knew what you meant; I was only joking.

Calandro

445It’s been a long time since I was in the saddle, but I’d do anything for that little angel that’s waiting for me up there.

Exit

Fessenio

446Angle, eh? Today we’re going to see stupidity joined to filth. He’s gone now to mount his mule, and I’ll go tell that pretty little Prostitute to get everything ready and wait for me. There is Calandro, already in the saddle. What a lot of strength there must be in that little mule to carry such a stupid old elephant!

Scene X

Calandro, Fulvia

Calandro

447Fulvia.

Fulvia

448What is it?

Calandro

449Come to the window.

Fulvia

450What do you want?

Calandro

451I’m going to the farm, to see Flaminio.

Fulvia

452When are you coming back?

Calandro

453Maybe tonight sometime.

Fulvia

454All right. Good-bye. (Exit Calandro) And bad luck! A fine husband my brothers married me off to! I can’t stand the sight of him.
