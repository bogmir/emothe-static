5.1

[Enter] Lovewit [and] Neighbors.

LOVEWIT

1Has there been such resort, say you?

FIRST NEIGHBOR

Has there been such resort, say you? Daily, sir.

SECOND NEIGHBOR

2And nightly, too.

THIRD NEIGHBOR

And nightly, too. Ay, some as brave as lords.

FORTH NEIGHBOR

3Ladies and gentlewomen.

FIFTH NEIGHBOR

Ladies and gentlewomen. Citizens' wives.

FIRST NEIGHBOR

4And knights.

SIXTH NEIGHBOR

And knights. In coaches.

SECOND NEIGHBOR

And knights. In coaches. Yes, and oyster-women.

FIRST NEIGHBOR

5Beside other gallants.

THIRD NEIGHBOR

Beside other gallants. Sailors' wives.

FOURTH NEIGHBOR

Beside other gallants. Sailors' wives. Tobacco men.

FIFTH NEIGHBOR

6Another Pimlico!

LOVEWIT

Another Pimlico! What should my knave advance
7To draw this company? He hung out no banners
8Of a strange calf with five legs to be seen,
9Or a huge lobster with six claws?

SIXTH NEIGHBOR

Or a huge lobster with six claws? No, sir.

THIRD NEIGHBOR

10We had gone in then, sir.

LOVEWIT

We had gone in then, sir. He has no gift
11Of teaching i'the nose that e'er I knew of.
12You saw no bills set up that promised cure
13Of agues or the toothache?

SECOND NEIGHBOR

Of agues or the toothache? No such thing, sir.

LOVEWIT

14Nor heard a drum struck for baboons or puppets?

FIFTH NEIGHBOR

15Neither, sir.

LOVEWIT

Neither, sir. What device should he bring forth now?
16I love a teeming wit as I love my nourishment.
17Pray God he ha' not kept such open house
18That he hath sold my hangings and my bedding!
19I left him nothing else. If he have eat 'em,
20A plague o'the moth, say I. Sure he has got
21Some bawdy pictures to call all this ging:
22The Friar and the Nun, or the new motion
23Of the knight's courser covering the parson's mare,
24The boy of six year old with the great thing,
25Or't may be he has the fleas that run at tilt
26Upon a table, or some dog to dance?
27When saw you him?

FIRST NEIGHBOR

When saw you him? Who, sir, Jeremy?

SECOND NEIGHBOR

When saw you him? Who, sir, Jeremy? Jeremy butler?
28We saw him not this month.

LOVEWIT

We saw him not this month. How!

FOURTH NEIGHBOR

We saw him not this month. How! Not these five weeks, sir.

FIRST NEIGHBOR

29These six weeks, at the least.

LOVEWIT

These six weeks, at the least. You amaze me, neighbors!

FIFTH NEIGHBOR

30Sure, if Your Worship know not where he is,
31He's slipped away.

SIXTH NEIGHBOR

He's slipped away. Pray God he be not made away!

LOVEWIT

32Ha? It's no time to question, then.

He knocks.

SIXTH NEIGHBOR

Ha? It's no time to question, then. About
33Some three weeks since I heard a doleful cry,
34As I sat up a-mending my wife's stockings.

LOVEWIT

35This's strange, that none will answer! Didst thou hear
36A cry, say'st thou?

SIXTH NEIGHBOR

A cry, say'st thou? Yes, sir, like unto a man
37That had been strangled an hour and could not speak.

SECOND NEIGHBOR

38I heard it too, just this day three weeks, at two o'clock
39Next morning.

LOVEWIT

Next morning. These be miracles, or you make 'em so!
40A man an hour strangled, and could not speak,
41And both you heard him cry?

THIRD NEIGHBOR

And both you heard him cry? Yes, downward, sir.

LOVEWIT

42Thou art a wise fellow. Give me thy hand, I pray thee.
43What trade art thou on?

THIRD NEIGHBOR

What trade art thou on? A smith, an't please Your Worship.

LOVEWIT

44A smith? Then lend me thy help to get this door open.

THIRD NEIGHBOR

45That I will presently, sir; but fetch my tools –

[Exit.]

FIRST NEIGHBOR

46Sir, best to knock again, afore you break it.

5.2

LOVEWIT

1[knocking again] I will.

[Enter] Face [in his butler's livery].

FACE

[knocking again] I will. What mean you, sir?

FIRST, SECOND, FOURTH NEIGHBORS

[knocking again] I will. What mean you, sir? Oh, here's Jeremy!

FACE

2Good sir, come from the door.

LOVEWIT

Good sir, come from the door. Why, what's the matter?

FACE

3Yet farther; you are too near, yet.

LOVEWIT

Yet farther; you are too near, yet. I'the name of wonder!
4What means the fellow?

FACE

What means the fellow? The house, sir, has been visited.

LOVEWIT

5What, with the plague? Stand thou then farther.

FACE

What, with the plague? Stand thou then farther. No, sir.
6I had it not.

LOVEWIT

I had it not. Who had it, then? O left
7None else but thee i'the house.

FACE

None else but thee i'the house. Yes, sir. My fellow,
8The cat, that kept the butt'ry, had it on her
9A week before I spied it; but I got her
10Conveyed away i'the night. And so I shut
11The house up for a month –

LOVEWIT

The house up for a month – How!

FACE

The house up for a month – How! Purposing then, sir,
12T'have burnt rose-vinegar, treacle, and tar,
13And ha' made it sweet, that you should ne'er ha' known it,
14Because I knew the news would but afflict you, sir.

LOVEWIT

15Breathe less, and farther off. Why, this is stranger!
16The neighbors tell me all here that the doors
17Have still been open –

FACE

Have still been open – How, sir!

LOVEWIT

Have still been open – How, sir! Gallants, men and women,
18And of all sorts, tag-rag, been seen to flock here
19In threaves, these ten weeks, as to a second Hoxton
20In days of Pimlico and Eye-bright.

FACE

In days of Pimlico and Eye-bright. Sir,
21Their wisdoms will not say so.

LOVEWIT

Their wisdoms will not say so. Today they speak
22Of coaches and gallants; one in a French hood
23Went in, they tell me, and another was seen
24In a velvet gown, at the window; divers more
25Pass in and out.

FACE

Pass in and out. They did pass through the doors, then,
26Or walls, I assure their eyesights and their spectacles;
27For here, sir, are the keys, and here have been
28In this my pocket now above twenty days.
29And for before, I kept the fort alone there.
30But that 'tis yet not deep i'the afternoon.
31I should believe my neighbors had seen double
32Through the black pot, and made these apparitions!
33For, on my faith to Your Worship, for these three weeks
34And upwards the door has not been opened.

LOVEWIT

And upwards the door has not been opened. Strange!

FIRST NEIGHBOR

35Good faith, I think I saw a coach.

SECOND NEIGHBOR

Good faith, I think I saw a coach. And I too,
36I'd ha' been sworn.

LOVEWIT

I'd ha' been sworn. Do you but think it now?
37And but one coach?

FOURTH NEIGHBOR

And but one coach? We cannot tell, sir. Jeremy
38Is a very honest fellow.

FACE

Is a very honest fellow. Did you see me at all?

FIRST NEIGHBOR

39No. That we are sure on.

SECOND NEIGHBOR

No. That we are sure on. I'll be sworn o'that.

LOVEWIT

40Fine rogues, to have your testimonies built on!

[Enter Third Neighbor with his tools.]

THIRD NEIGHBOR

41Is Jeremy come?

FIRST NEIGHBOR

Is Jeremy come? Oh, yes. You may leave your tools;
42We were deceived, he says.

SECOND NEIGHBOR

We were deceived, he says. He's had the keys,
43And the door has been shut these three weeks.

THIRD NEIGHBOR

And the door has been shut these three weeks. Like enough!

LOVEWIT

44Peace, and get hence, you changelings.

FACE

Peace, and get hence, you changelings. Surly come?aparte
45And Mammon made acquainted? They'll tell all.aparte
46How shall I beat them off? What shall I do?aparte
47Nothing's more wretched than a guilty conscience.aparte

5.3

[Enter] Surly [and Sir Epicure] Mammon.

SURLY

1[to Mammon] No, sir, he was a great physician. This,
2It was no bawdy house, but a mere chancel!
3You knew the lord and his sister.

MAMMON

You knew the lord and his sister. Nay, good Surly –

SURLY

4The happy word, “Be rich” –

MAMMON

The happy word, “Be rich” – Play not the tyrant –

SURLY

5Should be today pronounced to all your friends.
6And where be your andirons now, and your brass pots,
7That should ha' been golden flagons and great wedges?

MAMMON

8Let me but breathe. What! They ha' shut their doors,
9Methinks!

Mammon and Surly knock.

SURLY

Methinks! Ay, now 'tis holiday with them.

MAMMON

Methinks! Ay, now 'tis holiday with them. Rogues,
10Cozeners, impostors, bawds!

FACE

Cozeners, impostors, bawds! What mean you, sir?

MAMMON

11To enter if we can.

FACE

To enter if we can. Another man's house?
12Here is the owner, sir. Turn you to him
13And speak your business.

MAMMON

And speak your business. [to Lovewit] Are you, sir, the owner?

LOVEWIT

14Yes, sir.

MAMMON

Yes, sir. And are those knaves within your cheaters?

LOVEWIT

15What knaves? What cheaters?

MAMMON

What knaves? What cheaters? Subtle and his Lungs.

FACE

16The gentleman is distracted, sir. No lungs
17Nor lights ha' been seen here these three weeks, sir,
18Within these doors, upon my word.

SURLY

Within these doors, upon my word. Your word,
19Groom arrogant?

FACE

Groom arrogant? Yes, sir. I am the housekeeper,
20And know the keys ha' not been out o'my hands.

SURLY

21This's a new Face?

FACE

This's a new Face? You do mistake the house, sir.
22What sign was't at?

SURLY

What sign was't at? You rascal! –This is one
23O'the confederacy. Come, let's get officers,
24And force the door.

LOVEWIT

And force the door. Pray you stay, gentlemen.

SURLY

25No, sir, we'll come with warrant.

MAMMON

No, sir, we'll come with warrant. Ay, and then
26We shall ha' your doors open.

[Exeunt Mammon and Surly.]

LOVEWIT

We shall ha' your doors open. [to Face] What means this?

FACE

27I cannot tell, sir.

FIRST NEIGHBOR

I cannot tell, sir. These are two o'the gallants
28That we do think we saw.

FACE

That we do think we saw. Two o'the fools?
29You talk as idly as they. –Good faith, sir,
30I think the moon has crazed 'em all.

[Enter] Kastril.

I think the moon has crazed 'em all. Oh, me,aparte
31The angry boy come too? He'll make a noise,aparte
32And ne'er away till he have betrayed us all.aparte

Kastril knocks.

KASTRIL

33What, rogues, bawds, slaves! You'll open the door anon,
34Punk, cockatrice, my suster! By this light,
35I'll fetch the marshal to you. You are a whore
36To keep your castle –

FACE

To keep your castle – Who would you speak with, sir?

KASTRIL

37The bawdy doctor and the cozening captain,
38And Puss, my suster.

LOVEWIT

And Puss, my suster. This is something, sure!

FACE

39Upon my trust, the doors were never open, sir.

KASTRIL

40I have heard all their tricks told me twice over
41By the fat knight and the lean gentleman.

LOVEWIT

42Here comes another.

[Enter] Ananias and Tribulation.

FACE

Here comes another. Ananias too?aparte
43And his pastor?aparte

TRIBULATION

And his pastor? The doors are shut against us.

They beat too at the door.

ANANIAS

44Come forth, you seed of sulfur, sons of fire!
45You stench, it is broke forth; abomination
46Is in the house.

KASTRIL

Is in the house. Ay, my suster's there.

ANANIAS

Is in the house. Ay, my suster's there. The place,
47It is become a cage of unclean birds.

KASTRIL

48Yes, I will fetch the scavenger and the constable.

TRIBULATION

49You shall do well.

ANANIAS

You shall do well. We'll join to weed them out.

KASTRIL

50You will not come, then, punk device, my suster?

ANANIAS

51Call her not sister. She is a harlot, verily.

KASTRIL

52I'll raise the street.

LOVEWIT

I'll raise the street. Good gentlemen, a word.

ANANIAS

53Satan, avoid, and hinder not our zeal!

[Exeunt Ananias, Tribulation, and Kastril.]

LOVEWIT

54The world's turned Bedlam.

FACE

The world's turned Bedlam. These are all broke loose
55Out of Saint Katherine's, where they use to keep
56The better sort of madfolks.

FIRST NEIGHBOR

The better sort of madfolks. [to Lovewit] All these persons
57We saw go in and out here.

SECOND NEIGHBOR

We saw go in and out here. Yes, indeed, sir.

THIRD NEIGHBOR

58These were the parties.

FACE

These were the parties. Peace, you drunkards! –Sir,
59I wonder at it. Please you to give me leave
60To touch the door, I'll try an the lock be changed.

LOVEWIT

61It mazes me!

FACE

It mazes me! [examining the door] Good faith, sir, I believe
62There's no such thing. 'Tis all deceptio visus .
63Would I could get him away!aparte

DAPPER

Would I could get him away! (cries out within) Master Captain! Master Doctor!

LOVEWIT

64Who's that?

FACE

Who's that? Our clerk within, that I forgot! –aparte

DAPPER

65[within] For God's sake, when will Her Grace be at leisure?

FACE

[within] For God's sake, when will Her Grace be at leisure? Ha!
66His gag is melted,aparte
67And now he sets out the throat.aparte

DAPPER

And now he sets out the throat. [within] I am almost stifled –

FACE

68Would you were altogether!aparte

LOVEWIT

Would you were altogether! 'Tis i'the house.
69Ha! List!

FACE

Ha! List! Believe it, sir, i'the air.

LOVEWIT

Ha! List! Believe it, sir, i'the air. Peace, you –

DAPPER

70[within] Mine aunt's Grace does not use me well.

SUBTLE

[within] Mine aunt's Grace does not use me well. [within[ You fool,
71Peace, you'll mar all!

FACE

Peace, you'll mar all! [speaking through the keyhole] Or you will else, you rogue!

LOVEWIT

72[hearing Face] Oh, is it so? Then you converse with spirits!
73Come, sir. No more o'your tricks, good Jeremy.
74The truth, the shortest way.

FACE

The truth, the shortest way. Dismiss this rabble, sir.
75What shall I do? I am catched.aparte

LOVEWIT

What shall I do? I am catched. Good neighbors,
76I thank you all. You may depart.

[Exeunt Neighbors.]

I thank you all. You may depart. Come sir,
77You know that I am an indulgent master,
78And therefore conceal nothing. What's your med'cine
79To draw so many several sorts of wildfowl?

FACE

80Sir, you were wont to affect mirth and wit –
81But here's no place to talk on't i'the street.
82Give me but leave to make the best of my fortune,
83And only pardon me th'abuse of your house;
84It's all I beg. I'll help you to a widow,
85In recompence, that you shall gi' me thanks for,
86Will make you seven years younger, and a rich one.
87'Tis but your putting on a Spanish cloak.
88I have her within. You need not fear the house;
89It was not visited.

LOVEWIT

It was not visited. But by me, who came
90Sooner than you expected.

FACE

Sooner than you expected. It is true, sir.
91Pray you forgive me.

LOVEWIT

Pray you forgive me. Well, let's see your widow.

[Exeunt.]

5.4

[Enter] Subtle [and] Dapper.

SUBTLE

1How! Ha' you eaten your gag?

DAPPER

How! Ha' you eaten your gag? Yes, faith, it crumbled
2Away i'my mouth.

SUBTLE

Away i'my mouth. You ha' spoiled all, then.

DAPPER

Away i'my mouth. You ha' spoiled all, then. No!
3I hope my aunt of Faery will forgive me.

SUBTLE

4Your aunt's a gracious lady; but in troth
5You were to blame.

DAPPER

You were to blame. The fume did overcome me,
6And I did do't to stay my stomach. Pray you
7So satisfy Her Grace.

[Enter] Face [in his captain's uniform].

So satisfy Her Grace. Here comes the captain.

[Face and Subtle confer privately.]

FACE

8How now, is his mouth down?

SUBTLE

How now, is his mouth down? Ay, he has spoken.

FACE

9A pox! I heard him, and you too. He's undone, then.
10I have been fain to say the house is haunted
11With spirits, to keep churl back.

SUBTLE

With spirits, to keep churl back. And hast thou done it?

FACE

12Sure, for this night.

SUBTLE

Sure, for this night. Why, then triumph and sing
13Of Face so famous, the precious king
14Of present wits.

FACE

Of present wits. Did you not hear the coil
15About the door?

SUBTLE

About the door? Yes, and I dwindled with it.

FACE

16Show him his aunt, and let him be dispatched;
17I'll send her to you.

[Exit.]

SUBTLE

I'll send her to you. Well, sir, your aunt Her Grace
18Will give you audience presently, on my suit
19And the captain's word that you did not eat your gag
20In any contempt of Her Highness.

DAPPER

In any contempt of Her Highness. Not I, in troth, sir.

[Enter] Doll like the Queen of Faery.

SUBTLE

21Here she is come. Down o'your knees and wriggle!
22She has a stately presence.
23[Dapper kneels.] Good. Yet nearer,
24And bid, “God save you!”

DAPPER

And bid, “God save you!” Madam –

SUBTLE

And bid, “God save you!” Madam – And your aunt.

DAPPER

25And my most gracious aunt, God save Your Grace.

DOLL

26Nephew, we thought to have been angry with you,
27But that sweet face of yours hath turned the tide,
28And made it flow with joy that ebbed of love.
29Arise, and touch our velvet gown.

SUBTLE

Arise, and touch our velvet gown. The skirts,
30And kiss 'em. So.

[Dapper kisses the hem of her skirt.]

DOLL

And kiss 'em. So. Let me now stroke that head.
31Much, nephew, shalt thou win; much shalt thou spend;
32Much shalt thou give away; much shalt thou lend.

SUBTLE

33Ay, much, indeed. –aparte
34I cannot speak for joy.

SUBTLE

I cannot speak for joy. See, the kind wretch!
35Your Grace's kinsman right.

DOLL

Your Grace's kinsman right. Give me the bird. –
36Here is your fly, in a purse about your neck, cousin.

[She puts a purse on a chain around his neck.]

37Wear it, and feed it about this day sev'night,
38On your right wrist –

SUBTLE

On your right wrist – Open a vein with a pin
39And let it suck but once a week. Till then
40You must not look on't.

Doll

You must not look on't. No. And, kinsman,
41Bear yourself worthy of the blood you come on.

SUBTLE

42Her Grace would ha' you eat no more Woolsack pies,
43Nor Dagger frumenty.

DOLL

Nor Dagger frumenty. Nor break his fast
44In Heaven and Hell.

SUBTLE

In Heaven and Hell. She's with you everywhere!
45Nor play with costermongers at mumchance, tray-trip,
46God-make-you-rich (whenas your aunt has done it); but keep
47The gallant'st company and the best games –

DAPPER

The gallant'st company and the best games – Yes, sir.

SUBTLE

48Gleek and primero; and what you get, be true to us.

DAPPER

49By this hand, I will.

SUBTLE

By this hand, I will. You may bring 's a thousand pound
50Before tomorrow night (if but three thousand
51Be stirring), an you will.

DAPPER

Be stirring), an you will. I swear I will, then.

SUBTLE

52Your fly will learn you all games.

FACE

Your fly will learn you all games. [within] Ha' you done there?

SUBTLE

53[to Doll] Your Grace will command him no more duties?

DOLL

[to Doll] Your Grace will command him no more duties? No;
54But come and see me often. I may chance
55To leave him three or four hundred chests of treasure,
56And some twelve thousand acres of Faery land,
57If he game well and comely with good gamesters.

SUBTLE

58There's a kind aunt! Kiss her departing part.
59But you must sell your forty mark a year now.

DAPPER

60Ay, sir, I mean.

SUBTLE

Ay, sir, I mean. Or gi 't away. Pox on't!

DAPPER

61I'll gi 't mine aunt. I'll go and fetch the writings.

SUBTLE

62'Tis well. Away!

[Exit Dapper.]

[Enter] Face [with a cloak and hat].

FACE

'Tis well. Away! Where's Subtle?

SUBTLE

'Tis well. Away! Where's Subtle? Here. What news?

FACE

63Drugger is at the door. Go take his suit
64And bid him fetch a parson presently;
65Say he shall marry the widow. Thou shalt spend
66A hundred pound by the service!

[Exit Subtle.]

A hundred pound by the service! Now, queen Doll,
67Ha' you packed up all?

DOLL

Ha' you packed up all? Yes.

FACE

Ha' you packed up all? Yes. And how do you like
68The lady Pliant?

DOLL

The lady Pliant? A good, dull innocent.

[Enter Subtle, with garments.]

SUBTLE

69Here's your Hieronimo's cloak and hat.

FACE

Here's your Hieronimo's cloak and hat. [taking the garments] Give me 'em.

SUBTLE

70And the ruff too?

FACE

And the ruff too? [taking the ruff] Yes. I'll come to you presently.

[Exit.]

SUBTLE

71Now he is gone about his project, Doll,
72I told you of, for the widow.

DOLL

I told you of, for the widow. 'Tis direct
73Against our articles.

SUBTLE

Against our articles. Well, we'll fit him, wench.
74Hast thou gulled her of her jewels or her bracelets?

DOLL

75No, but I will do't.

SUBTLE

No, but I will do't. Soon at night, my Dolly,
76When we are shipped and all our goods aboard,
77Eastward for Ratcliff, we will turn our course
78To Brentford, westward, if thou say'st the word,
79And take our leaves of his o'erweening rascal,
80This peremptory Face.

DOLL

This peremptory Face. Content. I'm weary of him.

SUBTLE

81Thou'st cause, when the slave will run a-wiving, Doll,
82Against the instrument that was drawn between us.

DOLL

83I'll pluck his bird as bare as I can.

SUBTLE

I'll pluck his bird as bare as I can. Yes, tell her
84She must by any means address some present
85To th'cunning man, make him amends for wronging
86His art with her suspicion, send a ring
87Or chain of pearl; she will be tortured else
88Extremely in her sleep, say, and ha' strange things
89Come to her. Wilt thou?

DOLL

Come to her. Wilt thou? Yes.

SUBTLE

Come to her. Wilt thou? Yes. My fine flittermouse,
90My bird o'the night! We'll tickle it at the Pigeons,
91When we have all, and may unlock the trunks,
92And say, this's mine, and thine, and thine, and mine –

They kiss.

[Enter] Face.

FACE

93What now, a-billing?

SUBTLE

What now, a-billing? Yes, a little exalted
94In the good passage of our stock affairs.

FACE

95Drugger has brought his parson. Take him in, Subtle,
96And send Nab back again to wash his face.

SUBTLE

97I will –and shave himself?

FACE

I will –and shave himself? If you can get him.

[Exit Subtle.]

DOLL

98You are hot upon it, Face, whate'er it is!

FACE

99A trick that Doll shall spend ten pound a month by.

[Enter] Subtle.

100Is he gone?

SUBTLE

Is he gone? The chaplain waits you i'the hall, sir.

FACE

101I'll go bestow him.

[Exit.]

DOLL

I'll go bestow him. He'll now marry her instantly.

SUBTLE

102He cannot yet, he is not ready. Dear Doll,
103Cozen her of all thou canst. To deceive him
104Is no deceit, but justice, that would break
105Such an inextricable tie as ours was.

DOLL

106Let me alone to fit him.

[Enter] Face.

FACE

Let me alone to fit him. Come, my venturers,
107You ha' packed up all? Where be the trunks? Bring forth.

SUBTLE

You ha' packed up all? Where be the trunks? Bring forth. [producing the booty] Here.

FACE

108Let's see 'em. Where's the money?

SUBTLE

Let's see 'em. Where's the money? Here,
109In this.

FACE

In this. [counting the loot] Mammon's ten pound; eight score before;
110The Brethren's money, this; Drugger's and Dapper's.
111What paper's that?

DOLL

What paper's that? The jewel of the waiting-maid's,
112That stole it from her lady, to know certain –

FACE

113If she should have precedence of her mistress?

DOLL

If she should have precedence of her mistress? Yes.

FACE

114What box is that?

SUBTLE

What box is that? The fishwife's rings, I think,
115And th'alewife's single money. Is't not, Doll?

DOLL

116Yes, and the whistle that the sailor's wife
117Brought you to know an her husband were with Ward.

FACE

118We'll wet it tomorrow, and our silver beakers
119And tavern cups. Where be the French petticoats
120And girdles and hangers?

SUBTLE

And girdles and hangers? Here, i'the trunk,
121And the bolts of lawn.

FACE

And the bolts of lawn. Is Drugger's damask there?
122And the tobacco?

SUBTLE

And the tobacco? Yes.

FACE

And the tobacco? Yes. Give me the keys.

DOLL

123Why you the keys?

SUBTLE

Why you the keys? No matter, Doll, because
124We shall not open 'em before he comes.

FACE

125'Tis true, you shall not open them, indeed,
126Nor have 'em forth. Do you see? Not forth, Doll.

DOLL

Nor have 'em forth. Do you see? Not forth, Doll. No?

FACE

127No, my smock-rampant. The right is, my master
128Knows all, has pardoned me, and he will keep 'em.
129Doctor, 'tis true –you look –for all your figures;
130I sent for him, indeed. Wherefore, good partners,
131Both he and she, be satisfied, for here
132Determines the indenture tripartite
133'Twixt Subtle, Doll, and Face. All I can do
134Is to help you over the wall, o'the back-side,
135Or lend you a sheet to save your velvet gown, Doll.
136Here will be officers presently; bethink you
137Of some course suddenly to scape the dock,
138For thither you'll come else.
139(Some knock.) Hark you, thunder!

SUBTLE

140You are a precious fiend!

OFFICER

You are a precious fiend! [without] Open the door!

FACE

141Doll, I am sorry for thee, i'faith. But hear'st thou?
142It shall go hard but I will place thee somewhere;
143Thou shalt ha' my letter to Mistress Amo.

DOLL

Thou shalt ha' my letter to Mistress Amo. Hang you –

FACE

144Or Madam Caesarean.

DOLL

Or Madam Caesarean. Pox upon you, rogue!
145Would I had but time to beat thee!

FACE

Would I had but time to beat thee! Subtle,
146Let's know where you set up next; I'll send you
147A customer now and then, for old acquaintance.
148What new course ha' you?

SUBTLE

What new course ha' you? Rogue, I'll hang myself,
149That I may walk a greater devil than thou
150And haunt thee i'the flock-bed and the buttery.

[Exeunt.]

5.5

[Enter] Lovewit [in Spanish costume, with the parson. Knocking is heard at the door].

LOVEWIT

1[responding to the knocking] What do you mean, my masters?

MAMMON

[responding to the knocking] What do you mean, my masters? [without] Open your door,
2Cheaters, bawds, conjurers!

OFFICER

Cheaters, bawds, conjurers! [without] Or we'll break it open.

LOVEWIT

3What warrant have you?

OFFICER

What warrant have you? [without] Warrant enough, sir, doubt not,
4If you'll not open it.

LOVEWIT

If you'll not open it. Is there an officer there?

OFFICER

5[without] Yes, two, or three for failing.

LOVEWIT

[without] Yes, two, or three for failing. Have but patience,
6And I will open it straight.

[Enter] Face [as Jeremy the butler].

FACE

And I will open it straight. Sir, ha' you done?
7Is it a marriage? Perfect?

LOVEWIT

Is it a marriage? Perfect? Yes, my brain.

FACE

8Off with your ruff and cloak, then; be yourself, sir.

[Lovewit removes the Spanish costume.]

SURLY

9[without] Down with the door!

KASTRIL

[without] Down with the door! [without] 'Slight, ding it open!

LOVEWIT

[without] Down with the door! [without] 'Slight, ding it open! [opening the door] Hold,
10Hold, gentlemen. What means this violence?

[Enter] Mammon, Surly, Kastril, Ananias, Tribulation, [and] Officers.

MAMMON

11Where is this collier?

SURLY

Where is this collier? And my Captain Face?

MAMMON

12These day-owls.

SURLY

These day-owls. That are birding in men's purses.

MAMMON

13Madam Suppository.

KASTRIL

Madam Suppository. Doxy, my suster.

ANANIAS

Madam Suppository. Doxy, my suster. Locusts
14Of the foul pit.

TRIBULATION

Of the foul pit. Profane as Bel and the dragon.

ANANIAS

15Worse than the grasshoppers, or the lice of Egypt.

LOVEWIT

16Good gentlemen, hear me. –Are you officers,
17And cannot stay this violence?

OFFICER

And cannot stay this violence? Keep the peace!

LOVEWIT

18Gentlemen, what is the matter? Whom do you seek?

MAMMON

19The chemical cozener.

SURLY

The chemical cozener. And the captain pander.

KASTRIL

20The nun my suster.

MAMMON

The nun my suster. Madam Rabbi.

ANANIAS

The nun my suster. Madam Rabbi. Scorpions
21And caterpillars.

LOVEWIT

And caterpillars. Fewer at once, I pray you.

OFFICER

22One after another, gentlemen, I charge you,
23By virtue of my staff –

ANANIAS

By virtue of my staff – They are the vessels
24Of pride, lust, and the cart.

LOVEWIT

Of pride, lust, and the cart. Good zeal, lie still
25A little while.

TRIBULATION

A little while. Peace, Deacon Ananias.

LOVEWIT

26The house is mine here, and the doors are open.
27If there be any such persons as you seek for,
28Use your authority; search on, o'God's name.
29I am buy newly come to town, and, finding
30This tumult 'bout my door, to tell you true,
31It somewhat mazed me; till my man, here, fearing
32My more displeasure, told me he had done
33Somewhat an insolent part, let out my house
34(Belike presuming on my known aversion
35From any air o'the town while there was sickness)
36To a doctor and a captain –who, what they are
37Or where they be, he knows not.

MAMMON

Or where they be, he knows not. Are they gone?

LOVEWIT

38You may go in and search, sir.

They enter. [Mammon, Tribulation, Ananias, and the Officers disappear through stage doors as they begin their search of the house.]

You may go in and search, sir. Here I find
39The empty walls worse than I left 'em, smoked,
40A few cracked pots, and glasses, and a furnace,
41The ceiling filled with poesies of the candle,
42And “Madam with a dildo” writ o'the walls.
43Only one gentlewoman I met here,
44That is within, that said she was a widow –

KASTRIL

45Ay, that's my suster. I'll go thump her. Where is she?

[He goes in.]

LOVEWIT

46And should ha' married a Spanish count, but he,
47When he came to't, neglected her so grossly
48That I, a widower, am gone through with her.

SURLY

49How! Have I lost her, then?

LOVEWIT

How! Have I lost her, then? Were you the don, sir?
50Good faith, now, she does blame you extremely, and says
51You swore and told her you had ta'en the pains
52To dye your beard and umber o'er your face,
53Borrowed a suit and ruff, all for her love –
54And then did nothing. What an oversight
55And want of putting forward, sir, was this!
56Well fare an old harquebusier, yet,
57Could prime his powder, and give fire, and hit,
58All in a twinkling!

Mammon comes forth.

MAMMON

All in a twinkling! The whole nest are fled!

LOVEWIT

59What sort of birds were they?

MAMMON

What sort of birds were they? A kind of choughs,
60Or thievish daws, sir, that have picked my purse
61Of eightscore and ten pounds within these five weeks,
62Beside my first materials, and my goods,
63That lie i'the cellar, which I am glad they ha' left.
64I may have home yet.

LOVEWIT

I may have home yet. Think you so, sir?

MAMMON

I may have home yet. Think you so, sir? Ay.

LOVEWIT

65By order of law, sir, but not otherwise.

MAMMON

66Not mine own stuff?

LOVEWIT

Not mine own stuff? Sir, I can take no knowledge
67That they are yours but by public means.
68If you can bring certificate that you were gulled of 'em,
69Or any formal writ out of a court
70Than you did cozen yourself, I will not hold them.

MAMMON

71I'll rather lose 'em.

LOVEWIT

I'll rather lose 'em. That you shall not, sir,
72By me, in troth; upon these terms they are yours.
73What, should they ha' been, sir, turned into gold all?

MAMMON

What, should they ha' been, sir, turned into gold all? No.
74I cannot tell. It may be they should. What then?

LOVEWIT

75What a great loos in hope have you sustained!

MAMMON

76Not I; the commonwealth has.

FACE

Not I; the commonwealth has. Ay, he would ha' built
77The city new, and made a ditch about it
78Of silver, should have run with cream from Hoxton,
79That every Sunday in Moorfields the younkers
80And tits and tomboys should have fed on, gratis.

MAMMON

81I will go mount a turnip-cart and preach
82The end o'the world within these two months. –Surly,
83What! In a dream?

SURLY

What! In a dream? Must I needs cheat myself
84With that same foolish vice of honesty?
85Come, let us go and hearken out the rogues.
86That Face I'll mark for mine if e'er I meet him.

FACE

87If I can hear of him, sir, I'll bring you word
88Unto your lodging; for in troth they were strangers
89To me. I thought 'em honest as myself, sir.

[Exeunt Surly and Mammon.]

They [Tribulation and Ananias] come forth.

TRIBULATION

90'Tis well; the Saints shall not lose all yet. Go
91And get some carts –

LOVEWIT

And get some carts – For what, my zealous friends?

ANANIAS

92To bear away the portion of the righteous
93Out of this den of thieves.

LOVEWIT

Out of this den of thieves. What is that portion?

ANANIAS

94The goods, sometimes the orphans', that the Brethren
95Bought with their silver pence.

LOVEWIT

Bought with their silver pence. What, those i'the cellar
96The knight Sir Mammon claims?

ANANIAS

The knight Sir Mammon claims? I do defy
97The wicked Mammon; so do all the Brethren,
98Thou profane man! I ask thee with what conscience
99Thou canst advance that idol against us
100That have the seal? Were not the shillings numbered
101That made the pounds? Were not the pounds told out
102Upon the second day of the fourth week,
103In the eight month, upon the table dormant,
104The year of the last patience of the Saints,
105Six hundred and ten?

LOVEWIT

Six hundred and ten? Mine earnest, vehement botcher,
106And deacon also, I cannot dispute with you;
107But, if you get you not away the sooner,
108I shall confute you with a cudgel.

ANANIAS

I shall confute you with a cudgel. Sir!

TRIBULATION

109Be patient, Ananias.

ANANIAS

Be patient, Ananias. I am strong,
110And will stand up, well girt, against an host
111That threaten Gad in exile.

LOVEWIT

That threaten Gad in exile. I shall send you
112To Amsterdam, to your cellar.

ANANIAS

To Amsterdam, to your cellar. I will pray there
113Against thy house. May dogs defile thy walls
114And wasps and hornets breed beneath thy roof,
115This seat of falsehood and this cave of coz'nage!

[Exeunt Tribulation and Ananias.]

Drugger enters, and he beats him away.

LOVEWIT

116Another too?

DRUGGER

Another too? Not I, sir; I am no Brother.

LOVEWIT

117Away, you Harry Nicholas! Do you talk?

[Exit Drugger.]

FACE

118No, this was Abel Drugger.
119(To the parson) Good sir, go
120And satisfy him; tell him all is done;
121He stayed too long a-washing of his face.
122The doctor, he shall hear of him at Westchester,
123And of the captain, tell him, at Yarmouth, or
124Some good port town else, lying for a wind.

[Exit parson.]

125[To Lovewit] If you can get off the angry child, now, sir –

[Enter Kastril and Dame Pliant.]

KASTRIL

126(to his sister) Come on, you ewe! You have matched most sweetly, ha' you not?
127Did not I say I would never ha' you tupped
128But by a dubbed boy, to make you a lady-tom?
129'Slight, you are a mammet! Oh, I could touse you now.
130Death, mun you marry, with a pox?

LOVEWIT

Death, mun you marry, with a pox? You lie, boy.
131As sound as you; and I am aforehand with you.

KASTRIL

As sound as you; and I am aforehand with you. Anon?

LOVEWIT

132Come, will you quarrel? I will feeze you, sirrah.
133Why do you not buckle to your tools?

KASTRIL

Why do you not buckle to your tools? God's light!
134This is a fine old boy as e'er I saw!

LOVEWIT

135What, do you change your copy now? Proceed;
136Here stands my dove. Stoop at her, if you dare.

KASTRIL

137'Slight, I must love him! I cannot choose, i'faith,
138An I should be hanged for't! Suster, I protest
139I honor thee for this match.

LOVEWIT

I honor thee for this match. Oh, do you so, sir?

KASTRIL

140Yes. An thou canst take tobacco and drink, old boy,
141I'll give her five hundred pound more to her marriage
142Than her own state.

LOVEWIT

Than her own state. Fill a pipeful, Jeremy.

FACE

143Yes, but go in and take it, sir.

LOVEWIT

Yes, but go in and take it, sir. We will.
144I will be ruled by thee in anything, Jeremy.

KASTRIL

145'Slight, thou art not hidebound! Thou art a jovy boy!
146Come, let's in, I pray thee, and take our whiffs.

LOVEWIT

147Whiff in with your sister, brother boy.

[Exeunt Kastril and Dame Pliant.]

Whiff in with your sister, brother boy. That master
148That had received such happiness by a servant,
149In such a widow, and with so much wealth,
150Were very ungrateful if he would not be
151A little indulgent to that servant's wit,
152And help his fortune, though with some small strain
153Of his own candor.
154[To the audience] Therefore, gentlemen
155And kind spectators, if I have outstripped
156An old man's gravity or strict canon, think
157What a young wife and a good brain may do:
158Stretch age's truth sometimes, and crack it too. –
159Speak for thyself, knave.

FACE

Speak for thyself, knave. So I will, sir. –Gentlemen,
160My part a little fell in this last scene,
161Yet 'twas decorum. And though I am clean
162Got off from Subtle, Surly, Mammon, Doll,
163Hot Ananias, Dapper, Drugger, all
164With whom I traded, yet I put myself
165On you, that are my country; and this pelf
166Which I have got, if you do quit me, rests
167To feast you often, and invite new guests.

[Exeunt.]
