ACT 5

SCENE 1

(Enter Volpone.)

VOLPONE

1Well, I am here, and all this brunt is past.
2I ne'er was in dislike with my disguise
3Till this fled moment: here 'twas good, in private;
4But in your public, – cavè whilst I breathe.
5'Fore God, my left leg 'gan to have the cramp,
6And I apprehended straight some power had struck me
7With a dead palsy: well, I must be merry,
8And shake it off. A many of these fears
9Would put me into some villainous disease,
10Should they come thick upon me: I'll prevent 'em.
11Give me a bowl of lusty wine, to fright
12This humour from my heart. (He drinks.) – Hum, hum, hum!
13'Tis almost gone already; I shall conquer.
14Any device, now, of rare ingenious knavery,
15That would possess me with a violent laughter,
16Would make me up again. (Drinks again.) So, so, so, so.
17This heat is life; 'tis blood by this time:– Mosca!

ACT 5, SCENE 2

[Enter MOSCA.]

MOSCA

1How now, sir? Does the day look clear again?
2Are we recovered, and wrought out of error,
3Into our way, to see our path before us?
4Is our trade free once more?

VOLPONE

Is our trade free once more? Exquisite, Mosca!

MOSCA

5Was it not carried learnedly?

VOLPONE

Was it not carried learnedly? And stoutly:
6Good wits are greatest in extremities.

MOSCA

7It were a folly beyond thought, to trust
8Any grand act unto a cowardly spirit:
9You are not taken with it enough, methinks.

VOLPONE

10O, more than if I had enjoyed the wench:
11The pleasure of all woman-kind's not like it.

MOSCA

12Why, now you speak, sir. We must here be fixed;
13Here we must rest; this is our master-piece;
14We cannot think to go beyond this.

VOLPONE

We cannot think to go beyond this. True,
15Thou hast played thy prize, my precious Mosca.

MOSCA

Thou hast played thy prize, my precious Mosca. Nay, sir,
16To gull the court –

VOLPONE

To gull the court – And quite divert the torrent
17Upon the innocent.

MOSCA

Upon the innocent. Yes, and to make
18So rare a music out of discords –

VOLPONE

So rare a music out of discords – Right.
19That yet to me's the strangest, how thou hast borne it!
20That these, being so divided 'mongst themselves,
21Should not scent somewhat, or in me or thee,
22Or doubt their own side.

MOSCA

Or doubt their own side. True, they will not see't.
23Too much light blinds them, I think. Each of them
24Is so possessed and stuffed with his own hopes,
25That any thing unto the contrary,
26Never so true, or never so apparent,
27Never so palpable, they will resist it –

VOLPONE

28Like a temptation of the devil.

MOSCA

Like a temptation of the devil. Right, sir.
29Merchants may talk of trade, and your great signiors
30Of land that yields well; but if Italy
31Have any glebe more fruitful than these fellows,
32I am deceived. Did not your advocate rare?

VOLPONE

33O – "My most honoured fathers, my grave fathers,
34Under correction of your fatherhoods,
35What face of truth is here? If these strange deeds
36May pass, most honored fathers" – I had much ado
37To forbear laughing.

MOSCA

To forbear laughing. It seemed to me, you sweat, sir.

VOLPONE

38In troth, I did a little.

MOSCA

In troth, I did a little. But confess, sir,
39Were you not daunted?

VOLPONE

Were you not daunted? In good faith, I was
40A little in a mist, but not dejected;
41Never, but still my self.

MOSCA

Never, but still my self. I think it, sir.
42Now, so truth help me, I must needs say this, sir,
43And out of conscience for your advocate:
44He’s taken pains, in faith, sir, and deserved,
45In my poor judgment, I speak it under favour,
46Not to contrary you, sir, very richly –
47Well – to be cozened.

VOLPONE

Well – to be cozened. Troth, and I think so too,
48By that I heard him, in the latter end.

MOSCA

49O, but before, sir: had you heard him first
50Draw it to certain heads, then aggravate,
51Then use his vehement figures – I looked still
52When he would shift a shirt: and, doing this
53Out of pure love, no hope of gain –

VOLPONE

Out of pure love, no hope of gain – 'Tis right.
54I cannot answer him, Mosca, as I would,
55Not yet; but for thy sake, at thy entreaty,
56I will begin, even now– to vex them all,
57This very instant.

MOSCA

This very instant. Good sir.

VOLPONE

This very instant. Good sir. Call the dwarf
58And eunuch forth.

MOSCA

And eunuch forth. Castrone, Nano!

[Enter Castrone and Nano.]

NANO

And eunuch forth. Castrone, Nano! Here.

VOLPONE

59Shall we have a jig now?

MOSCA

Shall we have a jig now? What you please, sir.

VOLPONE

60Go, straight give out about the streets, you two,
61That I am dead; do it with constancy,
62Sadly, do you hear? Impute it to the grief
63Of this late slander.

[Exeunt Castrone and Nano.]

MOSCA

Of this late slander. What do you mean, sir?

VOLPONE

Of this late slander. What do you mean, sir? Oh,
64I shall have instantly my Vulture, Crow,
65Raven, come flying hither, on the news,
66To peck for carrion, my she-wolf, and all,
67Greedy, and full of expectation –

MOSCA

68And then to have it ravished from their mouths?

VOLPONE

69'Tis true. I will have thee put on a gown,
70And take upon thee, as thou wert mine heir:
71Show them a will: open that chest, and reach
72Forth one of those that has the blanks; I'll straight
73Put in thy name.

MOSCA

Put in thy name. [Getting a blank will] It will be rare, sir.

VOLPONE

Put in thy name. [Getting a blank will] It will be rare, sir. Aye,
74When they ev’n gape, and find themselves deluded –

MOSCA

75Yes.

VOLPONE

Yes. And thou use them scurvily!
76Dispatch, get on thy gown.

MOSCA

Dispatch, get on thy gown. (Dressing.) But, what, sir, if they ask
77After the body?

VOLPONE

After the body? Say, it was corrupted.

MOSCA

78I'll say it stunk, sir; and was fain to have it
79Coffined up instantly, and sent away.

VOLPONE

80Any thing; what thou wilt. Hold, here's my will.
81Get thee a cap, a count-book, pen and ink,
82Papers afore thee; sit as thou wert taking
83An inventory of parcels. I'll get up
84Behind the curtain, on a stool, and hearken;
85Sometime peep over, see how they do look,
86With what degrees their blood doth leave their faces.
87O, 'twill afford me a rare meal of laughter!

MOSCA

88Your advocate will turn stark dull upon it.

VOLPONE

89It will take off his oratory's edge.

MOSCA

90But your clarissimo, old round-back, he
91Will crump you like a hog-louse, with the touch.

VOLPONE

92And what Corvino?

MOSCA

And what Corvino? O, sir, look for him,
93To-morrow morning, with a rope and dagger,
94To visit all the streets; he must run mad.
95My lady too, that came into the court,
96To bear false witness for your worship –

VOLPONE

To bear false witness for your worship – Yes,
97And kissed me 'fore the fathers, when my face
98Flowed all with oils.

MOSCA

Flowed all with oils. And sweat– sir. Why, your gold
99Is such another med’cine, it dries up
100All those offensive savors: it transforms
101The most deformed, and restores them lovely,
102As 'twere the strange poetical girdle. Jove
103Could not invent t'himself a shroud more subtle
104To pass Acrisius' guards. It is the thing
105Makes all the world her grace, her youth, her beauty.

VOLPONE

106I think she loves me.

MOSCA

I think she loves me. Who? The lady, sir?
107She's jealous of you.

VOLPONE

She's jealous of you. Dost thou say so?

[Knocking outside.]

MOSCA

She's jealous of you. Dost thou say so? Hark,
108There's some already.

VOLPONE

There's some already. Look.

MOSCA

There's some already. Look. It is the Vulture;
109He has the quickest scent.

VOLPONE

He has the quickest scent. I'll to my place,
110Thou to thy posture.

[Conceals himself.]

MOSCA

Thou to thy posture. I am set.

VOLPONE

Thou to thy posture. I am set. But, Mosca,
111Play the artificer now, torture them rarely.

ACT 5, SCENE 3

[Enter Voltore.]

VOLTORE

1How now, my Mosca?

MOSCA

How now, my Mosca? Turkey carpets, nine –

VOLTORE

2Taking an inventory! That is well.

MOSCA

3Two suits of bedding, tissue–

VOLTORE

Two suits of bedding, tissue– Where's the will?
4Let me read that the while.

[Enter Corbaccio in a sedan chair.]

CORBACCIO

Let me read that the while. So, set me down,
5And get you home.

[Exeunt servants.]

VOLTORE

And get you home. Is he come now, to trouble us!

MOSCA

6Of cloth of gold, two more –

CORBACCIO

Of cloth of gold, two more – Is it done, Mosca?

MOSCA

7Of several velvets, eight –

VOLTORE

Of several velvets, eight – I like his care.

CORBACCIO

8Dost thou not hear?

[Enter Corvino.]

CORBACCIO

Dost thou not hear? Ha! is the hour come, Mosca?

(Volpone peeps from behind a traverse.)

VOLTORE

9Aye, now they muster.aparte

CORVINO

Aye, now they muster. What does the advocate here,
10Or this Corbaccio?

CORBACCIO

Or this Corbaccio? What do these here?

[Enter Lady Politic Would-be.]

LADY WOULD-BE

Or this Corbaccio? What do these here? Mosca!
11Is his thread spun?

MOSCA

Is his thread spun? Eight chests of linen–

VOLPONE

Is his thread spun? Eight chests of linen– O,
12My fine dame Would-be, too!

CORVINO

My fine dame Would-be, too! Mosca, the will,
13That I may show it these, and rid them hence.

MOSCA

14Six chests of diaper, four of damask. – There.

[Gives Corvino the will.]

CORBACCIO

15Is that the will?

MOSCA

Is that the will? Down-beds and bolsters –

VOLPONE

Is that the will? Down-beds and bolsters – Rare!aparte
16Be busy still. Now they begin to flutter;
17They never think of me. Look, see, see, see!
18How their swift eyes run over the long deed
19Unto the name, and to the legacies,
20What is bequeathed them there –

MOSCA

What is bequeathed them there – Ten suits of hangings –

VOLPONE

21Aye, in their garters, Mosca. Now their hopesaparte
22Are at the gasp.

VOLTORE

Are at the gasp. Mosca the heir!
Are at the gasp. Mosca the heir! CORBACCIO What's that?

VOLPONE

23My advocate is dumb; look to my merchant,aparte
24He has heard of some strange storm, a ship is lost,
25He faints; my lady will swoon. Old glazen-eyes,
26He hath not reached his despair yet.

CORBACCIO

He hath not reached his despair yet. All these
27Are out of hope; I am, sure, the man.

CORVINO

Are out of hope; I am, sure, the man. But, Mosca –

MOSCA

28Two cabinets.

CORVINO

Two cabinets. Is this in earnest?

MOSCA

Two cabinets. Is this in earnest? One
29Of ebony –

CORVINO

Of ebony – Or do you but delude me?

MOSCA

30The other, mother-of-pearl –I am very busy.
31Good faith, it is a fortune thrown upon me –
32Item, one salt of agate — not my seeking.

LADY WOULD-BE

33Do you hear, sir?

MOSCA

Do you hear, sir? A perfumed box - Pray you forbear,
34You see I’m troubled – made of an onyx–

LADY WOULD-BE

You see I’m troubled – made of an onyx– How?

MOSCA

35Tomorrow or next day, I shall be at leisure
36To talk with you all.

CORVINO

To talk with you all. Is this my large hope's issue?

LADY WOULD-BE

37Sir, I must have a fairer answer.

MOSCA

Sir, I must have a fairer answer. Madam!
38Marry, and shall: pray you, fairly quit my house.
39Nay, raise no tempest with your looks; but hark you,
40Remember what your ladyship offered me
41To put you in an heir; go to, think on it:
42And what you said e'en your best madams did
43For maintenance; and why not you? Enough.
44Go home, and use the poor Sir Pol, your knight, well,
45For fear I tell some riddles; go, be melancholy.

[Exit Lady Politic Would-be]

VOLPONE

46O, my fine devil!

CORVINO

O, my fine devil! Mosca, pray you a word.

MOSCA

47Lord! Will you not take your dispatch hence yet?
48Methinks, of all, you should have been the example.
49Why should you stay here? With what thought, what promise?
50Hear you; do you not know, I know you an ass,
51And that you would most fain have been a wittol,
52If fortune would have let you? That you are
53A declared cuckold, on good terms? This pearl,
54You'll say, was yours? Right. This diamond?
55I'll not deny't, but thank you. Much here else?
56It may be so. Why, think that these good works
57May help to hide your bad. I'll not betray you;
58Although you be but extraordinary
59And have it only in title, it sufficeth.
60Go home, be melancholic too, or mad.

[Exit Corvino.]

VOLPONE

61Rare, Mosca! How his villainy becomes him!aparte

VOLTORE

62Certain he doth delude all these for me.

CORBACCIO

63Mosca the heir!

VOLPONE

Mosca the heir! O, his four eyes have found it.aparte

CORBACCIO

64I’m cozened, cheated, by a parasite slave;
65Harlot, thou hast gulled me.

MOSCA

Harlot, thou hast gulled me. Yes, sir. Stop your mouth,
66Or I shall draw the only tooth is left.
67Are not you he, that filthy covetous wretch,
68With the three legs, that here, in hope of prey,
69Have, any time this three years, snuffed about,
70With your most grovelling nose, and would have hired
71Me to the poisoning of my patron, sir?
72Are not you he that have to-day in court
73Professed the disinheriting of your son?
74Perjured yourself? Go home, and die, and stink.
75If you but croak a syllable, all comes out:
76Away, and call your porters. Go, go stink.

[Exit Corbaccio.]

VOLPONE

77Excellent varlet!aparte

VOLTORE

Excellent varlet! Now, my faithful Mosca,aparte
78I find thy constancy.aparte

MOSCA

79Sir?

VOLTORE

Sir? Sincere.

MOSCA

Sir? Sincere. A table
80Of porphyry – I mar’l you'll be thus troublesome.

VOLTORE

81Nay, leave off now, they are gone.

MOSCA

Nay, leave off now, they are gone. Why, who are you?
82What? Who did send for you? O, cry you mercy,
83Reverend sir! Good faith, I am grieved for you,
84That any chance of mine should thus defeat
85Your (I must needs say) most deserving travails:
86But I protest, sir, it was cast upon me,
87And I could almost wish to be without it,
88But that the will of the dead must be observed.
89Marry, my joy is that you need it not;
90You have a gift, sir (thank your education),
91Will never let you want, while there are men,
92And malice, to breed causes. Would I had
93But half the like, for all my fortune, sir!
94If I have any suits, as I do hope;
95Things being so easy and direct, I shall not,
96I will make bold with your obstreperous aid,
97Conceive me, –for your fee, sir. In mean time
98You that have so much law, I know have the conscience
99Not to be covetous of what is mine.
100Good sir, I thank you for my plate; 'twill help
101To set up a young man. Good faith; you look
102As you were costive; best go home and purge, sir.

[Exit Voltore.]

VOLPONE

103[coming forward] Bid him eat lettuce well. My witty mischief,
104Let me embrace thee. O, that I could now
105Transform thee to a Venus! –Mosca, go,
106Straight take my habit of clarissimo,
107And walk the streets; be seen, torment them more:
108We must pursue, as well as plot. Who would
109Have lost this feast?

MOSCA

Have lost this feast? I doubt it will lose them.

VOLPONE

110O, my recovery shall recover all.
111That I could now but think on some disguise
112To meet them in, and ask them questions:
113How I would vex them still at every turn!

MOSCA

114Sir, I can fit you.

VOLPONE

Sir, I can fit you. Canst thou?

MOSCA

Sir, I can fit you. Canst thou? Yes, I know
115One o' the Commendadori, sir, so like you;
116Him will I straight make drunk, and bring you his habit.

VOLPONE

117A rare disguise, and answering thy brain!
118O, I will be a sharp disease unto them.

MOSCA

119Sir, you must look for curses –

VOLPONE

Sir, you must look for curses – Till they burst!
120The Fox fares ever best when he is curst.

[Exeunt.]

ACT 5, SCENE 4

[Enter Peregrine in disguise, and three Merchants.]

PEREGRINE

1Am I enough disguised?

FIRST MERCHANT

Am I enough disguised? I warrant you.

PEREGRINE

2All my ambition is to fright him only.

SECOND MERCHANT

3If you could ship him away, 'twere excellent.

THIRD MERCHANT

4To Zant, or to Aleppo?

PEREGRINE

To Zant, or to Aleppo? Yes, and have his
5Adventures put i'th’ Book of voyages,
6And his gulled story registered for truth.
7Well, gentlemen, when I am in a while,
8And that you think us warm in our discourse,
9Know your approaches.

FIRST MERCHANT

Know your approaches. Trust it to our care.

[Exeunt Merchants, enter Waiting-woman.]

PEREGRINE

10Save you, fair lady. Is sir Pol within?

WOMAN

11I do not know, sir.

PEREGRINE

I do not know, sir. Pray you say unto him,
12Here is a merchant, upon earnest business,
13Desires to speak with him.

WOMAN

Desires to speak with him. I will see, sir.

PEREGRINE

Desires to speak with him. I will see, sir. Pray you.
14I see the family is all female here.aparte

WOMAN

15He says, sir, he has weighty affairs of state
16That now require him whole; some other time
17You may possess him.

PEREGRINE

You may possess him. Pray you say again,
18If those require him whole, these will exact him,
19Whereof I bring him tidings.

[Exit Woman.]

Whereof I bring him tidings. What might be
20His grave affair of state, now! How to make
21Bolognian sausages here in Venice, sparing
22One o' the ingredients?

[Re-Enter Woman.]

WOMAN

One o' the ingredients? Sir, he says, he knows
23By your word “tidings” that you are no statesman,
24And therefore wills you stay.

PEREGRINE

And therefore wills you stay. Sweet, pray you return him;
25I have not read so many proclamations,
26And studied them for words, as he has done–
27But – here he deigns to come.

[Enter Sir Politic.]

SIR POLITIC

But – here he deigns to come. Sir, I must crave
28Your courteous pardon. There hath chanced to-day
29Unkind disaster 'twixt my lady and me;
30And I was penning my apology,
31To give her satisfaction, as you came now.

PEREGRINE

32Sir, I am grieved I bring you worse disaster:
33The gentleman you met at the port to-day,
34That told you, he was newly arrived –

SIR POLITIC

That told you, he was newly arrived – Aye, was
35A fugitive punk?

PEREGRINE

A fugitive punk? No, sir, a spy set on you;
36And he has made relation to the Senate,
37That you professed to him to have a plot
38To sell the state of Venice to the Turk.

SIR POLITIC

39O me!

PEREGRINE

O me! For which, warrants are signed by this time,
40To apprehend you, and to search your study
41For papers –

SIR POLITIC

For papers – Alas, sir, I have none, but notes
42Drawn out of play-books –

PEREGRINE

Drawn out of play-books – All the better, sir.

SIR POLITIC

43And some essays. What shall I do?

PEREGRINE

And some essays. What shall I do? Sir, best
44Convey yourself into a sugar-chest;
45Or, if you could lie round, a frail were rare,
46And I could send you aboard.

SIR POLITIC

And I could send you aboard. Sir, I but talked so,
47For discourse sake merely.

(They knock without.)

PEREGRINE

For discourse sake merely. Hark! they are there.

SIR POLITIC

48I am a wretch, a wretch!

PEREGRINE

I am a wretch, a wretch! What will you do, sir?
49Have you ne'er a currant-butt to leap into?
50They'll put you to the rack; you must be sudden.

SIR POLITIC

51Sir, I have an engine –

THIRD MERCHANT

Sir, I have an engine – Sir Politic Would-be!

SECOND MERCHANT

52Where is he?

SIR POLITIC

Where is he? That I have thought upon before time.

PEREGRINE

53What is it?

SIR POLITIC

What is it? I shall ne'er endure the torture.
54Marry, it is, sir, of a tortoise-shell,
55Fitted for these extremities. Pray you, sir, help me.
56[Get into the shell.] Here I’ve a place, sir, to put back my legs,
57Please you to lay it on, sir. With this cap,
58And my black gloves. I'll lie sir, like a tortoise,
59Till they are gone.

PEREGRINE

Till they are gone. And call you this an engine?

SIR POLITIC

60Mine own device – Good sir, bid my wife's women
61To burn my papers.

[Exit Peregrine and Woman. The three Merchants rush in.]

FIRST MERCHANT

To burn my papers. Where is he hid?

THIRD MERCHANT

To burn my papers. Where is he hid? We must,
62And will sure find him.

SECOND MERCHANT

And will sure find him. Which is his study?

[Re-enter Peregrine.]

FIRST MERCHANT

And will sure find him. Which is his study? What
63Are you, sir?

PEREGRINE

Are you, sir? I am a merchant, that came here
64To look upon this tortoise.

THIRD MERCHANT

To look upon this tortoise. How!

FIRST MERCHANT

To look upon this tortoise. How! St. Mark!
65What beast is this!

PEREGRINE

What beast is this! It is a fish.

SECOND MERCHANT

What beast is this! It is a fish. Come out here!

PEREGRINE

66Nay, you may strike him, sir, and tread upon him;
67He'll bear a cart.

FIRST MERCHANT

He'll bear a cart. What, to run over him?

PEREGRINE

He'll bear a cart. What, to run over him? Yes.

THIRD MERCHANT

68Let's jump upon him.

SECOND MERCHANT

Let's jump upon him. Can he not go?

PEREGRINE

Let's jump upon him. Can he not go? He creeps, sir.

FIRST MERCHANT

69Let's see him creep.

PEREGRINE

Let's see him creep. No, good sir, you will hurt him.

SECOND MERCHANT

70Heart, I will see him creep, or prick his guts.

THIRD MERCHANT

71Come out here!

PEREGRINE

Come out here! Pray you, sir!
72(To Sir Politic.) Creep a little.

FIRST MERCHANT

(To Sir Politic.) Creep a little. Forth.

SECOND MERCHANT

73Yet farther.

PEREGRINE

Yet farther. Good sir! - Creep.

SECOND MERCHANT

Yet farther. Good sir! - Creep. We'll see his legs.

[They pull off the shell and discover him.]

THIRD MERCHANT

74Godso, he has garters!

FIRST MERCHANT

Godso, he has garters! Aye, and gloves!

SECOND MERCHANT

Godso, he has garters! Aye, and gloves! Is this
75Your fearful tortoise?

PEREGRINE

Your fearful tortoise? Now, Sir Pol, we are even;
76For your next project I shall be prepared:
77I am sorry for the funeral of your notes, sir.

FIRST MERCHANT

78'Twere a rare motion to be seen in Fleet-Street.

SECOND MERCHANT

79Aye, in the Term.

FIRST MERCHANT

Aye, in the Term. Or Smithfield, in the fair.

THIRD MERCHANT

80Methinks 'tis but a melancholic sight.

PEREGRINE

81Farewell, most politic tortoise!

[Exeunt Peregrine and Merchants. Re-enter Waiting-woman.]

SIR POLITIC

Farewell, most politic tortoise! Where's my lady?
82Knows she of this?

WOMAN

Knows she of this? I know not, sir.

SIR POLITIC

Knows she of this? I know not, sir. Enquire.—

[Exit Woman.]

83O, I shall be the fable of all feasts,
84The freight of the gazetti, ship-boys’ tale;
85And, which is worst, even talk for ordinaries.

WOMAN

86My lady's come most melancholic home,
87And says, sir, she will straight to sea, for physic.

SIR POLITIC

88And I to shun this place and clime for ever,
89Creeping with house on back, and think it well
90To shrink my poor head in my politic shell.

[Exeunt.]

ACT 5, SCENE 5

[Enter Volpone, Mosca; the first, in the habit of a commendatore, and the other, of a clarissimo.]

VOLPONE

1Am I then like him?

MOSCA

Am I then like him? O, sir, you are he:
2No man can sever you.

VOLPONE

No man can sever you. Good.

149Take heart, and love to study 'em! Mischiefs feed
150Like beasts, till they be fat, and then they bleed.

[Exeunt.]

VOLPONE

151The seasoning of a play, is the applause.
152Now, though the Fox be punished by the laws,
153He yet doth hope, there is no suffering due,
154For any fact which he hath done 'gainst you;
155If there be, censure him; here he doubtful stands.
156If not, fare jovially, and clap your hands.

[Exit.]
