ACT 1

SCENE 1

(Enter Volpone and Mosca.)

VOLPONE

1Good morning to the day; and next, my gold! –
2Open the shrine, that I may see my saint.
3[Mosca reveals the treasure.] Hail the world's soul, and mine! more glad than is
4The teeming earth to see the longed-for sun
5Peep through the horns of the celestial Ram,
6Am I, to view thy splendour darkening his;
7That lying here, amongst my other hoards,
8Show'st like a flame by night, or like the day
9Struck out of chaos, when all darkness fled
10Unto the center. O thou son of Sol,
11But brighter than thy father, let me kiss,
12With adoration, thee, and every relic
13Of sacred treasure in this blessed room.
14Well did wise poets, by thy glorious name,
15Title that age which they would have the best;
16Thou being the best of things, and far transcending
17All style of joy, in children, parents, friends,
18Or any other waking dream on earth:
19Thy looks when they to Venus did ascribe,
20They should have given her twenty thousand Cupids;
21Such are thy beauties and our loves! Dear saint,
22Riches, the dumb god, that giv'st all men tongues,
23Thou canst do naught, and yet mak'st men do all things;
24The price of souls; even hell, with thee to boot,
25Is made worth heaven. Thou art virtue, fame,
26Honour, and all things else. Who can get thee,
27He shall be noble valiant, honest, wise –

MOSCA

28And what he will, sir. Riches are in fortune
29A greater good than wisdom is in nature.

VOLPONE

30True, my beloved Mosca. Yet I glory
31More in the cunning purchase of my wealth,
32Than in the glad possession, since I gain
33No common way; I use no trade, no venture;
34I wound no earth with plough-shares, fat no beasts,
35To feed the shambles; have no mills for iron,
36Oil, corn, or men, to grind them into powder;
37I blow no subtle glass; expose no ships
38To threat'nings of the furrow-faced sea;
39I turn no monies in the public bank,
40Nor usure private.

MOSCA

Nor usure private. No, sir, nor devour
41Soft prodigals. You shall have some will swallow
42A melting heir as glibly as your Dutch
43Will pills of butter, and ne'er purge for it;
44Tear forth the fathers of poor families
45Out of their beds, and coffin them alive
46In some kind clasping prison, where their bones
47May be forthcoming, when the flesh is rotten:
48But your sweet nature doth abhor these courses;
49You loathe the widow's or the orphan's tears
50Should wash your pavements, or their piteous cries
51Ring in your roofs, and beat the air for vengeance.

VOLPONE

52Right, Mosca, I do lothe it.

MOSCA

Right, Mosca, I do lothe it. And besides, sir,
53You are not like the thresher that doth stand
54With a huge flail, watching a heap of corn,
55And, hungry, dares not taste the smallest grain,
56But feeds on mallows, and such bitter herbs;
57Nor like the merchant, who hath filled his vaults
58With Romagnia, and rich Candian wines,
59Yet drinks the lees of Lombard's vinegar:
60You will lie not in straw, whilst moths and worms
61Feed on your sumptuous hangings and soft beds;
62You know the use of riches, and dare give now
63From that bright heap, to me, your poor observer,
64Or to your dwarf, or your hermaphrodite,
65Your eunuch, or what other household trifle
66Your pleasure allows maintenance –

VOLPONE

Your pleasure allows maintenance – Hold thee, Mosca,
67[Gives him money.] Take of my hand; thou strik'st on truth in all,
68And they are envious term thee parasite.
69Call forth my dwarf, my eunuch, and my fool,
70And let them make me sport.
And let them make me sport. [Exit Mosca.] What should I do
71But cocker up my genius, and live free
72To all delights my fortune calls me to?
73I have no wife, no parent, child, ally,
74To give my substance to; but whom I make
75Must be my heir: and this makes men observe me:
76This draws new clients daily to my house,
77Women and men of every sex and age,
78That bring me presents, send me plate, coin, jewels,
79With hope that when I die (which they expect
80Each greedy minute) it shall then return
81Ten-fold upon them; whilst some, covetous
82Above the rest, seek to engross me whole,
83And counter-work the one unto the other,
84Contend in gifts as they would seem in love:
85All which I suffer, playing with their hopes,
86And am content to coin them into profit,
87And look upon their kindness, and take more,
88And look on that; still bearing them in hand,
89Letting the cherry knock against their lips,
90And draw it by their mouths and back again. – How now!

ACT 1, Scene 2

(Enter Mosca with Nano, Androgyno, and Castrone.)

NANO

1Now, room for fresh gamester, who do will you to know,
2They do bring you neither play nor university show;
3And therefore do entreat you, that whatsoever they rehearse,
4May not fare a whit the worse, for the false pace of the verse.
5If you wonder at this, you will wonder more ere we pass,
6For know, here is enclosed the soul of Pythagoras,
7That juggler divine, as hereafter shall follow;
8Which soul, fast and loose, sir, came first from Apollo,
9And was breathed into Aethalides, Mercurius, his son,
10Where it had the gift to remember all that ever was done.
11From thence it fled forth, and made quick transmigration
12To goldilocked Euphorbus, who was killed in good fashion,
13At the siege of old Troy, by the cuckold of Sparta.
14Hermotimus was next (I find it in my charta)
15To whom it did pass, where no sooner it was missing
16But with one Pyrrhus of Delos it learned to go a-fishing;
17And thence did it enter the sophist of Greece.
18From Pythagore, she went into a beautiful piece,
19Hight Aspasia, the meretrix; and the next toss of her
20Was again of a whore, she became a philosopher,
21Crates the cynic, as itself doth relate it:
22Since kings, knights, and beggars, knaves, lords, and
23fools gat it,
24Besides ox and ass, camel, mule, goat, and brock,
25In all which it hath spoke, as in the cobbler’s cock.
26But I come not here to discourse of that matter,
27Or his one, two, or three, or his great oath, “By quarter!”
28His musics, his trigon, his golden thigh,
29Or his telling how elements shift, but I
30Would ask, how of late thou hast suffered translation,
31And shifted thy coat in these days of reformation.

ANDROGYNO

32Like one of the reformed, a fool, as you see,
33Counting all old doctrine heresy.

NANO

34But not on thine own forbid meats hast thou ventured?

ANDROGYNO

35On fish, when first a Carthusian I entered.

NANO

36Why, then thy dogmatical silence hath left thee?

ANDROGYNO

37Of that an obstreperous lawyer bereft me.

NANO

38O wonderful change, when sir lawyer forsook thee!
39For Pythagore's sake, what body then took thee?

ANDROGYNO

40A good dull mule.

NANO

A good dull mule. And how, by that means,
41Thou wert brought to allow of the eating of beans?

ANDROGYNO

42Yes.

NANO

Yes. But from the mule into whom didst thou pass?

ANDROGYNO

43Into a very strange beast, by some writers called an ass;
44By others, a precise, pure, illuminate brother,
45Of those devour flesh, and sometimes one another;
46And will drop you forth a libel, or a sanctified lie,
47Betwixt every spoonful of a nativity-pie.

NANO

48Now quit thee, for heaven, of that profane nation,
49And gently report thy next transmigration.

ANDROGYNO

50To the same that I am.

NANO

To the same that I am. A creature of delight,
51And, what is more than a fool, an hermaphrodite!
52Now, prithee, sweet soul, in all thy variation,
53Which body would’st thou choose, to keep up thy station?

ANDROGYNO

54Troth, this I am in: even here would I tarry.

NANO

55'Cause here the delight of each sex thou canst vary?

ANDROGYNO

56Alas, those pleasures be stale and forsaken;
57No, 'tis your fool wherewith I am so taken,
58The only one creature that I can call blessed;
59For all other forms I have proved most distressed.

NANO

60Spoke true, as thou wert in Pythagoras still.
61This learned opinion we celebrate will,
62Fellow eunuch, as behoves us, with all our wit and art,
63To dignify that whereof ourselves are so great and
64special a part.

VOLPONE

65Now, very, very pretty! Mosca, this
66Was thy invention?

MOSCA

Was thy invention? If it please my patron,
67Not else.

VOLPONE

Not else. It doth, good Mosca.

MOSCA

Not else. It doth, good Mosca. Then it was, sir.

53He ever liked your course, sir; that first took him.
54I oft have heard him say, how he admired
55Men of your large profession, that could speak
56To every cause, and things mere contraries,

58That, with most quick agility, could turn,
59And return; make knots, and undo them;
60Give forked counsel; take provoking gold
61On either hand, and put it up: these men,
62He knew, would thrive with their humility.

65So wise, so grave, of so perplexed a tongue,
66And loud withal, that would not wag, nor scarce
