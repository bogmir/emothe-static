ACT 2

SCENE 1

[Enter Politic Would-be, Peregrine]

SIR POLITIC

1Sir, to a wise man, all the world’s his soil:
2It is not Italy, nor France, nor Europe,
3That must bound me, if my fates call me forth.
4Yet, I protest, it is no salt desire
5Of seeing countries, shifting a religion,
6Nor any disaffection to the state
7Where I was bred, and unto which I owe
8My dearest plots, hath brought me out; much less,
9That idle, antique, stale, gray-headed project
10Of knowing men’s minds and manners, with Ulysses!
11But a peculiar humor of my wife’s
12Laid for this height of Venice, to observe,
13To quote, to learn the language, and so forth –
14I hope you travel, sir, with license?

PEREGRINE

I hope you travel, sir, with license? Yes.

SIR POLITIC

15I dare the safelier converse- How long, sir,
16Since you left England?

PEREGRINE

Since you left England? Seven weeks.

SIR POLITIC

Since you left England? Seven weeks. So lately!
17You have not been with my Lord Ambassador?

PEREGRINE

You have not been with my Lord Ambassador? Not yet, sir.

SIR POLITIC

18Pray you, what news, sir, vents our Climate?
19I heard last night a most strange thing reported
20By some of my lord’s followers, and I long
21To hear how ‘twill be seconded.

PEREGRINE

To hear how ‘twill be seconded. What was’t, sir?

SIR POLITIC

22Marry, sir, of a raven that should build
23In a ship royal of the king’s.

PEREGRINE

In a ship royal of the king’s. This fellow,aparte
24Does he gull me, trow? or is gulled? – Your name, sir?

SIR POLITIC

25My name is Politic Would-be,

PEREGRINE

My name is Politic Would-be, O, that speaks him –aparte
26A knight, sir?

SIR POLITIC

A knight, sir? A poor knight, sir.

PEREGRINE

A knight, sir? A poor knight, sir. Your lady
27Lies here in Venice, for intelligence
28Of tires, and fashions, and behaviour,
29Among the courtesans? The fine Lady Would-be?

SIR POLITIC

30Yes, sir; the spider and the bee, oft times,
31Suck from one flower.

PEREGRINE

Suck from one flower. Good Sir Politic,
32I cry you mercy; I have heard much of you:
33‘Tis true, sir, of your raven.

SIR POLITIC

‘Tis true, sir, of your raven. On your knowledge?

PEREGRINE

34Yes, and your lion’s whelping in the Tower.

SIR POLITIC

35Another whelp!

PEREGRINE

Another whelp! Another, sir.

SIR POLITIC

Another whelp! Another, sir. Now, heaven!
36What prodigies be these? The fires at Berwick!
37And the new star! These things concurring, strange,
38And full of omen! Saw you those meteors?

PEREGRINE

39I did, sir.

SIR POLITIC

I did, sir. Fearful! Pray you, sir, confirm me,
40Were there three porpoises seen above the Bridge,
41As they give out?

PEREGRINE

As they give out? Six, and a sturgeon, sir.

SIR POLITIC

42I am astonished.

PEREGRINE

I am astonished. Nay, sir, be not so;
43I’ll tell you a greater prodigy than these.

SIR POLITIC

44What should these things portend?

PEREGRINE

What should these things portend? The very day
45(Let me be sure) that I put forth from London,
46There was a whale discovered in the river,
47As high as Woolwich, that had waited there,
48Few know how many months, for the subversion
49Of the Stode Fleet.

SIR POLITIC

Of the Stode Fleet. Is’t possible? believe it,
50‘Twas either sent from Spain, or the archdukes:
51Spinola’s whale, upon my life, my credit!
52Will they not leave these projects? Worthy sir,
53Some other news.

PEREGRINE

Some other news. Faith, Stone the fool is dead,
54And they do lack a tavern fool extremely.

SIR POLITIC

55Is Mas’ Stone dead?

PEREGRINE

Is Mas’ Stone dead? He’s dead, sir; why, I hope
56You thought him not immortal?
You thought him not immortal? O, this knight,aparte
57Were he well known, would be a precious thing
58To fit our English stage: he that should write
59But such a fellow, should be thought to feign
60Extremely, if not maliciously.

SIR POLITIC

Extremely, if not maliciously. Stone dead!

PEREGRINE

61Dead.— Lord! how deeply, sir, you apprehend it?
62He was no kinsman to you?

SIR POLITIC

He was no kinsman to you? That I know of.
63Well! that same fellow was an unknown fool.

PEREGRINE

64And yet you knew him, it seems?

SIR POLITIC

And yet you knew him, it seems? I did so. Sir,
65I knew him one of the most dangerous heads
66Living within the state, and so I held him.

PEREGRINE

67Indeed, sir?

SIR POLITIC

Indeed, sir? While he lived, in action,
68He has received weekly intelligence,
69Upon my knowledge, out of the Low Countries,
70For all parts of the world, in cabbages;
71And those dispensed again to ambassadors
72In oranges, musk-melons, apricots,
73Lemons, pome-citrons, and such-like; sometimes
74In Colchester oysters, and your Selsey cockles.

PEREGRINE

75You make me wonder.

SIR POLITIC

You make me wonder. Sir, upon my knowledge.
76Nay, I’ve observed him, at your public ordinary,
77Take his advertisement from a traveller,
78A concealed statesman, in a trencher of meat;
79And instantly, before the meal was done,
80Convey an answer in a tooth-pick.

PEREGRINE

Convey an answer in a tooth-pick. Strange!
81How could this be, sir?

SIR POLITIC

How could this be, sir? Why, the meat was cut
82so like his character, and so laid, as he
83must easily read the cipher.

PEREGRINE

must easily read the cipher. I have heard,
84He could not read, sir.

SIR POLITIC

He could not read, sir. So ‘twas given out,
85In policy, by those that did employ him:
86But he could read, and had your languages,
87And to’t, as sound a noddle –

PEREGRINE

And to’t, as sound a noddle – I have heard, sir,
88That your baboons were spies, and that they were
89A kind of subtle nation near to China.

SIR POLITIC

90Aye, aye, your Mamuluchi. Faith, they had
91Their hand in a French plot or two; but they
92Were so extremely given to women, as
93They made discovery of all: yet I
94Had my advices here, on Wednesday last.
95From one of their own coat, they were returned,
96Made their relations, as the fashion is,
97And now stand fair for fresh employment.

PEREGRINE

And now stand fair for fresh employment. ’Heart!aparte
98This Sir Pol will be ignorant of nothing.
99It seems, sir, you know all.

SIR POLITIC

It seems, sir, you know all. Not all, sir, but
100I have some general notions. I do love
101To note and to observe: though I live out,
102Free from the active torrent, yet I’d mark
103The currents and the passages of things,
104For mine own private use; and know the ebbs
105And flows of state.

PEREGRINE

And flows of state. Believe it, sir, I hold
106Myself in no small tie unto my fortunes,
107For casting me thus luckily upon you,
108Whose knowledge, if your bounty equal it,
109May do me great assistance, in instruction
110For my behaviour, and my bearing, which
111Is yet so rude and raw.

SIR POLITIC

Is yet so rude and raw. Why, came you forth
112Empty of rules for travel?

PEREGRINE

Empty of rules for travel? Faith, I had
113Some common ones, from out that vulgar grammar,
114Which he that cried Italian to me, taught me.

SIR POLITIC

115Why this it is that spoils all our brave bloods,
116Trusting our hopeful gentry unto pedants.
117Fellows of outside, and mere bark. You seem
118To be a gentleman, of ingenuous race: —
119I not profess it, but my fate hath been
120To be, where I have been consulted with,
121In this high kind, touching some great men’s sons,
122Persons of blood and honour. –

PEREGRINE

Persons of blood and honour. – Who be these, sir?

ACT 1, SCENE 2

(Enter Mosca, Nano followed by Grege.)

MOSCA

1Under that window, there it must be. The same.

[They set out a platform.]

SIR POLITIC

2Fellows, to mount a bank. Did your instructor
3In the dear tongues, never discourse to you
4Of the Italian mountebanks?

PEREGRINE

Of the Italian mountebanks? Yes, sir.

SIR POLITIC

Of the Italian mountebanks? Yes, sir. Why,
5Here you shall see one.

PEREGRINE

Here you shall see one. They are quacksalvers;
6Fellows, that live by venting oils and drugs.

SIR POLITIC

7Was that the character he gave you of them?

PEREGRINE

8As I remember.

SIR POLITIC

As I remember. Pity his ignorance.
9They are the only knowing men of Europe!
10Great general scholars, excellent physicians,
11Most admired statesmen, professed favourites,
12And cabinet counsellors to the greatest princes;
13The only languaged men of all the world!

PEREGRINE

14And I have heard, they are most lewd impostors;
15Made all of terms and shreds; no less beliers
16Of great men’s favours, than their own vile med’cines;
17Which they will utter upon monstrous oaths:
18Selling that drug for two-pence, ere they part,
19Which they have valued at twelve crowns before.

SIR POLITIC

20Sir, calumnies are answered best with silence.
21Yourself shall judge. — Who is it mounts, my friends?

MOSCA

22Scoto of Mantua, sir.

SIR POLITIC

Scoto of Mantua, sir. Is’t he? Nay, then
23I’ll proudly promise, sir, you shall behold
24Another man than has been phant’sied to you.
25I wonder yet, that he should mount his bank,
26Here in this nook, that has been wont t’appear
27In face of the piazza!—Here he comes.

[Enter Volpone, disguised as a mountebank doctor, and followed by a crowd of people.]

VOLPONE

28[To Nano] Mount, zany.

GREGE

[To Nano] Mount, zany. Follow, follow, follow, follow, follow!

SIR POLITIC

29See how the people follow him! He’s a man
30May write ten thousand crowns in bank here. Note,
31[Volpone mounts the platform.] Mark but his gesture—I do use to observe
32The state he keeps in getting up.

PEREGRINE

The state he keeps in getting up. ‘Tis worth it, sir.

VOLPONE

Most noble gentlemen, and my worthy patrons! It may seem strange, that I, your Scoto Mantuano, who was ever wont to fix my bank in face of the public Piazza, near the shelter of the Portico to the Procuratia, should now, after eight months absence from this illustrious city of Venice, humbly retire myself into an obscure nook of the Piazza.

SIR POLITIC

40Did not I now object the same?

PEREGRINE

Did not I now object the same? Peace, sir.

VOLPONE

Let me tell you: I am not, as your Lombard proverb saith, cold on my feet; or content to part with my commodities at a cheaper rate, than I accustomed: look not for it. Nor that the calumnious reports of that impudent detractor, and shame to our profession (Alessandro Buttone, I mean), who gave out, in public, I was condemned a sforzato to the galleys, for poisoning the cardinal Bembo's... cook, hath at all attached, much less dejected me. No, no, worthy gentlemen; to tell you true, I cannot endure to see the rabble of these ground ciarlitani , that spread their cloaks on the pavement, as if they meant to do feats of activity, and then come in lamely, with their mouldy tales out of Boccacio, like stale Tabarine, the fabulist: some of them discoursing their travels, and of their tedious captivity in the Turks' galleys, when, indeed, were the truth known, they were the Christians' galleys, where very temperately they eat bread, and drunk water, as a wholesome penance, enjoined them by their confessors, for base pilferies.

SIR POLITIC

61Note but his bearing, and contempt of these.

VOLPONE

These turdy-facy-nasty-paty-lousy-fartical rogues, with one poor groat's-worth of unprepared antimony, finely wrapped up in several scartoccios , are able, very well, to kill their twenty a week, and play; yet, these meagre, starved spirits, who have half stopped the organs of their minds with earthy oppilations, want not their favourers among your shrivelled salad-eating artisans, who are overjoyed that they may have their half-pe'rth of physic; though it purge them into another world, it makes no matter.

SIR POLITIC

72Excellent! Have you heard better language, sir?

VOLPONE

Well, let them go. And, gentlemen, honourable gentlemen, know, that for this time, our bank, being thus removed from the clamours of the canaglia , shall be the scene of pleasure and delight; for I have nothing to sell, little or nothing to sell.

SIR POLITIC

78I told you, sir, his end.

PEREGRINE

I told you, sir, his end. You did so, sir.

VOLPONE

I protest, I, and my six servants, are not able to make of this precious liquor, so fast as it is fetched away from my lodging by gentlemen of your city; strangers of the terra-firma; worshipful merchants; aye, and senators too: who, ever since my arrival, have detained me to their uses, by their splendidous liberalities. And worthily; for, what avails your rich man to have his magazines stuffed with moscadelli, or of the purest grape, when his physicians prescribe him, on pain of death, to drink nothing but water cocted with aniseeds? O, health! Health! The blessing of the rich! the riches of the poor! Who can buy thee at too dear a rate, since there is no enjoying this world without thee? Be not then so sparing of your purses, honourable gentlemen, as to abridge the natural course of life –

PEREGRINE

94You see his end.

SIR POLITIC

You see his end. Aye, is’t not good?

VOLPONE

For, when a humid flux, or catarrh, by the mutability of air, falls from your head into an arm or shoulder, or any other part; take you a ducat, or your cecchine of gold, and apply to the place affected: see what good effect it can work. No, no, 'tis this blessed unguento , this rare extraction, that hath only power to disperse all malignant humours, that proceed either of hot, cold, moist, or windy causes –

PEREGRINE

103I would he had put in dry too.

SIR POLITIC

I would he had put in dry too. Pray you, observe.

VOLPONE

To fortify the most indigest and crude stomach, aye, were it of one that, through extreme weakness, vomited blood, applying only a warm napkin to the place, after the unction and fricace;— for the vertigine in the head, putting but a drop into your nostrils, likewise behind the ears; a most sovereign and approved remedy: the mal caduco, cramps, convulsions, paralyses, epilepsies, tremor-cordia, retired nerves, ill vapours of the spleen, stopping of the liver, the stone, the strangury, hernia ventosa, iliaca passio; stops a dysenteria immediately; easeth the torsion of the small guts; and cures melancholia hypocondriaca, being taken and applied according to my printed receipt. (Pointing to his bill and his glass) For, this is the physician, this the medicine; this counsels, this cures; this gives the direction, this works the effect; and, in sum, both together may be termed an abstract of the theoric and practic in the Aesculapian art. 'Twill cost you eight crowns. And,—Zan Fritada, prithee sing a verse extempore in honour of it.

SIR POLITIC

124How do you like him, sir?

PEREGRINE

How do you like him, sir? Most strangely, I!

SIR POLITIC

125Is not his language rare?

PEREGRINE

Is not his language rare? But alchemy,
126I never heard the like; or Broughton’s books.

SONG

NANO

1Had old Hippocrates or Galen,
2(That to their books put med’cines all in,)
3But known this secret, they had never
4(Of which they will be guilty ever)
5Been murderers of so much paper,
6Or wasted many a hurtless taper;
7No Indian drug has e’er been famed,
8Tobacco, sassafras not named;
9Ne yet, of guacum one small stick, sir,
10Nor Raymond Lully’s great elixir.
11Ne had been known the Danish Gonswart
12Or Paracelsus, with his long sword.

PEREGRINE

13All, this, yet, will not do; eight crowns is high.

VOLPONE

No more. Gentlemen, if I had but time to discourse to you the miraculous effects of this my oil, surnamed Oglio del Scoto; with the countless catalogue of those I have cured of the aforesaid, and many more diseases; the patents and privileges of all the princes and commonwealths of Christendom; or but the depositions of those that appeared on my part, before the Signiory of the Sanita and most learned College of Physicians; where I was authorized, upon notice taken of the admirable virtues of my medicaments, and mine own excellency in matter of rare and unknown secrets, not only to disperse them publicly in this famous city, but in all the territories, that happily joy under the govern- ment of the most pious and magnificent states of Italy. But may some other gallant fellow say, O, there be diverse that make profession to have as good, and as experimented receipts as yours: indeed, very many have assayed, like apes, in imitation of that, which is really and essentially in me, to make of this oil; bestowed great cost in furnaces, stills, alembics, continual fires, and preparation of the ingredients (as indeed there goes to it six hundred several simples, besides some quantity of human fat, for the conglutination, which we buy of the anatomists), but, when these practitioners come to the last decoction, blow, blow, puff, puff, and all flies in fumo: ha, ha, ha! Poor wretches! I rather pity their folly and indiscretion, than their loss of time and money; for these may be recovered by industry: but to be a fool born, is a disease incurable. For myself, I always from my youth have endeavoured to get the rarest secrets, and book them, either in exchange, or for money; I spared nor cost nor labour, where anything was worthy to be learned. And, gentlemen, honourable gentlemen, I will undertake, by virtue of chemical art, out of the honour- able hat that covers your head, to extract the four elements; that is to say, the fire, air, water, and earth, and return you your felt without burn or stain. For, whilst others have been at the Balloo, I have been at my book; and am now past the craggy paths of study, and come to the flowery plains of honour and reputation.

SIR POLITIC

54I do assure you, sir, that is his aim.

VOLPONE

55But to our price-

PEREGRINE

But to our price- And that withal, Sir Politic.

VOLPONE

You all know, honourable gentlemen, I never valued this ampulla, or vial, at less than eight crowns; but for this time, I am content to be deprived of it for six: six crowns is the price, and less in courtesy I know you cannot offer me; take it or leave it, howsoever, both it and I am at your service. I ask you not as the value of the thing, for then I should demand of you a thousand crowns, so the Cardinals Montalto, Fernese, the great Duke of Tuscany, my gossip, with diverse other princes, have given me; but I despise money. Only to show my affection to you, honourable gentlemen, and your illustrious state here, I have neglected the messages of these princes, mine own offices, framed my journey hither, only to present you with the fruits of my travels. Tune your voices once more to the touch of your instruments, and give the honourable assembly some delightful recreation.

PEREGRINE

73What monstrous and most painful circumstance
74Is here, to get some three or four gazets,
75Some three-pence, in the whole! for that ‘twill come to.

(Song)

NANO

1You that would last long, list to my song,
2Make no more coil, but buy of this oil.
3Would you be ever fair and young?
4Stout of teeth, and strong of tongue?
5Tart of palate? quick of ear?
6Sharp of sight? of nostril clear?
7Moist of hand? and light of foot?
8Or, I will come nearer to't,
9Would you live free from all diseases?
10Do the act your mistress pleases,
11Yet fright all aches from your bones?
12Here's a medicine, for the nones.

VOLPONE

Well, I am in a humour at this time to make a present of the small quantity my coffer contains; to the rich in courtesy, and to the poor for God's sake. Wherefore now mark: I asked you six crowns; and six crowns, at other times, you have paid me; you shall not give me six crowns, nor five, nor four, nor three, nor two, nor one; nor half a ducat; no, nor a moccenigo. Six–pence it will cost you, or six hundred pound –expect no lower price, for, by the banner of my front, I will not bate a bagatine, —that I will have, only, a pledge of your loves, to carry something from amongst you, to show I am not contemned by you. Therefore, now, toss your handkerchiefs, cheerfully, cheerfully; and be advertised, that the first heroic spirit that deigns to grace me with a handkerchief, I will give it a little remembrance of something, beside, shall please it better, than if I had presented it with a double pistolet.

PEREGRINE

30Will you be that heroic spark, Sir Politic?
31[Celia throws down her handkerchief from the window above]. O, see! The window has prevented you.

VOLPONE

Lady, I kiss your bounty; and, for this timely grace you have done your poor Scoto of Mantua, I will return you, over and above my oil, a secret of that high and inestimable nature, shall make you for ever enamoured on that minute, wherein your eye first descended on so mean, yet not altogether to be despised, an object. Here is a powder concealed in this paper, of which, if I should speak to the worth, nine thousand volumes were but as one page, that page as a line, that line as a word; so short is this pilgrimage of man (which some call life) to the expressing of it. Would I reflect on the price? Why, the whole world is but as an empire, that empire as a province, that province as a bank, that bank as a private purse to the purchase of it. I will only tell you; it is the powder that made Venus a goddess (given her by Apollo), that kept her perpetually young, cleared her wrinkles, firmed her gums, filled her skin, coloured her hair; from her derived to Helen, and at the sack of Troy unfortunately lost: till now, in this our age, it was as happily recovered, by a studious antiquary, out of some ruins of Asia, who sent a moiety of it to the court of France (but much sophisticated), wherewith the ladies there, now, colour their hair. The rest, at this present, remains with me; extracted to a quintessence: so that, wherever it but touches, in youth it perpetually preserves, in age restores the complexion; seats your teeth, did they dance like virginal jacks, firm as a wall; makes them white as ivory, that were black as –

ACT 2, SCENE 3

(Enter Corvino.)

CORVINO

1Spite o’the devil, and my shame! Come down here;
2Come down; —No house but mine to make your scene?
3Signior Flaminio, will you down, sir? Down?
4What, is my wife your Franciscina, sir?
5No windows on the whole Piazza, here,
6To make your properties, but mine? but mine?

[Beats away Volpone, Nano, etc.]

7Heart! ere tomorrow I shall be new-christened,
8And called the Pantalone di Besogniosi
9About the town.

PEREGRINE

About the town. What should this mean, Sir Pol?

SIR POLITIC

10Some trick of state, believe it; I will home.

PEREGRINE

11It may be some design on you.

SIR POLITIC

It may be some design on you. I know not,
12I’ll stand upon my guard.

PEREGRINE

I’ll stand upon my guard. It is your best, sir.

SIR POLITIC

13This three weeks, all my advices, all my letters.
14They have been intercepted.

PEREGRINE

They have been intercepted. Indeed, sir!
15Best have a care.

SIR POLITIC

Best have a care. Nay, so I will.

PEREGRINE

Best have a care. Nay, so I will. This knight,
16I may not lose him, for my mirth, till night.

[Exeunt]

ACT 2, SCENE 4

(Enter Volpone, Mosca.)

VOLPONE

1O, I am wounded!

MOSCA

O, I am wounded! Where, sir?

VOLPONE

O, I am wounded! Where, sir? Not without;
2Those blows were nothing: I could bear them ever.
3But angry Cupid, bolting from her eyes,
4Hath shot himself into me like a flame;
5Where, now, he flings about his burning heat,
6As in a furnace an ambitious fire,
7Whose vent is stopped. The fight is all within me.
8I cannot live, except thou help me, Mosca;
9My liver melts, and I, without the hope
10Of some soft air, from her refreshing breath,
11Am but a heap of cinders.

MOSCA

Am but a heap of cinders. ‘Las, good sir,
12Would you had never seen her!

VOLPONE

Would you had never seen her! Nay, would thou
13Hads’t never told me of her!

MOSCA

Hads’t never told me of her! Sir, ‘tis true;
14I do confess I was unfortunate,
15And you unhappy: but I’m bound in conscience,
16No less than duty, to effect my best
17To your release of torment, and I will, sir.

VOLPONE

18Dear Mosca, shall I hope?

MOSCA

Dear Mosca, shall I hope? Sir, more than dear,
19I will not bid you to despair of aught
20Within a human compass.

VOLPONE

Within a human compass. O, there spoke
21My better angel, Mosca, take my keys,
22Gold, plate, and jewels, all’s at thy devotion;
23Employ them how thou wilt; nay, coin me too:
24So thou, in this, but crown my longings, Mosca.

MOSCA

25Use but your patience.

VOLPONE

Use but your patience. So I have.

MOSCA

Use but your patience. So I have. I doubt not
26To bring success to your desires.

VOLPONE

To bring success to your desires. Nay, then,
27I not repent me of my late disguise.

MOSCA

28If you can horn him, sir, you need not.

VOLPONE

If you can horn him, sir, you need not. True;
29Besides, I never meant him for my heir.
30Is not the colour of my beard and eyebrows
31To make me known?

MOSCA

To make me known? No jot.

VOLPONE

To make me known? No jot. I did it well.

MOSCA

32So well, would I could follow you in mine,
33With half the happiness! —and yet I would
34Escape your epilogue.

VOLPONE

Escape your epilogue. But were they gulled
35With a belief that I was Scoto?

MOSCA

With a belief that I was Scoto? Sir,
36Scoto himself could hardly have distinguished!
37I have not time to flatter you now; we’ll part;
38And as I prosper, so applaud my art.

(Exeunt.)

ACT 2, SCENE 5

[Enter Corvino, Celia.)

CORVINO

1Death of mine honour, with the city’s fool!
2A juggling, tooth-drawing, prating mountebank!
3And at a public window! where, whilst he,
4With his strained action, and his dole of faces,
5To his drug-lecture draws your itching ears,
6A crew of old, unmarried, noted lechers,
7Stood leering up like satyrs; and you smile
8Most graciously, and fan your favours forth,
9To give your hot spectators satisfaction!
10What, was your mountebank their call? Their whistle?
11Or were you enamoured on his copper rings,
12His saffron jewel, with the toad-stone in’t?
13Or his embroidered suit, with the cope-stitch,
14Made of a hearse cloth? Or his old tilt-feather?
15Or his starched beard? Well, you shall have him, yes!
16He shall come home, and minister unto you
17The fricace for the mother. Or, let me see,
18I think you’d rather mount; would you not mount?
19Why, if you’ll mount, you may; yes, truly, you may:
20And so you may be seen, down to the foot.
21Get you a cittern, Lady Vanity,
22And be a dealer with the virtuous man;
23Make one: I’ll but protest myself a cuckold
24And save your dowry. I’m a Dutchman, I!
25For, if you thought me an Italian,
26You would be damned, ere you did this, you whore!
27Thou’Idst tremble, to imagine that the murder
28Of father, mother, brother, all thy race,
29Should follow, as the subject of my justice.

CELIA

30Good sir, have patience.

CORVINO

Good sir, have patience. What couldst thou propose
31Less to thyself than, in this heat of wrath
32And stung with my dishonor, I should strike

[draws his sword]

33This steel unto thee, with as many stabs
34As thou wert gazed upon with goatish eyes?

CELIA

35Alas, sir, be appeased! I could not think
36My being at the window should more now
37Move your impatience, than at other times.

CORVINO

38No! not to seek and entertain a parley
39With a known knave, before a multitude!
40You were an actor with your handkerchief,
41Which he most sweetly kissed in the receipt,
42And might, no doubt, return it with a letter,
43And ‘point the place where you might meet; your sister’s,
44Your mother’s, or your aunt’s might serve the turn.

CELIA

45Why, dear sir, when do I make these excuses,
46Or ever stir abroad but to the church?
47And that so seldom–

CORVINO

And that so seldom– Well, it shall be less;
48And thy restraint before was liberty,
49To what I now decree: and therefore mark me.
50First, I will have this bawdy light dammed up;
51And, till’t be done, some two or three yards off,
52I’ll chalk a line, o’er which if thou but chance
53To set thy desperate foot, more hell, more horror,
54More wild remorseless rage shall seize on thee,
55Than on a conjuror, that had heedless left
56His circle’s safety ere his devil was laid.
57Then here’s a lock which I will hang upon thee,
58And, now I think on’t, I will keep thee backwards;
59Thy lodging shall be backwards, thy walks backwards;
60Thy prospect, all be backwards; and no pleasure,
61That thou shalt know but backwards: nay, since you force
62My honest nature, know it is your own,
63Being too open, makes me use you thus:
64Since you will not contain your subtle nostrils
65In a sweet room, but they must snuff the air
66Of rank and sweaty passengers–
Of rank and sweaty passengers– [Knocking.] One knocks.
67Away, and be not seen, pain of thy life;
68Not look toward the window. If thou dost–
69Nay stay, hear this–let me not prosper, whore,
70But I will make thee an anatomy,
71Dissect thee mine own self, and read a lecture
72Upon thee to the city, and in public.
73Away!
Away! [Exit Celia.] Who’s there?

[Enter Servitore]

SERVANT

Away! [Exit Celia.] Who’s there? ‘Tis Signor Mosca, sir.

ACT 2, SCENE 6

CORVINO

1Let him come in. His master’s dead: there’s yet
2Some good to help the bad.-
Some good to help the bad.- (Enter Mosca.) My Mosca, welcome!
3I guess your news.

MOSCA

I guess your news. I fear you cannot, sir.

CORVINO

4Is’t not his death?

MOSCA

Is’t not his death? Rather the contrary.

CORVINO

5Not his recovery?

MOSCA

Not his recovery? Yes, sir.

CORVINO

Not his recovery? Yes, sir. I am cursed,
6I am bewitched! My crosses meet to vex me!
7How? how? how? how?

MOSCA

8Why, sir, with Scoto’s oil;
9Corbaccio and Voltore brought of it,
10Whilst I was busy in an inner room–

CORVINO

11Death! That damned mountebank; but for the law,
12Now, I could kill the rascal. It cannot be,
13His oil should have that virtue. Have not I
14Known him a common rogue, come fidding in
15To the osteria with a tumbling whore,
16And, when he has done all his forced tricks, been glad
17Of a poor spoonful of dead wine with flies in’t?
18It cannot be. All his ingredients
19Are a sheep’s gall, a roasted bitch’s marrow,
20Some few sod earwings, pounded caterpillars,
21A little capon’s grease, and fasting spittle:
22I know them to a dram.

MOSCA

I know them to a dram. I know not, sir;
23But some on’t there they poured into his ears,
24Some in his nostrils, and recovered him,
25Applying but the fricace.

CORVINO

Applying but the fricace. Pox o’that fricace!

MOSCA

26And since, to seem the more officious
27And flattering of his health, there, they have had,
28At extreme fees, the College of Physicians
29Consulting on him, how they might restore him;
30Where one would have a cataplasm of spices,
31Another a flayed ape clapped to his breast,
32A third would have it a dog, a fourth an oil,
33With wild cats' skins: at last, they all resolved
34That, to preserve him, was no other means,
35But some young woman must be straight sought out,
36Lusty, and full of juice, to sleep by him;
37And to this service, most unhappily,
38And most unwillingly, am I now employed,
39Which here I thought to pre-acquaint you with,
40For your advice, since it concerns you most;
41Because, I would not do that thing might cross
42Your ends, on whom I have my whole dependence, sir:
43Yet, if I do it not, they may delate
44My slackness to my patron, work me out
45Of his opinion; and there all your hopes,
46Ventures, or whatsoever, are all frustrate!
47I do but tell you, sir. Besides, they are all
48Now striving, who shall first present him; therefore—
49I could entreat you, briefly conclude somewhat;
50Prevent them if you can.

CORVINO

Prevent them if you can. Death to my hopes!
51This is my villainous fortune! Best to hire
52Some common courtesan?

MOSCA

Some common courtesan? Aye, I thought on that, sir;
53But they are all so subtle, full of art—
54And age again doting and flexible,
55So as–I cannot tell–we may, perchance,
56Light on a quean may cheat us all.

CORVINO

Light on a quean may cheat us all. ‘Tis true.

MOSCA

57No, no: it must be one that has no tricks, sir,
58Some simple thing, a creature made unto it;
59Some wrench you may command. Have you no kinswoman?
60God’s so –Think, think, think, think, think, think, think, sir.
61One o’the doctors offered there his daughter.

CORVINO

62How!

MOSCA

How! Yes, Signior Lupo, the physician.

CORVINO

63His daughter?

MOSCA

His daughter? And a virgin, sir. Why, alas,
64He knows the state of’s body, what it is,
65That naught can warm his blood, sir, but a fever;
66Nor any incantation raise his spirit:
67A long forgetfulness hath seized that part.
68Besides, sir, who shall know it? Some one or two–

CORVINO

69I pray thee give me leave. [Walks aside.] If any man
70But I had has this luck–The thing in itself,
71I know, is nothing–Wherefore should not I
72As well command my blood and my affections
73As this dull doctor? In the point of honour
74The cases are all one, of wife and daughter.

MOSCA

75I hear him coming.aparte

CORVINO

I hear him coming. She shall do’t: ‘tis done.
76‘Slight! if this doctor, who is not engaged,
77Unless it be for his counsel, which is nothing,
78Offer his daughter, what should I, that am
79So deeply in? I will prevent him— Wretch!
80Covetous wretch! Mosca, I have determined.

MOSCA

81How, sir?

CORVINO

How, sir? We’ll make all sure. The party you wot of
82Shall be mine own wife, Mosca.

MOSCA

Shall be mine own wife, Mosca. Sir, the thing
83But that I would not seem the counsel you
84I should have motioned to you, at the first:
85And make your count, you have cut all their throats.
86Why, ‘tis directly taking a possession!
87And in his next fit, we may let him go.
88‘Tis but to pull the pillow from his head,
89And he is throttled; it had been done before,
90But for your scrupulous doubts.

CORVINO

But for your scrupulous doubts. Aye, a plague on’t,
91My conscience fools my wit! Well, I’ll be brief,
92And so be thou, lest they should be before us:
93Go home, prepare him, tell him with what zeal
94And willingness I do it; swear it was
95On the first hearing, as thou may’st do, truly,
96Mine own free motion.

MOSCA

Mine own free motion. Sir, I warrant you,
97I’ll so possess him with it, that the rest
98Of his starved clients shall be banished all;
99And, only you received. But come not, sir,
100Until I send, for I have something else
101To ripen for your good- you must not know’t.

CORVINO

102But do not you forget to send now.

MOSCA

But do not you forget to send now. Fear not.

[Exit Mosca.]

ACT 2, SCENE 7

CORVINO

1Where are you, wife? my Celia! wife!
Where are you, wife? my Celia! wife! [Enter Celia.] —What, blubbering?
2Come, dry those tears. I think thou thought’st me in earnest;
3Ha! By this light I talked so but to try thee.
4Methinks the lightness of the occasion.
5Should have confirmed thee. Come, I am not jealous.

CELIA

6No!

CORVINO

No! Faith I am not, I, nor never was;
7It is a poor unprofitable humour.
8Do not I know, if women have a will,
9They’ll do ‘gainst all the watches of the world,
10And that the fiercest spies are tamed with gold?
11Tut, I am confident in thee, thou shalt see’t;
12And see I’ll give thee cause too, to believe it.
13Come kiss me. Go, and make thee ready, straight,
14In all thy best attire, thy choicest jewels,
15Put them all on, and, with them, thy best looks;
16We are invited to a solemn feast,
17At old Volpone’s, where it shall appear
18How fair I am free from jealousy or fear.

[Exeunt.]
