ACT 4

SCENE 1

[Enter Sir Politic Would-be and Peregrine.]

SIR POLITIC

1I told you, sir, it was a plot: you see
2What observation is! You mentioned me
3For some instructions: I will tell you, sir
4(Since we are met here in the height of Venice),
5Some few particulars I have set down,
6Only for this meridian, fit to be known
7Of your crude traveller, and they are these.
8I will not touch, sir, at your phrase, or clothes,
9For they are old.

PEREGRINE

For they are old. Sir, I have better.

SIR POLITIC

For they are old. Sir, I have better. Pardon,
10I meant as they are themes.

PEREGRINE

I meant as they are themes. O, sir, proceed:
11I'll slander you no more of wit, good sir.

SIR POLITIC

12First, for your garb, it must be grave and serious,
13Very reserved and locked; not tell a secret
14On any terms, not to your father, scarce
15A fable, but with caution: make sure choice
16Both of your company, and discourse; beware
17You never speak a truth –

PEREGRINE

You never speak a truth – How!

SIR POLITIC

You never speak a truth – How! Not to strangers,
18For those be they you must converse with most;
19Others I would not know, sir, but at distance,
20So as I still might be a saver in them:
21You shall have tricks else past upon you hourly.
22And then, for your religion, profess none,
23But wonder at the diversity of all:
24And, for your part, protest, were there no other
25But simply the laws o' the land, you could content you,
26Nick Machiavel, and Monsieur Bodin, both
27Were of this mind. Then must you learn the use
28And handling of your silver fork at meals,
29The metal of your glass; (these are main matters
30With your Italian;) and to know the hour
31When you must eat your melons, and your figs.

PEREGRINE

32Is that a point of state, too?

SIR POLITIC

Is that a point of state, too? Here it is:
33For your Venetian, if he see a man
34Preposterous in the least, he has him straight;
35He has: he strips him. I'll acquaint you, sir.
36I now have lived here, 'tis some fourteen months;
37Within the first week of my landing here,
38All took me for a citizen of Venice,
39I knew the forms so well –

PEREGRINE

I knew the forms so well – And nothing else.aparte

SIR POLITIC

40I had read Contarine, took me a house,
41Dealt with my Jews to furnish it with moveables –
42Well, if I could but find one man, one man
43To mine own heart, whom I durst trust, I would –

PEREGRINE

44What, what, sir?

SIR POLITIC

What, what, sir? Make him rich; make him a fortune:
45He should not think again. I would command it.

PEREGRINE

46As how?

SIR POLITIC

As how? With certain projects that I have –
47Which I may not discover.

PEREGRINE

Which I may not discover. If I hadaparte
48But one to wager with, I would lay odds now,
49He tells me instantly.

SIR POLITIC

He tells me instantly. One is, and that
50I care not greatly who knows, to serve the state
51Of Venice with red herrings for three years,
52And at a certain rate, from Rotterdam,
53Where I have correspondence. There's a letter,
54Sent me from one o' the States, and to that purpose:
55He cannot write his name, but that's his mark.

PEREGRINE

56He is a chandler?

SIR POLITIC

He is a chandler? No, a cheesemonger.
57There are some others too, with whom I treat
58About the same negotiation;
59And I will undertake it: for 'tis thus.
60I'll do't with ease, I have cast it all: Your hoy
61Carries but three men in her and a boy;
62And she shall make me three returns a year:
63So, if there come but one of three, I save;
64If two, I can defalk. But this is now,
65If my main project fail.

PEREGRINE

If my main project fail. Then you have others?

SIR POLITIC

66I should be loath to draw the subtle air
67Of such a place, without my thousand aims.
68I'll not dissemble, sir: where'er I come,
69I love to be considerative; and 'tis true,
70I have at my free hours thought upon
71Some certain goods unto the state of Venice,
72Which I do call my cautions; and, sir, which
73I mean, in hope of pension, to propound
74To the Great Council, then unto the Forty,
75So to the Ten. My means are made already –

PEREGRINE

76By whom?

SIR POLITIC

By whom? Sir, one that though his place be obscure,
77Yet he can sway, and they will hear him. He's
78A commendatore.

PEREGRINE

A commendatore. What, a common sergeant?

SIR POLITIC

79Sir, such as they are, put it in their mouths,
80What they should say, sometimes; as well as greater:
81I think I have my notes to show you –

PEREGRINE

I think I have my notes to show you – Good sir.

SIR POLITIC

82But you shall swear unto me, on your gentry,
83Not to anticipate –

PEREGRINE

Not to anticipate – I, sir!

SIR POLITIC

Not to anticipate – I, sir! Nor reveal
84A circumstance – My paper is not with me.

PEREGRINE

85O, but you can remember, sir.

SIR POLITIC

O, but you can remember, sir. My first is
86Concerning tinder-boxes. You must know
87No family is here without its box.
88Now, sir, it being so portable a thing,
89Put case, that you or I were ill affected
90Unto the state, sir; with it in our pockets
91Might not I go into the Arsenale,
92Or you, come out again, and none the wiser?

PEREGRINE

93Except yourself, sir.

SIR POLITIC

Except yourself, sir. Go to, then. I therefore
94Advertise to the state, how fit it were,
95That none but such as were known patriots,
96Sound lovers of their country, should be suffered
97To enjoy them in their houses; and even those
98Sealed at some office, and at such a bigness
99As might not lurk in pockets.

PEREGRINE

As might not lurk in pockets. Admirable!

SIR POLITIC

100My next is, how to inquire, and be resolved,
101By present demonstration, whether a ship,
102Newly arrived from Soria, or from
103Any suspected part of all the Levant,
104Be guilty of the plague: and where they use
105To lie out forty, fifty days, sometimes
106About the Lazaretto, for their trial;
107I'll save that charge and loss unto the merchant,
108And in an hour clear the doubt.

PEREGRINE

And in an hour clear the doubt. Indeed, sir!

SIR POLITIC

109Or – I will lose my labour.

PEREGRINE

Or – I will lose my labour. My faith, that's much.

SIR POLITIC

110Nay, sir, conceive me. It will cost me in onions,
111Some thirty livres –

PEREGRINE

Some thirty livres – Which is one pound sterling.

SIR POLITIC

112Beside my water-works: for this I do, sir,
113First, I bring in your ship 'twixt two brick walls;
114But those the state shall venture: on the one
115I strain me a fair tarpaulin, and in that
116I stick my onions, cut in halves: the other
117Is full of loop-holes, out at which I thrust
118The noses of my bellows; and those bellows
119I keep, with water-works, in perpetual motion,
120Which is the easiest matter of a hundred.
121Now, sir, your onion, which doth naturally
122Attract the infection, and your bellows blowing
123The air upon him, will show, instantly,
124By his changed colour, if there be contagion;
125Or else remain as fair as at the first.
126—Now it is known, 'tis nothing.

PEREGRINE

—Now it is known, 'tis nothing. You are right, sir.

SIR POLITIC

127I would I had my note.

PEREGRINE

I would I had my note. Faith, so would I:
128But you have done well for once, sir.

SIR POLITIC

But you have done well for once, sir. Were I false,
129Or would be made so, I could show you reasons
130How I could sell this state now to the Turk,
131Spite of their galleys, or their –

PEREGRINE

Spite of their galleys, or their – Pray you, sir Politic.

SIR POLITIC

132I have them not about me.

PEREGRINE

I have them not about me. That I feared:
133They are there, sir.

SIR POLITIC

They are there, sir. No, this is my diary,
134Wherein I note my actions of the day.

PEREGRINE

135Pray you, let's see, sir. What is here?
136“Notandum,
137A rat had gnawn my spur leathers; notwithstanding,
138I put on new, and did go forth: but first
139I threw three beans over the threshold. Item,
140I went and bought two toothpicks, whereof one
141I burst immediately, in a discourse
142With a Dutch merchant, 'bout ragion’ del stato.
143From him I went and paid a mocenigo
144For piecing my silk stockings; by the way
145I cheapened sprats; and at St. Mark's I urined.
146Faith, these are politic notes!

SIR POLITIC

Faith, these are politic notes! Sir, I do slip
147No action of my life, but thus I quote it.

PEREGRINE

148Believe me, it is wise!

SIR POLITIC

Believe me, it is wise! Nay, sir, read forth.

ACT 4, SCENE 2

(Enter Lady Politic Would-be, Nano, and the two Women.)

LADY POLITIC

1Where should this loose knight be, trow?
2Sure he's housed.

NANO

Sure he's housed. Why, then he's fast.

LADY POLITIC

3Aye, he plays both with me.
4I pray you stay. This heat will do more harm
5To my complexion, than his heart is worth.
6I do not care to hinder, but to take him.—
7How it comes off!
How it comes off! My master's yonder.

LADY POLITIC

How it comes off! My master's yonder. Where?
8With a young gentleman.

LADY POLITIC

With a young gentleman. That same's the party;
9In man's apparel! Pray you, sir, jog my knight:
10I will be tender to his reputation,
11However he demerit.

SIR POLITIC

However he demerit. My lady!

PEREGRINE

However he demerit. My lady! Where?

SIR POLITIC

12'Tis she indeed, sir; you shall know her. She is,
13Were she not mine, a lady of that merit,
14For fashion and behaviour; and for beauty
15I durst compare –

PEREGRINE

I durst compare – It seems you are not jealous,
16That dare commend her.

SIR POLITIC

That dare commend her. Nay, and for discourse –

PEREGRINE

17Being your wife, she cannot miss that.

SIR POLITIC

Being your wife, she cannot miss that. Madam,
18Here is a gentleman; pray you use him fairly;
19He seems a youth, but he is—

LADY POLITIC

He seems a youth, but he is— None.

SIR POLITIC

He seems a youth, but he is— None. Yes, one
20Has put his face as soon into the world –

LADY POLITIC

21You mean, as early? But to-day?

SIR POLITIC

You mean, as early? But to-day? How's this?

LADY POLITIC

22Why, in this habit, sir; you apprehend me:—
23Well, Master Would-be, this doth not become you;
24I had thought the odour, sir, of your good name
25Had been more precious to you; that you would not
26Have done this dire massacre on your honour;
27One of your gravity and rank besides!
28But knights, I see, care little for the oath
29They make to ladies; chiefly, their own ladies.

SIR POLITIC

30Now, by my spurs, the symbol of my knighthood, –

PEREGRINE

31Lord, how his brain is humbled for an oath!aparte

SIR POLITIC

32I reach you not.

LADY POLITIC

I reach you not. Right, sir, your policy
33May bear it through thus.
May bear it through thus. [To Peregrine] —Sir, a word with you.
34I would be loath to contest publicly
35With any gentlewoman, or to seem
36Forward, or violent; as The Courtier says;
37It comes too near rusticity in a lady,
38Which I would shun by all means: and however
39I may deserve from master Would-be, yet
40T' have one fair gentlewoman thus be made
41The unkind instrument to wrong another,
42And one she knows not, aye, and to perséver;
43In my poor judgment, is not warranted
44From being a solecism in our sex,
45If not in manners.

PEREGRINE

If not in manners. How is this!

SIR POLITIC

If not in manners. How is this! Sweet madam,
46Come nearer to your aim.

LADY POLITIC

Come nearer to your aim. Marry, and will, sir.
47Since you provoke me with your impudence,
48And laughter of your light land-siren here,
49Your Sporus, your hermaphrodite—.

PEREGRINE

Your Sporus, your hermaphrodite—. What's here?
50Poetic fury and historic storms!

SIR POLITIC

51The gentleman, believe it, is of worth,
52And of our nation.

LADY POLITIC

And of our nation. Aye, your Whitefriars nation.
53Come, I blush for you, Master Would-be, I:
54And am ashamed you should have no more forehead,
55Than thus to be the patron, or St. George,
56To a lewd harlot, a base fricatrice,
57A female devil, in a male outside.

SIR POLITIC

A female devil, in a male outside. Nay,
58An you be such a one, I must bid adieu
59To your delights. The case appears too liquid.

[Exit.]

LADY POLITIC

60Aye, you may carry't clear, with your state-face!—
61But for your carnival concupiscence,
62Who here is fled for liberty of conscience,
63From furious persecution of the marshal,
64Her will I disple.

PEREGRINE

Her will I disple. This is fine, i'faith!
65And do you use this often? Is this part
66Of your wit's exercise, 'gainst you have occasion?
67Madam –

LADY POLITIC

Madam – Go to, sir.

PEREGRINE

Madam – Go to, sir. Do you hear me, lady?
68Why, if your knight have set you to beg shirts,
69Or to invite me home, you might have done it
70A nearer way, by far.

LADY POLITIC

A nearer way, by far. This cannot work you
71Out of my snare.

PEREGRINE

Out of my snare. Why, am I in it, then?
72Indeed your husband told me you were fair.
73And so you are; only your nose inclines,
74That side that's next the sun, to the queen-apple.

LADY POLITIC

75This cannot be endured by any patience.

ACT 4, SCENE 3

[Enter Mosca]

MOSCA

1What is the matter, madam?

LADY POLITIC

What is the matter, madam? If the Senate
2Right not my quest in this, I will protest them
3To all the world, no aristocracy.

MOSCA

To all the world, no aristocracy. What is the injury, lady?

LADY POLITIC

To all the world, no aristocracy. What is the injury, lady? Why, the callet
4You told me of, here I have ta’en disguised.

MOSCA

5Who? this! What means your ladyship? The creature
6I mentioned to you is apprehended now,
7Before the senate; you shall see her –

LADY POLITIC

Before the senate; you shall see her – Where?

MOSCA

8I'll bring you to her. This young gentlernan,
9I saw him land this morning at the port.

LADY POLITIC

10Is't possible? How has my judgment wandered?
11Sir, I must, blushing, say to you, I have erred;
12And plead your pardon.

PEREGRINE

And plead your pardon. What, more changes yet?

LADY POLITIC

13I hope you have not the malice to remember
14A gentlewoman's passion. If you stay
15In Venice here, please you to use me, sir —

MOSCA

16Will you go, madam?

LADY POLITIC

Will you go, madam? Pray you, sir, use me; in faith,
17The more you see me, the more I shall conceive
18You have forgot our quarrel.

[Exeunt Mosca, Lady Would-be, Nano, and Waiting- women.]

PEREGRINE

You have forgot our quarrel. This is rare!
19Sir Politick Would-be? No; Sir Politick Bawd,
20To bring me thus acquainted with his wife!
21Well, wise Sir Pol, since you have practised thus
22Upon my freshmanship, I'll try your salt-head,
23What proof it is against a counter-plot.

ACT 4, SCENE 4

[Enter Voltore, Corbaccio, Corvino, and Mosca.]

VOLTORE

1Well, now you know the carriage of the business,
2Your constancy is all that is required
3Unto the safety of it.

MOSCA

Unto the safety of it. Is the lie
4Safely conveyed amongst us? is that sure?
5Knows every man his burden?

CORVINO

Knows every man his burden? Yes.

MOSCA

Knows every man his burden? Yes. Then shrink not.

CORVINO

6But knows the advocate the truth?

MOSCA

But knows the advocate the truth? O, sir,
7By no means; I devised a formal tale,
8That salved your reputation. But be valiant, sir.

CORVINO

9I fear no one but him, that this his pleading
10Should make him stand for a co-heir—

MOSCA

Should make him stand for a co-heir— Co-halter!
11Hang him; we will but use his tongue, his noise,
12As we do Croaker’s, here.

CORVINO

As we do Croaker’s, here. Aye, what shall he do?

MOSCA

13When we have done, you mean?

CORVINO

When we have done, you mean? Yes.

MOSCA

When we have done, you mean? Yes. Why, we'll think –
14Sell him for mummia; he's half dust already.
15(To Voltore.) Do you not smile, to see this buffalo,
16How he doth sport it with his head?
How he doth sport it with his head? I should,aparte
17If all were well and past.aparte
If all were well and past. (To Corbaccio) Sir, only you
18Are he that shall enjoy the crop of all,
19And these not know for whom they toil.

CORBACCIO

And these not know for whom they toil. Aye, peace.

MOSCA

20(To Corvino) But you shall eat it. Much!
(To Corvino) But you shall eat it. Much! (Then to Voltore again) —Worshipful sir,
21Mercury sit upon your thundering tongue,
22Or the French Hercules, and make your language
23As conquering as his club, to beat along,
24As with a tempest, flat, our adversaries;
25But much more yours, sir.

VOLTORE

But much more yours, sir. Here they come, have done.

MOSCA

26I have another witness, if you need, sir,
27I can produce.

VOLTORE

I can produce. Who is it?

MOSCA

I can produce. Who is it? Sir, I have her.

ACT 4, SCENE 5

(Enter 4 Avocatori, Bonario, Celia, Notario, Commendatori, and Others.)

1 AVOCATORI

1The like of this the Senate never heard of.

2 AVOCATORI

2'Twill come most strange to them when we report it.

4 AVOCATORI

3The gentlewoman has been ever held
4Of unreproved name.

3 AVOCATORI

Of unreproved name. So, the young man.

4 AVOCATORI

5The more unnatural part that of his father.

2 AVOCATORI

6More of the husband.

1 AVOCATORI

More of the husband. I not know to give
7His act a name, it is so monstrous!

4 AVOCATORI

8But the impostor, he's a thing created
9To exceed example!

1 AVOCATORI

To exceed example! And all after-times!

2 AVOCATORI

10I never heard a true voluptuary
11Described, but him.

3 AVOCATORI

Described, but him. Appear yet those were cited?

NOTARIO

12All but the old magnifico, Volpone.

1 AVOCATORI

13Why is not he here?

MOSCA

14Please your fatherhoods,
15Here is his advocate: himself's so weak,
16So feeble –

4 AVOCATORI

So feeble – What are you?

BONARIO

So feeble – What are you? His parasite,
17His knave, his pander. I beseech the court
18He may be forced to come, that your grave eyes
19May bear strong witness of his strange impostures.

VOLTORE

20Upon my faith and credit with your virtues,
21He is not able to endure the air.

2 AVOCATORI

22Bring him, however.

3 AVOCATORI

Bring him, however. We will see him.

4 AVOCATORI

Bring him, however. We will see him. Fetch him.

VOLTORE

23Your fatherhoods' fit pleasures be obeyed;
24But sure, the sight will rather move your pities,
25Than indignation. May it please the court,
26In the mean time, he may be heard in me;
27I know this place most void of prejudice,
28And therefore crave it, since we have no reason
29To fear our truth should hurt our cause.

3 AVOCATORI

To fear our truth should hurt our cause. Speak free.

VOLTORE

30Then know, most honoured fathers, I must now
31Discover to your strangely abusèd ears,
32The most prodigious and most frontless piece
33Of solid impudence, and treachery,
34That ever vicious nature yet brought forth
35To shame the state of Venice. This lewd woman,
36That wants no artificial looks or tears
37To help the visor she has now put on,
38Hath long been known a close adulteress
39To that lascivious youth there; not suspected,
40I say, but known, and taken in the act
41With him; and by this man, the easy husband,
42Pardoned; whose timeless bounty makes him now
43Stand here, the most unhappy, innocent person,
44That ever man's own goodness made accused.
45For these not knowing how to owe a gift
46Of that dear grace, but with their shame; being placed
47So above all powers of their gratitude,
48Began to hate the benefit; and, in place
49Of thanks, devise to extirpe the memory
50Of such an act: wherein I pray your fatherhoods
51To observe the malice, yea, the rage of creatures
52Discovered in their evils; and what heart
53Such take, even from their crimes:— but that anon
54Will more appear.—This gentleman, the father,
55Hearing of this foul fact, with many others,
56Which daily struck at his too tender ears,
57And grieved in nothing more than that he could not
58Preserve himself a parent, (his son's ills
59Growing to that strange flood,) at last decreed
60To disinherit him.

1 AVOCATORI

To disinherit him. These be strange turns!

2 AVOCATORI

61The young man's fame was ever fair and honest.

VOLTORE

62So much more full of danger is his vice,
63That can beguile so under shade of virtue.
64But, as I said, my honoured sires, his father
65Having this settled purpose, by what means
66To him betrayed, we know not, and this day
67Appointed for the deed; that parricide,
68I cannot style him better, by confederacy
69Preparing this his paramour to be there,
70Entered Volpone's house, (who was the man,
71Your fatherhoods must understand, designed
72For the inheritance,) there sought his father:—
73But with what purpose sought he him, my lords?
74(I tremble to pronounce it, that a son
75Unto a father, and to such a father,
76Should have so foul, felonious intent)
77It was to murder him. When, being prevented
78By his more happy absence, what then did he?
79Not check his wicked thoughts: no, now new deeds:
80(Mischief doth never end where it begins)
81An act of horror, fathers! he dragged forth
82The agèd gentleman that had there lain bed-rid
83Three years and more, out of his innocent couch,
84Naked upon the floor, there left him; wounded
85His servant in the face: and with this strumpet
86The stale to his forged practice, who was glad
87To be so active, (I shall here desire
88Your Fatherhoods to note but my collections,
89As most remarkable) thought, at once, to stop
90His father's ends; discredit his free choice,
91In the old gentleman; redeem themselves,
92By laying infamy upon this man,
93To whom, with blushing, they should owe their lives.

1 AVOCATORI

94What proofs have you of this?

BONARIO

What proofs have you of this? Most honored fathers,
95I humbly crave there be no credit given
96To this man's mercenary tongue.

2 AVOCATORI

To this man's mercenary tongue. Forbear.

BONARIO

97His soul moves in his fee.

3 AVOCATORI

His soul moves in his fee. O, sir.

BONARIO

His soul moves in his fee. O, sir. This fellow,
98For six sols more, would plead against his maker.

1 AVOCATORI

99You do forget yourself.

VOLTORE

You do forget yourself. Nay, nay, grave fathers,
100Let him have scope: can any man imagine
101That he will spare his accuser, that would not
102Have spared his parent?

1 AVOCATORI

Have spared his parent? Well, produce your proofs.

CELIA

103I would I could forget I were a creature.

VOLTORE

104Signior Corbaccio!

4 AVOCATORI

Signior Corbaccio! What is he?

VOLTORE

Signior Corbaccio! What is he? The father.

2 AVOCATORI

105Has he had an oath?

NOTARIO

Has he had an oath? Yes.

CORBACCIO

Has he had an oath? Yes. What must I do now?

NOTARIO

106Your testimony's craved.

CORBACCIO

Your testimony's craved. Speak to the knave?
107I'll have my mouth first stopped with earth; my heart
108Abhors his knowledge: I disclaim in him.

1 AVOCATORI

109But for what cause?

CORBACCIO

But for what cause? The mere portent of nature!
110He is an utter stranger to my loins.

BONARIO

111Have they made you to this?

CORBACCIO

Have they made you to this? I will not hear thee,
112Monster of men, swine, goat, wolf, parricide!
113Speak not, thou viper.

BONARIO

Speak not, thou viper. Sir, I will sit down,
114And rather wish my innocence should suffer,
115Than I resist the authority of a father.

VOLTORE

116Signior Corvino!

2 AVOCATORI

Signior Corvino! This is strange.

1 AVOCATORI

Signior Corvino! This is strange. Who's this?

NOTARIO

117The husband.

4 AVOCATORI

The husband. Is he sworn?

NOTARIO

The husband. Is he sworn? He is.

3 AVOCATORI

The husband. Is he sworn? He is. Speak, then.

CORVINO

118This woman, please your fatherhoods, is a whore,
119Of most hot exercise, more than a partridge,
120Upon record –

1 AVOCATORI

Upon record – No more.

CORVINO

Upon record – No more. Neighs like a jennet.

NOTARIO

121Preserve the honour of the court.

CORVINO

Preserve the honour of the court. I shall,
122And modesty of your most reverend ears.
123And yet I hope that I may say, these eyes
124Have seen her glued unto that piece of cedar,
125That fine well-timbered gallant; and that here
126The letters may be read, thorough the horn,
127That makes the story perfect.

MOSCA

That makes the story perfect. Excellent! sir.

CORVINO

128There is no shame in this now, is there?

MOSCA

There is no shame in this now, is there? None.

CORVINO

129Or if I said, I hoped that she were onward
130To her damnation, if there be a hell
131Greater than whore and woman; a good Christian
132May make the doubt.

3 AVOCATORI

May make the doubt. His grief hath made him frantic.

1 AVOCATORI

133Remove him hence.

2 AVOCATORI

Remove him hence. Look to the woman.

CORVINO

Remove him hence. Look to the woman. Rare!
134Prettily feigned, again!

4 AVOCATORI

Prettily feigned, again! Stand from about her.

1 AVOCATORI

135Give her the air.

3 AVOCATORI

Give her the air. [to Mosca] What can you say?

MOSCA

Give her the air. [to Mosca] What can you say? My wound,
136May it please your wisdoms, speaks for me, received
137In aid of my good patron, when he missed
138His sought-for father, when that well-taught dame
139Had her cue given her, to cry out, “a rape!”

BONARIO

140O, most laid impudence! Fathers—

3 AVOCATORI

O, most laid impudence! Fathers— Sir, be silent;
141You had your hearing free, so must they theirs.

2 AVOCATORI

142I do begin to doubt the imposture here.

4 AVOCATORI

143This woman has too many moods.

VOLTORE

This woman has too many moods. Grave fathers,
144She is a creature of a most professed
145And prostituted lewdness.

CORVINO

And prostituted lewdness. Most impetuous,
146Unsatisfied, grave fathers!

VOLTORE

Unsatisfied, grave fathers! May her feignings
147Not take your wisdoms, but this day she baited
148A stranger, a grave knight, with her loose eyes,
149And more lascivious kisses. This man saw them
150Together on the water, in a gondola.

MOSCA

151Here is the lady herself, that saw them too;
152Without; who then had in the open streets
153Pursued them, but for saving her knight's honor.

1 AVOCATORI

154Produce that lady.

2 AVOCATORI

Produce that lady. Let her come.

[Exit Mosca.]

4 AVOCATORI

Produce that lady. Let her come. These things,
155They strike with wonder!

3 AVOCATORI

They strike with wonder! I am turned a stone!

ACT 4, SCENE 6

(Enter Mosca and Lady Would-Be.)

MOSCA.

1Be resolute, madam.

LADY WOULD-BE

Be resolute, madam. Aye, this same is she.
2Out, thou chameleon harlot! Now thine eyes
3Vie tears with the hyena. Dar'st thou look
4Upon my wronged face?– I cry your pardons.
5I fear I have forgettingly transgressed
6Against the dignity of the court –

2 AVOCATORI

Against the dignity of the court – No, madam.

LADY WOULD-BE

7And been exorbitant –

2 AVOCATORI

And been exorbitant – You have not, lady.

4 AVOCATORI

8These proofs are strong.

LADY WOULD-BE

These proofs are strong. Surely, I had no purpose
9To scandalize your honours, or my sex's.

3 AVOCATORI

10We do believe it.

LADY WOULD-BE

We do believe it. Surely, you may believe it.

2 AVOCATORI

11Madam, we do.

LADY WOULD-BE

Madam, we do. Indeed you may. My breeding
12Is not so coarse –

4 AVOCATORI

Is not so coarse – We know it.

LADY WOULD-BE

Is not so coarse – We know it. To offend
13With pertinacy –

3 AVOCATORI

With pertinacy – Lady –

LADY WOULD-BE

With pertinacy – Lady – Such a presence!
14No, surely.

1 AVOCATORI

No, surely. We well think it.

LADY WOULD-BE

No, surely. We well think it. You may think it.

1 AVOCATORI

15Let her o'ercome. What witnesses have you
16To make good your report?

BONARIO

To make good your report? Our consciences.

CELIA

17And heaven, that never fails the innocent.

4 AVOCATORI

18These are no testimonies.

BONARIO

These are no testimonies. Not in your courts,
19Where multitude and clamour overcomes.

1 AVOCATORI

20Nay, then you do wax insolent.

(Volpone is brought in, as impotent.)

VOLTORE

Nay, then you do wax insolent. Here, here,
21The testimony comes, that will convince,
22And put to utter dumbness their bold tongues:
23See here, grave fathers, here's the ravisher,
24The rider on men's wives, the great impostor,
25The grand voluptuary! Do you not think
26These limbs should affect venery? Or these eyes
27Covet a concubine? Pray you mark these hands;
28Are they not fit to stroke a lady's breasts?–
29Perhaps he doth dissemble!

BONARIO

Perhaps he doth dissemble! So he does.

VOLTORE

30Would you have him tortured?

BONARIO

Would you have him tortured? I would have him proved.

VOLTORE

31Best try him then with goads, or burning irons;
32Put him to the strappado: I have heard
33The rack hath cured the gout; faith, give it him,
34And help him of a malady; be courteous.
35I'll undertake, before these honored fathers,
36He shall have yet as many left diseases
37As she has known adulterers, or thou strumpets.
38O, my most equal hearers, if these deeds,
39Acts of this bold and most exorbitant strain,
40May pass with sufferance, what one citizen
41But owes the forfeit of his life, yea, fame,
42To him that dares traduce him? Which of you
43Are safe, my honoured fathers? I would ask,
44With leave of your grave fatherhoods, if their plot
45Have any face or colour like to truth?
46Or if, unto the dullest nostril here,
47It smell not rank, and most abhorrèd slander?
48I crave your care of this good gentleman,
49Whose life is much endangered by their fable;
50And as for them, I will conclude with this,
51That vicious persons, when they are hot and fleshed
52In impious acts, their constancy abounds:
53Damned deeds are done with greatest confidence.

1 AVOCATORI

54Take them to custody, and sever them.

2 AVOCATORI

55'Tis pity two such prodigies should live.

1 AVOCATORI

56Let the old gentleman be returned with care.
57I'm sorry your credulity hath wronged him.

[Exeunt Officers with Volpone.]

4 AVOCATORI

58These are two creatures!

3 AVOCATORI

These are two creatures! I’ve an earthquake in me.

2 AVOCATORI

59Their shame, even in their cradles, fled their faces.

4 AVOCATORI

60You have done a worthy service to the state, sir,
61In their discovery.

1 AVOCATORI

In their discovery. You shall hear, ere night,
62What punishment the court decrees upon them.

VOLTORE

63We thank your fatherhoods.

[Exeunt Avocatori, Notario, Officers with Bonario and Celia.]

We thank your fatherhoods. How like you it?

MOSCA

We thank your fatherhoods. How like you it? Rare.
64I'd have your tongue, sir, tipped with gold for this;
65I'd have you be the heir to the whole city;
66The earth I'd have want men, ere you want living:
67They're bound to erect your statue in St. Mark's.
68Signior Corvino, I would have you go
69And show yourself, that you have conquered.

CORVINO

And show yourself, that you have conquered. Yes.

MOSCA

70It was much better that you should profess
71Yourself a cuckold thus, than that the other
72Should have been proved.

CORVINO

Should have been proved. Nay, I considered that:
73Now it is her fault.

MOSCA

Now it is her fault. Then it had been yours.

CORVINO

74True; I do doubt this advocate still.

MOSCA

True; I do doubt this advocate still. I'faith
75You need not; I dare ease you of that care.

CORVINO

76I trust thee, Mosca.

MOSCA

I trust thee, Mosca. As your own soul, sir.

[Exit CORVINO.]

CORBACCIO

I trust thee, Mosca. As your own soul, sir. Mosca!

MOSCA

77Now for your business, sir.

CORBACCIO

Now for your business, sir. How! Have you business?

MOSCA

78Yes, yours, sir.

CORBACCIO

Yes, yours, sir. O, none else?

MOSCA

Yes, yours, sir. O, none else? None else, not I.

CORBACCIO

79Be careful, then.

MOSCA

Be careful, then. Rest you with both your eyes, sir.

CORBACCIO

80Dispatch it.

MOSCA

Dispatch it. Instantly.

CORBACCIO

Dispatch it. Instantly. And look that all,
81Whatever, be put in, jewels, plate, moneys.
82Household stuff, bedding, curtains.

MOSCA

Household stuff, bedding, curtains. Curtain-rings, sir.
83Only the advocate's fee must be deducted.

CORBACCIO

84I'll pay him now; you'll be too prodigal.

MOSCA

85Sir, I must tender it.

CORBACCIO

Sir, I must tender it. Two cecchines is well?

MOSCA

86No, six, sir.

CORBACCIO

No, six, sir. 'Tis too much.

MOSCA

No, six, sir. 'Tis too much. He talked a great while;
87You must consider that, sir.

CORBACCIO

You must consider that, sir. Well, there's three –

MOSCA

88I'll give it him.

CORBACCIO

I'll give it him. Do so, and there's for thee.

[Exit Corbaccio.]

MOSCA

89Bountiful bones! What horrid strange offence
90Did he commit 'gainst nature, in his youth,
91Worthy this age? [To Voltore.] You see, sir, how I work
92Unto your ends: take you no notice.

VOLTORE

Unto your ends: take you no notice. No,
93I'll leave you.

[Exit Voltore.]

MOSCA

I'll leave you. All is yours, the devil and all:
94Good advocate— Madam, I'll bring you home.

LADY WOULD-BE

95No, I'll go see your patron.

MOSCA

No, I'll go see your patron. That you shall not:
96I'll tell you why. My purpose is to urge
97My patron to reform his will; and for
98The zeal you have shown to-day, whereas before
99You were but third or fourth, you shall be now
100Put in the first: which would appear as begged
101If you were present. Therefore –

LADY WOULD-BE

If you were present. Therefore – You shall sway me.

[Exeunt.]
