ACT 3

SCENE 1

[Enter Mosca.]

MOSCA

1I fear, I shall begin to grow in love
2With my dear self, and most prosperous parts,
3They do so spring and burgeon; I can feel
4A whimsy in my blood: I know not how,
5Success hath made me wanton. I could skip
6Out of my skin, now, like a subtle snake,
7I am so limber. O! your parasite
8Is a most precious thing, dropt from above,
9Not bred ‘mongst clods and clotpoles, here on earth.
10I muse, the mystery was not made a science,
11It is so liberally professed! Almost
12All the wise world is little else, in nature,
13But parasites or sub-parasites. And yet,
14I mean not those that have your bare town-art,
15To know who’s fit to feed them; have no house,
16No family, no care, and therefore mould
17Tales for men’s ears, to bait that sense; or get
18Kitchen-invention, and some stale receipts
19To please the belly, and the groin; nor those,
20With their court dog-tricks, that can fawn and fleer,
21Make their revenue out of legs and faces,
22Echo my lord, and lick away a moth:
23But your fine elegant rascal, that can rise,
24And stoop, almost together, like an arrow,
25Shoot through the air as nimbly as a star;
26Turn short as doth a swallow, and be here
27And there, and here, and yonder, all at once;
28Present to any humour, all occasion;
29And change a visor, swifter than a thought!
30This is the creature had the art born with him;
31Toils not to learn it, but doth practice it
32Out of most excellent nature: and such sparks
33Are the true parasites, others but their zanies.

ACT 3, SCENE 2

[Enter Bonario.]

MOSCA

1Who’s this? Bonario, old Corbaccio’s son?
2The person I was bound to seek. –Fair sir,
3You are happily met.

BONARIO

You are happily met. That cannot be by thee.

MOSCA

4Why, sir?

BONARIO

Why, sir? Nay, pray thee, know thy way, and
5leave me:
leave me: I would be loath to interchange discourse
6With such a mate as thou art.

MOSCA

With such a mate as thou art. Courteous sir,
7Scorn not my poverty.

BONARIO

Scorn not my poverty. Not I, by heaven;
8But thou shalt give me leave to hate thy baseness.

MOSCA

9Baseness!

BONARIO

Baseness! Aye; answer me, is not thy sloth
10Sufficient argument? Thy flattery?
11Thy means of feeding?

MOSCA

Thy means of feeding? Heaven be good to me!
12These imputations are too common, sir,
13And easily stuck on virtue when she’s poor.
14You are unequal to me, and however
15Your sentence may be righteous, yet you are not,
16That, ere you know me, thus proceed in censure;
17St. Mark bear witness ‘gainst you, ‘tis inhuman.

[Weeps.]

BONARIO

18What! does he weep? the sign is soft and good:
19I do repent me that I was so harsh.

MOSCA

20‘Tis true, that, swayed by strong necessity,
21I am enforced to eat my careful bread
22With too much obsequy; ‘tis true, beside,
23That I am fain to spin mine own poor raiment
24Out of my mere observance, being not born
25To a free fortune: but that I have done
26Base offices in rending friends asunder,
27Dividing families, betraying counsels,
28Whispering false lies, or mining men with praises,
29Trained their credulity with perjuries,
30Corrupted chastity, or am in love
31With mine own tender ease, but would not rather
32Prove the most rugged, and laborious course,
33That might redeem my present estimation,
34Let me here perish in all hope of goodness.

BONARIO

35This cannot be a personated passion!aparte
36I was to blame, so to mistake thy nature;
37Prithee, forgive me: and speak out thy business.

MOSCA

38Sir, it concerns you; and though I may seem
39At first to make a main offense in manners,
40And in my gratitude unto my master;
41Yet, for the pure love, which I bear all right
42And hatred of the wrong, I must reveal it.
43This very hour your father is in purpose
44To disinherit you–

BONARIO

To disinherit you– How!

MOSCA

To disinherit you– How! And thrust you forth,
45As a mere stranger to his blood. ‘tis true, sir.
46The work no way engageth me, but, as
47I claim an interest in the general state
48Of goodness and true virtue, which I hear
49To abound in you: and for which mere respect,
50Without a second aim, sir, I have done it.

BONARIO

51This tale hath lost thee much of the late trust
52Thou hadst with me; it is impossible:
53I know not how to lend it any thought,
54My father should be so unnatural.

MOSCA

55It is a confidence that well becomes,
56Your piety; and formed, no doubt, it is
57From your own simple innocence, which makes
58Your wrong more monstrous and abhorred. But, sir,
59I now will tell you more. This very minute
60It is or will be doing; and if you
61Shall be but pleased to go with me, I’ll bring you,
62I dare not say where you shall see, but where
63Your ear shall be a witness of the deed;
64Hear yourself written bastard, and professed
65The common issue of the earth.

BONARIO

The common issue of the earth. I’m mazed!

MOSCA

66Sir, if I do it not, draw your just sword
67And score your vengeance on my front and face:
68Mark me your villain: you have too much wrong,
69And I do suffer for you, sir. My heart
70Weeps blood in anguish–

BONARIO

Weeps blood in anguish– Lead. I follow thee.

[Exeunt.]

ACT 3, SCENE 3

(Enter Volpone, Nano, Androgyno, and Castrone)

VOLPONE

1Mosca stays long, methinks. Bring forth your sports,
2And help to make the wretched time more sweet.

NANO

3Dwarf, fool, and eunuch, well met here we be.
4A question it were now, whether of us three,
5Being all the known delicates of a rich man,
6In pleasing him, claim the precedency can?

CASTRONE

7I claim for myself.

ANDROGYNO

I claim for myself. And so doth the fool.

NANO

8‘Tis foolish indeed; let me set you both to school.
9First for your dwarf: he’s little and witty,
10And every thing, as it is little, is pretty;
11Else why do men say to a creature of my shape,
12So soon as they see him. It’s a pretty little ape?
13And why a pretty ape? But for pleasing imitation
14Of greater men’s actions in a ridiculous fashion?
15Beside, this feat body of mine doth not crave
16Half the meat, drink, and cloth, one of your bulks will have.
17Admit your fool’s face be the mother of laughter,
18Yet, for his brain, it must always come after:
19And though that do feed him, it’s a pitiful case,
20His body is beholding to such a bad face.

(One knocks).

VOLPONE

21Who’s there? My couch; away! look! Nano, see:
22Give me my caps, first–go, inquire.
Give me my caps, first–go, inquire. [Exeunt Nano, Androgyno, and Castrone.] Now, Cupid
23Send it be Mosca, and with fair return!

NANO

24(Within.) It is the beauteous madam–

VOLPONE

(Within.) It is the beauteous madam– Would-be–is it?

NANO

25The same.

VOLPONE

ACT 3 SCENE 5

(Enter Mosca)

MOSCA

1God save you, madam!

LADY POLITIC

God save you, madam! Good sir.

VOLPONE

God save you, madam! Good sir. Mosca? Welcome,
2Welcome to my redemption.

MOSCA

Welcome to my redemption. Why, sir?

VOLPONE

Welcome to my redemption. Why, sir? Oh,
3Rid me of this my torture, quickly, there;
4My madam, with the everlasting voice:
5The bells, in time of pestilence, ne'er made
6Like noise, or were in that perpetual motion!
7The cock-pit comes not near it. All my house,
8But now, steamed like a bath with her thick breath,
9A lawyer could not have been heard; nor scarce
10Another woman, such a hail of words
11She has let fall. For hell's sake, rid her hence.

MOSCA

12Has she presented?

VOLPONE

Has she presented? Oh, I do not care;
13I'll take her absence, upon any price,
14With any loss.

MOSCA

With any loss. Madam –

LADY POLITIC

With any loss. Madam – I have brought your patron
15A toy, a cap here, of mine own work.

LADY POLITIC

Where you would little think it. – Where?

18Where yet, if you make haste, you may apprehend
19Rowing upon the water in a gondola

LADY POLITIC

21Is't true?

113This once. No! Not! I shall remember this.
114Will you disgrace me thus? Do you thirst my undoing?

MOSCA

115Nay, gentle lady, be advised.

CORVINO

Nay, gentle lady, be advised. No, no.
116She has watched her time. God’s precious, this is scurvy,
117'Tis very scurvy; and you are—

MOSCA

'Tis very scurvy; and you are— Nay, good, sir.

CORVINO

118An arrant locust, by heaven, a locust!
119Whore, crocodile, that hast thy tears prepared,
120Expecting how thou'lt bid ‘em flow—

MOSCA

Expecting how thou'lt bid ‘em flow— Nay, pray you, sir!
121She will consider.

CELIA

She will consider. Would my life would serve
122To satisfy—

CORVINO

To satisfy— S'death! if she would but speak to him,
123And save my reputation, it were somewhat;
124But spitefully to affect my utter ruin!

MOSCA

125Aye, now you have put your fortune in her hands.
126Why i'faith, it is her modesty, I must quit her.
127If you were absent, she would be more coming;
128I know it: and dare undertake for her.
129What woman can before her husband? Pray you,
130Let us depart, and leave her here.

CORVINO

Let us depart, and leave her here. Sweet Celia,
131Thou may’st redeem all, yet; I'll say no more:
132If not, esteem yourself as lost.
If not, esteem yourself as lost. (Celia starts to leave.) Nay, stay there.

[Exeunt Corvino and Mosca.]

CELIA

133O God, and his good angels! whither, whither,
134Is shame fled human breasts? that with such ease,
135Men dare put off your honours, and their own?
136Is that, which ever was a cause of life,
137Now placed beneath the basest circumstance,
138And modesty an exile made, for money?

VOLPONE

139Aye, in Corvino, and such earth-fed minds,
140(He leaps off from his couch.) That never tasted the true heaven of love.
141Assure thee, Celia, he that would sell thee,
142Only for hope of gain, and that uncertain,
143He would have sold his part of Paradise
144For ready money, had he met a copeman.
145Why art thou mazed to see me thus revived?
146Rather applaud thy beauty's miracle;
147'Tis thy great work: that hath, not now alone,
148But sundry times raised me, in several shapes,
149And, but this morning, like a mountebank,
150To see thee at thy window: aye, before
151I would have left my practice, for thy love,
152In varying figures, I would have contended
153With the blue Proteus, or the horned flood.
154Now art thou welcome.

CELIA

Now art thou welcome. Sir!

VOLPONE

Now art thou welcome. Sir! Nay, fly me not.
155Nor let thy false imagination
156That I was bed-rid, make thee think I am so:
157Thou shalt not find it. I am, now, as fresh,
158As hot, as high, and in as jovial plight,
159As when, in that so celebrated scene,
160At recitation of our comedy,
161For entertainment of the great Valois,
162I acted young Antinous, and attracted
163The eyes and ears of all the ladies present,
164To admire each graceful gesture, note, and footing.

(Song.)

165Come, my Celia, let us prove,
166While we can, the sports of love,
167Time will not be ours for ever,
168He, at length, our good will sever;
169Spend not then his gifts in vain;
170Suns, that set, may rise again;
171But if once we lose this light,
172'Tis with us perpetual night.
173Why should we defer our joys?
174Fame and rumour are but toys.
175Cannot we delude the eyes
176Of a few poor household spies?
177Or his easier ears beguile,
178Thus removed by our wile?—
179'Tis no sin love's fruits to steal:
180But the sweet thefts to reveal;
181To be taken, to be seen,
182These have crimes accounted been.

CELIA

183Some serene blast me, or dire lightning strike
184This my offending face!

VOLPONE

This my offending face! Why droops my Celia?
185Thou hast, in place of a base husband, found
186A worthy lover: use thy fortune well,
187With secrecy and pleasure. See, behold,
188What thou art queen of; not in expectation,
189As I feed others: but possessed and crowned.
190See, here, a rope of pearl; and each, more orient
191Than that the brave Egyptian queen caroused;
192Dissolve and drink them. See, a carbuncle,
193May put out both the eyes of our St. Mark;
194A diamond, would have bought Lollia Paulina,
195When she came in like star-light, hid with jewels,
196That were the spoils of provinces; take these,
197And wear, and lose them: yet remains an ear-ring
198To purchase them again, and this whole state.
199A gem but worth a private patrimony,
200Is nothing: we will eat such at a meal.
201The heads of parrots, tongues of nightingales,
202The brains of peacocks, and of ostriches,
203Shall be our food: and, could we get the phoenix,
204Though nature lost her kind, she were our dish.

CELIA

205Good sir, these things might move a mind affected
206With such delights; but I, whose innocence
207Is all I can think wealthy, or worth the enjoying,
208And which, once lost, I have naught to lose beyond it,
209Cannot be taken with these sensual baits:
210If you have conscience—

VOLPONE

If you have conscience— 'Tis the beggar's virtue;
211If thou hast wisdom, hear me, Celia.
212Thy baths shall be the juice of July-flowers,
213Spirit of roses, and of violets,
214The milk of unicorns, and panthers' breath
215Gathered in bags, and mixed with Cretan wines.
216Our drink shall be prepared gold and amber,
217Which we will take, until my roof whirl round
218With the vertigo: and my dwarf shall dance,
219My eunuch sing, my fool make up the antic,
220Whilst we, in changed shapes, act Ovid's tales,
221Thou, like Europa now, and I like Jove,
222Then I like Mars, and thou like Erycine:
223So, of the rest, till we have quite run through,
224And wearied all the fables of the gods.
225Then will I have thee in more modern forms,
226Attired like some sprightly dame of France,
227Brave Tuscan lady, or proud Spanish beauty;
228Sometimes, unto the Persian Sophy's wife;
229Or the Grand Signior's mistress; and, for change,
230To one of our most artful courtesans,
231Or some quick Negro, or cold Russian;
232And I will meet thee in as many shapes:
233Where we may so transfuse our wandering souls
234Out at our lips, and score up sums of pleasures,
235[Sings.] That the curious shall not know
236How to tell them as they flow;
237And the envious, when they find
238What their number is, be pined.

CELIA

239If you have ears that will be pierced—or eyes
240That can be opened—a heart that may be touched—
241Or any part that yet sounds man about you—
242If you have touch of holy saints—or heaven—
243Do me the grace to let me ‘scape—if not,
244Be bountiful and kill me. You do know,
245I am a creature, hither ill betrayed,
246By one, whose shame I would forget it were.—
247If you will deign me neither of these graces,
248Yet feed your wrath, sir, rather than your lust —
249(It is a vice comes nearer manliness)—
250And punish that unhappy crime of nature,
251Which you miscall my beauty— flay my face,
252Or poison it with ointments, for seducing
253Your blood to this rebellion—Rub these hands,
254With what may cause an eating leprosy,
255E'en to my bones and marrow— any thing,
256That may disfavour me, save in my honour—
257And I will kneel to you, pray for you, pay down
258A thousand hourly vows, sir, for your health
259Report and think you virtuous—

VOLPONE

Report and think you virtuous— Think me cold,
260Frozen and impotent, and so report me?
261That I had Nestor's hernia, thou wouldst think.
262I do degenerate, and abuse my nation,
263To play with opportunity thus long;
264I should have done the act, and then have parleyed.
265Yield, or I'll force thee.

CELIA

Yield, or I'll force thee. O! just God!

VOLPONE

Yield, or I'll force thee. O! just God! In vain –

BONARIO

266(He leaps out from where Mosca had placed him.) Forbear, foul ravisher, libidinous swine!
267Free the forced lady, or thou diest, impostor.
268But that I'm loath to snatch thy punishment
269Out of the hand of justice, thou shouldst, yet,
270Be made the timely sacrifice of vengeance,
271Before this altar, and this dross, thy idol.—
272Lady, let's quit the place., it is the den
273Of villainy; fear naught, you have a guard:
274And he, ere long, shall meet his just reward.

[Exeunt Bonario and Celia.]

VOLPONE

275Fall on me, roof, and bury me in ruin!
276Become my grave, that wert my shelter! O!
277I am unmasked, unspirited, undone,
278Betrayed to beggary, to infamy—

ACT 3, SCENE 8

[Enter Mosca, bleeding.]

MOSCA

1Where shall I run, most wretched shame of men,
2To beat out my unlucky brains?

VOLPONE

To beat out my unlucky brains? Here, here.
3What! dost thou bleed?

MOSCA

What! dost thou bleed? O that his well-driven sword
4Had been so courteous to have cleft me down
5Unto the navel, ere I lived to see
6My life, my hopes, my spirits, my patron, all
7Thus desperately engaged, by my error!

VOLPONE

8Woe on thy fortune!

MOSCA

Woe on thy fortune! And my follies, sir.

VOLPONE

9Thou hast made me miserable.

MOSCA

Thou hast made me miserable. And myself, sir.
10Who would have thought he would have hearkened so?

VOLPONE

11What shall we do?

MOSCA

What shall we do? I know not; if my heart
12Could expiate the mischance, I'd pluck it out.
13Will you be pleased to hang me, or cut my throat?
14And I'll requite you, sir. Let's die like Romans,
15Since we have lived like Grecians.

[They knock without.]

VOLPONE

Since we have lived like Grecians. Hark! who's there?
16I hear some footing: officers, the Saffi,
17Come to apprehend us! I do feel the brand
18Hissing already at my forehead; now
19Mine ears are boring.

MOSCA

Mine ears are boring. To your couch, sir, you
20[Volpone lies down.]{tf}Guilty men Make that place good, however.
21Suspect what they deserve still.
Suspect what they deserve still. [Opens door.] Signior Corbaccio!

ACT 3, SCENE 9

[Enter Corbaccio .]

CORBACCIO

1Why, how now, Mosca!

[Enter Voltore, behind]

MOSCA

Why, how now, Mosca! Oh, undone, amazed, sir.
2Your son, I know not by what accident,
3Acquainted with your purpose to my patron,
4Touching your will, and making him your heir,
5Entered our house with violence, his sword drawn,
6Sought for you, called you wretch, unnatural,
7Vowed he would kill you.

CORBACCIO

Vowed he would kill you. Me!

MOSCA

Vowed he would kill you. Me! Yes, and my patron.

CORBACCIO

8This act shall disinherit him indeed;
9Here is the will.

MOSCA

Here is the will. 'Tis well, sir.

CORBACCIO

Here is the will. 'Tis well, sir. Right and well:
10Be you as careful now for me.

MOSCA

Be you as careful now for me. My life, sir,

CORBACCIO

12How does he? Will he die shortly,

MOSCA

think'st thou? I fear

CORBACCIO

MOSCA

He'll outlast May. To-day? No, last our May, sir.

CORBACCIO

15Could’st thou not give him a dram?

MOSCA

Could’st thou not give him a dram? O, by no means, sir.

CORBACCIO

16Nay, I'll not bid you.

VOLTORE

MOSCA

17How! Signior Voltore! Did he hear me?aparte

VOLTORE

18Who's that? O, sir, most timely welcome—

VOLTORE

Who's that? O, sir, most timely welcome— Scarce,
19To the discovery of your tricks, I fear.
20You are his, only? And mine also, are you not?

MOSCA

21Who? I, sir?

VOLTORE

Who? I, sir? You, sir. What device is this
22About a will?

MOSCA

About a will? A plot for you, sir.

VOLTORE

About a will? A plot for you, sir. Come,
23Put not your foists upon me; I shall scent them.

MOSCA

24Did you not hear it?

VOLTORE

Did you not hear it? Yes, I hear Corbaccio
25Hath made your patron there his heir.

MOSCA

Hath made your patron there his heir. 'Tis true,
26By my device, drawn to it by my plot,
27With hope—

VOLTORE

With hope— Your patron should reciprocate?
28And you have promised?

MOSCA

And you have promised? For your good I did, sir.
29Nay, more, I told his son, brought, hid him here,
30Where he might hear his father pass the deed:
31Being persuaded to it by this thought, sir,
32That the unnaturalness, first, of the act,
33And then his father's oft disclaiming in him
34(Which I did mean t' help on) would sure enrage him
35To do some violence upon his parent,
36On which the law should take sufficient hold,
37And you be stated in a double hope:
38Truth be my comfort, and my conscience,
39My only aim was to dig you a fortune
40Out of these two old rotten sepulchres –

VOLTORE

41I cry thee mercy, Mosca.

MOSCA

I cry thee mercy, Mosca. Worth your patience,
42And your great merit, sir. And see the change!

VOLTORE

43Why, what success?

MOSCA

Why, what success? Most hapless! You must help, sir.
44Whilst we expected the old raven, in comes
45Corvino's wife, sent hither by her husband –

VOLTORE

46What, with a present?

MOSCA

What, with a present? No, sir, on visitation –
47I'll tell you how anon — and staying long,
48The youth he grows impatient, rushes forth,
49Seizeth the lady, wounds me, makes her swear –
50Or he would murder her, that was his vow –
51To affirm my patron to have done her rape,
52Which how unlike it is, you see! and hence,
53With that pretext he's gone, to accuse his father,
54Defame my patron, defeat you –

VOLTORE

Defame my patron, defeat you – Where is her husband?
55Let him be sent for straight.

MOSCA

Let him be sent for straight. Sir, I'll go fetch him.

VOLTORE

56Bring him to the Scrutineo.

MOSCA

Bring him to the Scrutineo. Sir, I will.

VOLTORE

57This must be stopped.

MOSCA

This must be stopped. O you do nobly, sir.
58Alas, 'twas laboured all, sir, for your good;
59Nor was there want of counsel in the plot:
60But fortune can, at any time, o’erthrow
61The projects of a hundred learned clerks, sir.

CORBACCIO

62[Listening] What's that?

VOLTORE

[Listening] What's that? Will't please you, sir, to go along?

[Exeunt Corbaccio and Voltore.]

MOSCA

63Patron, go in, and pray for our success.

VOLTORE

64Need makes devotion: heaven your labour bless!

[Exeunt]
