ACT 1

SCENE 1

(Enter Volpone and Mosca.)

VOLPONE

1Good morning to the day; and next, my gold! –
2Open the shrine, that I may see my saint.
3[Mosca reveals the treasure.] Hail the world's soul, and mine! more glad than is
4The teeming earth to see the longed-for sun
5Peep through the horns of the celestial Ram,
6Am I, to view thy splendour darkening his;
7That lying here, amongst my other hoards,
8Show'st like a flame by night, or like the day
9Struck out of chaos, when all darkness fled
10Unto the center. O thou son of Sol,
11But brighter than thy father, let me kiss,
12With adoration, thee, and every relic
13Of sacred treasure in this blessed room.
14Well did wise poets, by thy glorious name,
15Title that age which they would have the best;
16Thou being the best of things, and far transcending
17All style of joy, in children, parents, friends,
18Or any other waking dream on earth:
19Thy looks when they to Venus did ascribe,
20They should have given her twenty thousand Cupids;
21Such are thy beauties and our loves! Dear saint,
22Riches, the dumb god, that giv'st all men tongues,
23Thou canst do naught, and yet mak'st men do all things;
24The price of souls; even hell, with thee to boot,
25Is made worth heaven. Thou art virtue, fame,
26Honour, and all things else. Who can get thee,
27He shall be noble valiant, honest, wise –

MOSCA

28And what he will, sir. Riches are in fortune
29A greater good than wisdom is in nature.

VOLPONE

30True, my beloved Mosca. Yet I glory
31More in the cunning purchase of my wealth,
32Than in the glad possession, since I gain
33No common way; I use no trade, no venture;
34I wound no earth with plough-shares, fat no beasts,
35To feed the shambles; have no mills for iron,
36Oil, corn, or men, to grind them into powder;
37I blow no subtle glass; expose no ships
38To threat'nings of the furrow-faced sea;
39I turn no monies in the public bank,
40Nor usure private.

MOSCA

Nor usure private. No, sir, nor devour
41Soft prodigals. You shall have some will swallow
42A melting heir as glibly as your Dutch
43Will pills of butter, and ne'er purge for it;
44Tear forth the fathers of poor families
45Out of their beds, and coffin them alive
46In some kind clasping prison, where their bones
47May be forthcoming, when the flesh is rotten:
48But your sweet nature doth abhor these courses;
49You loathe the widow's or the orphan's tears
50Should wash your pavements, or their piteous cries
51Ring in your roofs, and beat the air for vengeance.

VOLPONE

52Right, Mosca, I do lothe it.

MOSCA

Right, Mosca, I do lothe it. And besides, sir,
53You are not like the thresher that doth stand
54With a huge flail, watching a heap of corn,
55And, hungry, dares not taste the smallest grain,
56But feeds on mallows, and such bitter herbs;
57Nor like the merchant, who hath filled his vaults
58With Romagnia, and rich Candian wines,
59Yet drinks the lees of Lombard's vinegar:
60You will lie not in straw, whilst moths and worms
61Feed on your sumptuous hangings and soft beds;
62You know the use of riches, and dare give now
63From that bright heap, to me, your poor observer,
64Or to your dwarf, or your hermaphrodite,
65Your eunuch, or what other household trifle
66Your pleasure allows maintenance –

VOLPONE

Your pleasure allows maintenance – Hold thee, Mosca,
67[Gives him money.] Take of my hand; thou strik'st on truth in all,
68And they are envious term thee parasite.
69Call forth my dwarf, my eunuch, and my fool,
70And let them make me sport.
And let them make me sport. [Exit Mosca.] What should I do
71But cocker up my genius, and live free
72To all delights my fortune calls me to?
73I have no wife, no parent, child, ally,
74To give my substance to; but whom I make
75Must be my heir: and this makes men observe me:
76This draws new clients daily to my house,
77Women and men of every sex and age,
78That bring me presents, send me plate, coin, jewels,
79With hope that when I die (which they expect
80Each greedy minute) it shall then return
81Ten-fold upon them; whilst some, covetous
82Above the rest, seek to engross me whole,
83And counter-work the one unto the other,
84Contend in gifts as they would seem in love:
85All which I suffer, playing with their hopes,
86And am content to coin them into profit,
87And look upon their kindness, and take more,
88And look on that; still bearing them in hand,
89Letting the cherry knock against their lips,
90And draw it by their mouths and back again. – How now!

ACT 1, Scene 2

(Enter Mosca with Nano, Androgyno, and Castrone.)

NANO

1Now, room for fresh gamester, who do will you to know,
2They do bring you neither play nor university show;
3And therefore do entreat you, that whatsoever they rehearse,
4May not fare a whit the worse, for the false pace of the verse.
5If you wonder at this, you will wonder more ere we pass,
6For know, here is enclosed the soul of Pythagoras,
7That juggler divine, as hereafter shall follow;
8Which soul, fast and loose, sir, came first from Apollo,
9And was breathed into Aethalides, Mercurius, his son,
10Where it had the gift to remember all that ever was done.
11From thence it fled forth, and made quick transmigration
12To goldilocked Euphorbus, who was killed in good fashion,
13At the siege of old Troy, by the cuckold of Sparta.
14Hermotimus was next (I find it in my charta)
15To whom it did pass, where no sooner it was missing
16But with one Pyrrhus of Delos it learned to go a-fishing;
17And thence did it enter the sophist of Greece.
18From Pythagore, she went into a beautiful piece,
19Hight Aspasia, the meretrix; and the next toss of her
20Was again of a whore, she became a philosopher,
21Crates the cynic, as itself doth relate it:
22Since kings, knights, and beggars, knaves, lords, and
23fools gat it,
24Besides ox and ass, camel, mule, goat, and brock,
25In all which it hath spoke, as in the cobbler’s cock.
26But I come not here to discourse of that matter,
27Or his one, two, or three, or his great oath, “By quarter!”
28His musics, his trigon, his golden thigh,
29Or his telling how elements shift, but I
30Would ask, how of late thou hast suffered translation,
31And shifted thy coat in these days of reformation.

ANDROGYNO

32Like one of the reformed, a fool, as you see,
33Counting all old doctrine heresy.

NANO

34But not on thine own forbid meats hast thou ventured?

ANDROGYNO

35On fish, when first a Carthusian I entered.

NANO

36Why, then thy dogmatical silence hath left thee?

ANDROGYNO

37Of that an obstreperous lawyer bereft me.

NANO

38O wonderful change, when sir lawyer forsook thee!
39For Pythagore's sake, what body then took thee?

ANDROGYNO

40A good dull mule.

NANO

A good dull mule. And how, by that means,
41Thou wert brought to allow of the eating of beans?

ANDROGYNO

42Yes.

NANO

Yes. But from the mule into whom didst thou pass?

ANDROGYNO

43Into a very strange beast, by some writers called an ass;
44By others, a precise, pure, illuminate brother,
45Of those devour flesh, and sometimes one another;
46And will drop you forth a libel, or a sanctified lie,
47Betwixt every spoonful of a nativity-pie.

NANO

48Now quit thee, for heaven, of that profane nation,
49And gently report thy next transmigration.

ANDROGYNO

50To the same that I am.

NANO

To the same that I am. A creature of delight,
51And, what is more than a fool, an hermaphrodite!
52Now, prithee, sweet soul, in all thy variation,
53Which body would’st thou choose, to keep up thy station?

ANDROGYNO

54Troth, this I am in: even here would I tarry.

NANO

55'Cause here the delight of each sex thou canst vary?

ANDROGYNO

56Alas, those pleasures be stale and forsaken;
57No, 'tis your fool wherewith I am so taken,
58The only one creature that I can call blessed;
59For all other forms I have proved most distressed.

NANO

60Spoke true, as thou wert in Pythagoras still.
61This learned opinion we celebrate will,
62Fellow eunuch, as behoves us, with all our wit and art,
63To dignify that whereof ourselves are so great and
64special a part.

VOLPONE

65Now, very, very pretty! Mosca, this
66Was thy invention?

MOSCA

Was thy invention? If it please my patron,
67Not else.

VOLPONE

Not else. It doth, good Mosca.

MOSCA

Not else. It doth, good Mosca. Then it was, sir.

[song]

68Fools, they are the only nation
69Worth men's envy or admiration:
70Free from care or sorrow-taking,
71Selves and others merry making:
72All they speak or do is sterling.
73Your fool he is your great man's darling,
74And your ladies’ sport and pleasure;
75Tongue and bauble are his treasure.
76E'en his face begetteth laughter,
77And he speaks truth free from slaughter;
78He's the grace of every feast,
79And sometimes the chiefest guest;
80Hath his trencher and his stool,
81When wit waits upon the fool.
82O, who would not be
83He, he, he?

One knocks without.

VOLPONE

84Who's that? Away!
85[Exeunt Nano and Castrone.] Look, Mosca.

[Exit Androgyno.]

MOSCA

[Exeunt Nano and Castrone.] Look, Mosca. Fool begone!
86'Tis Signor Voltore, the advocate;
87I know him by his knock.

VOLPONE

I know him by his knock. Fetch me my gown,
88My furs and night-caps; say, my couch is changing,
89And let him entertain himself awhile
90Without i' the gallery.
Without i' the gallery. [Exit Mosca.] Now, now, my clients
91Begin their visitation! Vulture, kite,
92Raven, and gor-crow, all my birds of prey,
93That think me turning carcass, now they come;
94I am not for them yet-
I am not for them yet- [Re-enter Mosca, with the gown, etc.] How now! The news?

MOSCA

95A piece of plate, sir.

VOLPONE

A piece of plate, sir. Of what bigness?

MOSCA

A piece of plate, sir. Of what bigness? Huge,
96Massy, and antique, with your name inscribed,
97And arms engraven.

VOLPONE

And arms engraven. Good! And not a fox
98Streched on the earth, with fine delusive sleights,
99Mocking a gaping crow? ha, Mosca!

MOSCA

Mocking a gaping crow? ha, Mosca! Sharp, sir.

VOLPONE

100Give me my furs. Why dost thou laugh so, man?

MOSCA

101I cannot choose, sir, when I apprehend
102What thoughts he has without now, as he walks:
103That this might be the last gift he should give;
104That this would fetch you; if you died to-day,
105And gave him all, what he should be to-morrow;
106What large return would come of all his ventures;
107How he should worshiped be, and reverenced;
108Ride with his furs, and foot-cloths; waited on
109By herds of fools, and clients; have clear way
110Made for his mule, as lettered as himself;
111Be called the great and learned advocate:
112And then concludes, there's naught impossible.

VOLPONE

113Yes, to be learned, Mosca.

MOSCA

Yes, to be learned, Mosca. O, no: rich
114Implies it. Hood an ass with reverend purple,
115So you can hide his two ambitious ears,
116And he shall pass for a cathedral doctor.

VOLPONE

117My caps, my caps, good Mosca. Fetch him in.

MOSCA

118Stay, sir; your ointment for your eyes.

VOLPONE

Stay, sir; your ointment for your eyes. That's true;
119Dispatch, dispatch: I long to have possession
120Of my new present.

MOSCA

Of my new present. That, and thousands more,
121I hope to see you lord of.

VOLPONE

I hope to see you lord of. Thanks, kind Mosca.

MOSCA

122And that, when I am lost in blended dust,
123And hundred such as I am, in succession -

VOLPONE

124Nay, that were too much, Mosca.

MOSCA

Nay, that were too much, Mosca. You shall live
125Still, to delude these harpies.

VOLPONE

Still, to delude these harpies. Loving Mosca!
126'Tis well: my pillow now, and let him enter.
127[Exit Mosca.] Now, my feigned cough, my phthisic, and my gout,
128My apoplexy, palsy, and catarrhs,
129Help, with your forced functions, this my posture,
130Wherein, this three year, I have milked their hopes.
131He comes; I hear him-Uh!
He comes; I hear him-Uh! [Coughing.] uh, uh, uh, uh! O -

ACT 1, Scene 3

[Enter Mosca, ushering in Voltore, carrying a platter.]

MOSCA

1You still are what you were, sir. Only you,
2Of all the rest, are he commands his love,
3And you do wisely to preserve it thus,
4With early visitation, and kind notes
5Of your good meaning to him, which, I know,
6Cannot but come most grateful. Patron! Sir!
7Here's Signior Voltore is come-

VOLPONE

Here's Signior Voltore is come- [Faintly.] What say you?

MOSCA

8Sir, Signior Voltore is come this morning
9To visit you.

VOLPONE

To visit you. I thank him.

MOSCA

To visit you. I thank him. And hath brought
10A piece of antique plate, bought of St. Mark,
11With which he here presents you.

VOLPONE

With which he here presents you. He is welcome.
12Pray him to come more often.

MOSCA

Pray him to come more often. Yes.

VOLTORE

Pray him to come more often. Yes. What says he?

MOSCA

13He thanks you, and desires you see him often.

VOLPONE

14Mosca.

MOSCA

Mosca. My patron!

VOLPONE

Mosca. My patron! Bring him near, where is he?
15I long to feel his hand.

MOSCA

I long to feel his hand. The plate is here, sir.

VOLTORE

16How fare you, sir?

VOLPONE

How fare you, sir? I thank you, Signor Voltore;
17Where is the plate? Mine eyes are bad.

VOLTORE

Where is the plate? Mine eyes are bad. [Putting it into his hands.] I'm sorry,
18To see you still thus weak.

MOSCA

To see you still thus weak. That he’s not weaker.aparte

VOLPONE

VOLTORE

[Taking hold of the platter.] You are too munificent. No, sir; would to heaven,
20I could as well give health to you, as that plate!

VOLPONE

21You give, sir, what you can: I thank you. Your love
22Hath taste in this, and shall not be unanswered:
23I pray you see me often.

VOLTORE

I pray you see me often. Yes, I shall, sir.

VOLPONE

24Be not far from me.

MOSCA

Be not far from me. Do you observe that, sir?aparte

VOLPONE

25Hearken unto me still; it will concern you.

MOSCA

26You are a happy man, sir; know your good.aparte

VOLPONE

27I cannot now last long -

MOSCA

I cannot now last long - You are his heir, sir.aparte

VOLTORE

28Am I?

VOLPONE

29I feel me going; uh! uh! uh! uh!
30I’m sailing to my port, uh! uh! uh! uh!
31And I am glad I am so near my haven.

MOSCA

32Alas, kind gentleman! Well, we must all go -

VOLTORE

33But, Mosca -

MOSCA

But, Mosca - Age will conquer.

VOLTORE

But, Mosca - Age will conquer. Pray thee, hear me:
34Am I inscribed his heir for certain?

MOSCA

Am I inscribed his heir for certain? Are you!
35I do beseech you, sir, you will vouchsafe
36To write me in your family. All my hopes
37Depend upon your worship: I am lost,
38Except the rising sun do shine on me.

VOLTORE

39It shall both shine, and warm thee, Mosca.

MOSCA

40Sir,
41I am a man, that hath not done your love
42All the worst offices: here I wear your keys,
43See all your coffers and your caskets locked,
44Keep the poor inventory of your jewels,
45Your plate and monies; am your steward, sir,
46Husband your goods here.

VOLTORE

Husband your goods here. But am I sole heir?

MOSCA

47Without a partner, sir; confirmed this morning:
48The wax is warm yet, and the ink scarce dry
49Upon the parchment.

VOLTORE

Upon the parchment. Happy, happy, me!
50By what good chance, sweet Mosca?

MOSCA

By what good chance, sweet Mosca? Your desert, sir;
51I know no second cause.

VOLTORE

I know no second cause. Thy modesty
52Is not to know it; well, we shall requite it.

MOSCA

53He ever liked your course, sir; that first took him.
54I oft have heard him say, how he admired
55Men of your large profession, that could speak
56To every cause, and things mere contraries,
57Till they were hoarse again, yet all be law;
58That, with most quick agility, could turn,
59And return; make knots, and undo them;
60Give forked counsel; take provoking gold
61On either hand, and put it up: these men,
62He knew, would thrive with their humility.
63And, for his part, he thought he should be blest
64To have his heir of such a suffering spirit,
65So wise, so grave, of so perplexed a tongue,
66And loud withal, that would not wag, nor scarce
67Lie still, without a fee, when every word
68Your worship but lets fall, is a cecchine!
69(Another knocks.) Who's that? one knocks; I would not have you seen, sir.
70And yet — pretend you came, and went in haste:
71I'll fashion an excuse — and, gentle sir,
72When you do come to swim in golden lard,
73Up to the arms in honey, that your chin
74Is borne up stiff, with fatness of the flood,
75Think on your vassal; but remember me:
76I have not been your worst of clients.

VOLTORE

I have not been your worst of clients. Mosca!—

MOSCA

77When will you have your inventory brought, sir?
78Or see a copy of the will? —Anon! —
79I'll bring them to you, sir. Away, be gone,
80Put business in your face.

[Exit Voltore.]

VOLPONE

Put business in your face. [Springing up.] Excellent, Mosca!
81Come hither, let me kiss thee.

MOSCA

Come hither, let me kiss thee. Keep you still, sir.
82Here is Corbaccio.

VOLPONE

Here is Corbaccio. Set the plate away:
83The vulture's gone, and the old raven's come!

ACT 1, Scene 4

3A wretch who is indeed more impotent
4Than this can feign to be; yet hopes to hop

Over his grave- [Enter Corbaccio.] Signior Corbaccio!
6You're very welcome, sir.

CORBACCIO

You're very welcome, sir. How does your patron?

MOSCA

7Troth, as he did, sir; no amends.

CORBACCIO

Troth, as he did, sir; no amends. What! mends he?

MOSCA

8No, sir: he’s rather worse.

CORBACCIO

No, sir: he’s rather worse. That's well. Where is he?

MOSCA

9Upon his couch, sir, newly fallen asleep.

CORBACCIO

10Does he sleep well?

MOSCA

Does he sleep well? No wink, sir, all this night.

CORBACCIO

Nor yesterday; but slumbers. Good! He should take
12Some counsel of physicians: I have brought him
13An opiate here, from mine own doctor.

MOSCA

14He will not hear of drugs.

CORBACCIO

He will not hear of drugs. Why? I myself
15Stood by while it was made, saw all the ingredients:
16And know, it cannot but most gently work:
17My life for his, 'tis but to make him sleep.

18Aye, his last sleep, if he would take it.aparte

19Sir,

CORBACCIO

Sir, He has no faith in physic. Say you, say you?

MOSCA

20He has no faith in physic: he does think
21Most of your doctors are the greater danger
22And worse disease, to escape. I often have
23Heard him protest, that your physician
24Should never be his heir.

CORBACCIO

Should never be his heir. Not I his heir?

MOSCA

25Not your physician, sir.

CORBACCIO

Not your physician, sir. O, no, no, no,
26I do not mean it.

MOSCA

I do not mean it. No, sir, nor their fees
27He cannot brook: he says, they flay a man,
28Before they kill him.

CORBACCIO

Before they kill him. Right, I do conceive you.

MOSCA

29And then they do it by experiment;
30For which the law not only doth absolve them,
31But gives them great reward: and he is loath
32To hire his death, so.

CORBACCIO

To hire his death, so. It is true, they kill
33With as much license as a judge.

MOSCA

With as much license as a judge. Nay, more;
34For he but kills, sir, where the law condemns,
35And these can kill him too.

CORBACCIO

And these can kill him too. Aye, or me;
36Or any man. How does his apoplex?
37Is that strong on him still?

MOSCA

Is that strong on him still? Most violent.
38His speech is broken, and his eyes are set,
39His face drawn longer than 'twas wont–

CORBACCIO

His face drawn longer than 'twas wont– How! how!
40Stronger than he was wont?

MOSCA

Stronger than he was wont? No, sir: his face
41Drawn longer than 'twas wont.

CORBACCIO

Drawn longer than 'twas wont. O, good!

MOSCA

Drawn longer than 'twas wont. O, good! His mouth
42Is ever gaping, and his eyelids hang.

CORBACCIO

Is ever gaping, and his eyelids hang. Good.

MOSCA

43A freezing numbness stiffens all his joints,
44And makes the colour of his flesh like lead.

CORBACCIO

And makes the colour of his flesh like lead. ‘Tis good.

MOSCA

45His pulse beats slow, and dull.

CORBACCIO

His pulse beats slow, and dull. Good symptoms still.

MOSCA

46And from his brain–

CORBACCIO

And from his brain– Ha? How? not from his brain?

MOSCA

47Yes, sir, and from his brain–

CORBACCIO

Yes, sir, and from his brain– I conceive you; good.

MOSCA

48Flows a cold sweat, with a continual rheum,
49Forth the resolved corners of his eyes.

CORBACCIO

50Is't possible? Yet I am better, ha!
51How does he, with the swimming of his head?

MOSCA

52O, sir, 'tis past the scotomy; he now

CORBACCIO

55Excellent, excellent! sure I shall outlast him:

MOSCA

57I was a coming for you, sir.

CORBACCIO

I was a coming for you, sir. Has he made his will?
58What has he given me?

MOSCA

What has he given me? No, sir.

CORBACCIO

What has he given me? No, sir. Nothing! Ha?

MOSCA

59He has not made his will, sir.

CORBACCIO

He has not made his will, sir. Oh, oh, oh!

MOSCA

61He smelt a carcass, sir, when he but heard

63As I did urge him to it for your good -

CORBACCIO

64He came unto him, did he? I thought so.

MOSCA

65Yes, and presented him this piece of plate.

CORBACCIO

66To be his heir?

MOSCA

To be his heir? I do not know, sir.

CORBACCIO

To be his heir? I do not know, sir. True:

I know it too. By your own scale, sir.aparte

CORBACCIO

I know it too. By your own scale, sir. Well,
68I shall prevent him, yet. See, Mosca, look,

MOSCA

Will quite weigh down his plate. [Taking the bag.] Yea, marry, sir.

CORBACCIO

73'Tis aurum palpabile , if not potabile .

MOSCA

CORBACCIO

75Ay, do, do, do.

MOSCA

Ay, do, do, do. Most blessed cordial!

CORBACCIO

MOSCA

CORBACCIO

I think it were not best, sir. What?

MOSCA

CORBACCIO

78O, no, no, no; by no means.

MOSCA

O, no, no, no; by no means. Why, sir, this

CORBACCIO

CORBACCIO

84How?

MOSCA

CORBACCIO

Decreed by destiny. How, how, good Mosca?

MOSCA

87I'll tell you, sir. This fit he shall recover.

CORBACCIO

88I do conceive you.

MOSCA

I do conceive you. And, on first advantage
89Of his gained sense, will I re-importune him
90Unto the making of his testament:
91And show him this.

[Pointing to the money.]

CORBACCIO

And show him this. Good, good.

MOSCA

And show him this. Good, good. 'Tis better yet,
92If you will hear, sir.

CORBACCIO

If you will hear, sir. Yes, with all my heart.

MOSCA

95My master your sole heir.

CORBACCIO

My master your sole heir. And disinherit

MOSCA

My son! O, sir, the better: for that colour

CORBACCIO

Shall make it much more taking. O, but colour?

MOSCA

98This will, sir, you shall send it unto me.
99Now, when I come to enforce, as I will do,
100Your cares, your watchings, and your many prayers,
101Your more than many gifts, your this day’s present,
102And last, produce your will; where, without thought,
103Or least regard, unto your proper issue,
104A son so brave, and highly meriting,
105The stream of your diverted love hath thrown you
106Upon my master, and made him your heir:
107He cannot be so stupid, or stone-dead,
108But out of conscience, and mere gratitude–

CORBACCIO

109He must pronounce me his?

MOSCA

He must pronounce me his? ‘Tis true.

CORBACCIO

CORBACCIO

111Do you not believe it?

MOSCA

Do you not believe it? Yes, sir.

CORBACCIO

Do you not believe it? Yes, sir. Mine own project.

MOSCA

CORBACCIO

Which, when he hath done, sir– Published me his heir?

MOSCA

113And you so certain to survive him–

CORBACCIO

And you so certain to survive him– Aye.

MOSCA

114Being so lusty a man–

CORBACCIO

Being so lusty a man– ‘Tis true.

MOSCA

Being so lusty a man– ‘Tis true. Yes, sir–

CORBACCIO

115I thought on that too. See, how he should be

MOSCA

CORBACCIO

MOSCA

But multiplied it on my son. ‘Tis right, sir.

CORBACCIO

119Still, my invention.

MOSCA

120It hath been all my study, all my care,

CORBACCIO

122I do conceive, sweet Mosca.

MOSCA

I do conceive, sweet Mosca. You are he,
123For whom I labour here.

CORBACCIO

For whom I labour here. Ay, do, do, do:
124I’ll straight about it.

VOLPONE

MOSCA

Let out my sides, let out my sides – Contain

VOLPONE

136Oh, but thy working, and thy placing it!
137I cannot hold; good rascal, let me kiss thee:
138I never knew thee in so rare a humour.

MOSCA

139Alas, sir, I but do as I am taught;
140Follow your grave instructions; give them words;
141Pour oil into their ears, and send them hence.

VOLPONE

142‘Tis true, ‘tis true. What a rare punishment
143Is avarice to itself!

MOSCA

Is avarice to itself! Aye, with our help, sir.

VOLPONE

144So many cares, so many maladies,
145So many fears attending on old age,
146Yea, death so often called on, as no wish
147Can be more frequent with them, their limbs faint,
148Their senses dull, their seeing, hearing, going,
149All dead before them; yea, their very teeth,
150Their instruments of eating, failing them:
151Yet this is reckoned life! nay, here was one,
152Is now gone home, that wishes to live longer!
153Feels not his gout, nor palsy; feigns himself
154Younger by scores of years, flatters his age
155With confident belying it, hopes he may,
156With charms, like Aeson, have his youth restored:
157And with these thoughts so battens, as if fate
158Would be as easily cheated on, as he,
159And all turns air!

VOLPONE

ACT 1, Scene 5

[Enter Corvino.]

MOSCA

1Signior Corvino! Come most wished for! O,
2How happy were you, if you knew it, now!

CORVINO

3Why? what? wherein?

MOSCA

Why? what? wherein? The tardy hour is come, sir.

CORVINO

4He is not dead?

MOSCA

He is not dead? Not dead, sir, but as good;
5He knows no man.

CORVINO

He knows no man. How shall I do, then?

MOSCA

He knows no man. How shall I do, then? Why, sir?

CORVINO

6I have brought him here a pearl.

MOSCA

I have brought him here a pearl. Perhaps he has
7So much remembrance left, as to know you, sir:
8He still calls on you; nothing but your name
9Is in his mouth. Is your pearl orient, sir?

CORVINO

10Venice was never owner of the like.

VOLPONE

11[Faintly.] Signior Corvino!

MOSCA

[Faintly.] Signior Corvino! Hark.

VOLPONE

[Faintly.] Signior Corvino! Hark. Signior Corvino!

MOSCA

12He calls you; step and give it him. – He’s here, sir,
13And he has brought you a rich pearl.

CORVINO

And he has brought you a rich pearl. [to Voltone] How do you, sir?
14[To Mosca] Tell him, it doubles the twelfth carat.

MOSCA

[To Mosca] Tell him, it doubles the twelfth carat. Sir,
15He cannot understand, his hearing’s gone;
16And yet it comforts him to see you –

CORVINO

And yet it comforts him to see you – Say,
17I have a diamond for him, too.

MOSCA

I have a diamond for him, too. Best show it, sir;
18Put it into his hand; ‘tis only there
19He apprehends: he has his feeling, yet.
20See how he grasps it!

CORVINO

See how he grasps it! ‘Las, good gentleman!
21How pitiful the sight is!

MOSCA

How pitiful the sight is! Tut! forget, sir.
22The weeping of an heir should still be laughter
23Under a visor.

CORVINO

Under a visor. Why, am I his heir?

MOSCA

24Sir, I am sworn, I may not show the will
25Till he be dead; But here has been Corbaccio,
26Here has been Voltore, here were others too,
27I cannot number ‘em, they were so many;
28All gaping here for legacies: but I,
29Taking the vantage of his naming you,
30“Signior Corvino! Signior Corvino!”, took
31Paper, and pen, and ink, and there I asked him,
32Whom he would have his heir. “Corvino.” Who
33Should be executor? “Corvino.” And,
34To any question he was silent to,
35I still interpreted the nods he made,
36Through weakness, for consent: and sent home th’others,
37Nothing bequeathed them, but to cry and curse.

[They embrace.]

CORVINO

38O, my dear Mosca! Does he not perceive us?

MOSCA

39No more than a blind harper. He knows no man,
40No face of friend, nor name of any servant,
41Who ‘twas that fed him last, or gave him drink:
42Not those he hath begotten, or brought up,
43Can he remember.

CORVINO

Can he remember. Has he children?

MOSCA

Can he remember. Has he children? Bastards,
44Some dozen, or more, that he begot on beggars,
45Gypsies, and Jews, and black-moors, when he was drunk.
46Knew you not that, sir? ‘tis the common fable.
47The dwarf, the fool, the eunuch, are all his;
48He’s the true father of his family,
49In all, save me:- but he has given them nothing.

CORVINO

50That’s well, that’s well! Art sure he does not hear us?

MOSCA

51Sure, sir! why, look you, credit your own sense.
52[Shouts in Volpone’s ear] The pox approach, and add to your diseases,
53If it would send you hence the sooner, sir.
54For your incontinence, it hath deserved it
55Throughly, and throughly, and the plague to boot!—
56You may come near, sir. —Would you would once close
57Those filthy eyes of yours, that flow with slime,
58Like two frog-pits; and those same hanging cheeks,
59Covered with hide instead of skin – Nay, help, sir –
60That look like frozen dish-clouts set on end!

CORVINO

61Or like an old smoked wall, on which the rain
62Ran down in streaks!

MOSCA

Ran down in streaks! Excellent, sir! speak out:
63You may be louder yet; a culverin
64Discharged in his ear would hardly bore it.

CORVINO

65His nose is like a common sewer, still running.

MOSCA

66‘Tis good! And what his mouth?

CORVINO

‘Tis good! And what his mouth? A very draught.

MOSCA

67O, stop it up –

CORVINO

O, stop it up – By no means.

MOSCA

O, stop it up – By no means. ‘Pray you, let me:

69As well as any woman that should keep him.

CORVINO

70Do you as will; but I’ll be gone.

MOSCA

Do you as will; but I’ll be gone. Be so:
71It is your presence makes him last so long.

CORVINO

72I pray you, use no violence.

MOSCA

I pray you, use no violence. No, sir? Why?

CORVINO

74Nay, at your discretion.

MOSCA

Nay, at your discretion. Well, good sir, begone.

CORVINO

75I will not trouble him now, to take my pearl.

MOSCA

76Puh! nor your diamond. What a needless care

78Am not I here, whom you have made your creature?
79That owe my being to you?

CORVINO

That owe my being to you? Grateful Mosca!

MOSCA

82Excepting one.

CORVINO

Excepting one. What’s that?

MOSCA

MOSCA

MOSCA

Their wives to all encounters! Sir, this knight
103Had not his name for nothing, he is politic,
104And knows, howe’er his wife affect strange airs,
105She hath not yet the face to be dishonest:
106But had she Signior Corvino’s wife’s face –

VOLPONE

107Has she so rare a face?

MOSCA

Has she so rare a face? O, sir, the wonder,
108The blazing star of Italy! a wench
109Of the first year! a beauty ripe as harvest!
110Whose skin is whiter than a swan all over,
111Than silver, snow, or lilies! a soft lip,
112Would tempt you to eternity of kissing!
113And flesh that melteth in the touch to blood!
114Bright as your gold, and lovely as your gold!

VOLPONE

115Why had not I known this before?

MOSCA

Why had not I known this before? Alas, sir,
116Myself but yesterday discovered it.

VOLPONE

117How might I see her?

MOSCA

How might I see her? O, not possible;
118She’s kept as warily as is your gold;
119Never does come abroad, never takes air,
120But at a window. All her looks are sweet,
121As the first grapes or cherries, and are watched
122As near as they are.

VOLPONE

As near as they are. I must see her.

MOSCA

As near as they are. I must see her. Sir,
123There is a guard of spies ten thick upon her,
124All his whole household; each of which is set
125Upon his fellow, and have all their charge,
126When he goes out, when he comes in, examined.

VOLPONE

127I will go see her, though but at her window.

MOSCA

128In some disguise, then.

VOLPONE

In some disguise, then. That is true; I must
129Maintain mine own shape still the same: we’ll think.
