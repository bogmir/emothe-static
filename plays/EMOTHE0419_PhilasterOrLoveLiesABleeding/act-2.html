John Fletcher

Philaster, or Love Lies a-Bleeding

<<Actus Secundus>>

<<Scena Prima>>

[Philaster's Lodging.]

Enter Philaster and his boy, called Bellario

Philaster

1And thou shalt find her honourable, boy;
2Full of regard unto thy tender youth
3For thine own modesty; and, for my sake,
4Apter to give than thou wilt be to ask,
5Ay, or deserve.

Bellario

Ay, or deserve. Sir, you did take me up
6When I was nothing; and only yet am something
7By being yours. You trusted me unknown;
8And that which you are apt to conster
9A simple innocence in me, perhaps
10Might have been craft, the cunning of a boy
11Harden'd in lies and theft: yet ventur'd you
12To part my miseries and me: for which,
13I never can expect to serve a lady
14That bears more honour in her breast than you.

Philaster

15But, boy, it will prefer thee. Thou art young,
16And bear'st a childish overflowing love
17To them that clap thy cheeks and speak thee fair yet;
18But when thy judgment comes to rule those passions,
19Thou wilt remember best those careful friends
20That plac'd thee in the noblest way of life.
21She is a princess I prefer thee to.

Bellario

22In that small time that I have seen the world,
23I never knew a man hasty to part
24With a servant he thought trusty. I remember,
25My father would prefer the boys he kept
26To greater men than he; but did it not
27Till they were grown too saucy for himself.

Philaster

28Why, gentle boy, I find no fault at all
29In thy behaviour.

Bellario

In thy behaviour. Sir, if I have made
30A fault of ignorance, instruct my youth:
31I shall be willing, if not apt, to learn;
32Age and experience will adorn my mind
33With larger knowledge; and if I have done
34A wilful fault, think me not past all hope
35For once. What master holds so strict a hand
36Over his boy, that he will part with him
37Without one warning? Let me be corrected
38To break my stubbornness, if it be so,
39Rather than turn me off; and I shall mend.

Philaster

40Thy love doth plead so prettily to stay,
41That (trust me) I could weep to part with thee.
42Alas, I do not turn thee off! Thou knowest
43It is my business that doth call thee hence;
44And when thou art with her, thou dwell'st with me,
45Think so, and 't is so; and when time is full,
46That thou hast well discharg'd this heavy trust,
47Laid on so weak a one, I will again
48With joy receive thee; as I live, I will!
49Nay, weep not, gentle boy. 'T is more than time
50Thou didst attend the princess.

Bellario

Thou didst attend the princess. I am gone.
51But since I am to part with you, my lord,
52And none knows whether I shall live to do
53More service for you, take this little prayer:
54Heaven bless your loves, your sighs, all your designs!
55May sick men, if they have your wish, be well;
56And Heaven hate those you curse, though I be one!

Exit.

Philaster

57The love of boys unto their lords is strange;
58I have read wonders of it: yet this boy
59For my sake (if a man may judge by looks
60And speech) would out-do story. I may see
61A day to pay him for his loyalty.

Exit.

[SCENE II]

[Lobby of the Court.]

Enter Pharamond

Pharamond

Why should these ladies stay so long? They must come this way. I know the queen employs 'em not; for the reverend mother sent me word, they would all be for the garden. If they should all prove honest now, I were in a fair taking. I was never so long without sport in my life, and, in my conscience, 't is not my fault. Oh, for our country ladies! Enter Galatea Here's one bolted; I'll hound at her. – Madam!

Galatea

10Your grace!

Pharamond

11Shall I not be a trouble?

Galatea

Shall I not be a trouble? Not to me, sir.

[Going.]

Pharamond

12Nay, nay, you are too quick. By this sweet hand –

Galatea

13You'll be forsworn, sir; 't is but an old glove.
14If you will talk at distance, I am for you:
15But, good prince, be not bawdy, nor do not brag.
16These two I only bar;
17And then, I think, I shall have sense enough
18To answer all the weighty apophthegms
19Your royal blood shall manage.

Pharamond

Dear lady, can you love?

Galatea

Dear prince! how dear? I ne'er cost you a coach yet, nor put you to the dear repentance of a banquet. Here's no scarlet, sir, to blush the sin out it was given for. This wire mine own hair covers; and this face has been so far from being dear to any, that it ne'er cost penny painting; and, for the rest of my poor wardrobe, such as you see, it leaves no hand behind it, to make the jealous mercer's wife curse our good doings.

Pharamond

31You mistake me, lady.

Galatea

32Lord, I do so; would you or I could help it!

Pharamond

33Y' are very dangerous bitter, like a potion.

Galatea

34No, sir, I do not mean to purge you, though
35I mean to purge a little time on you.

Pharamond

36Do ladies of this country use to give
37No more respect to men of my full being?

Galatea

Full being! I understand you not, unless your grace means growing to fatness; and then your only remedy (upon my knowledge, prince) is, in a morning, a cup of neat white wine brewed with carduus, then fast till supper; about eight you may eat. Use exercise, and keep a sparrow-hawk; you can shoot in a tiller: but, of all, your grace must fly phlebotomy, fresh pork, conger, and clarified whey; they are all dullers of the vital spirits.

Pharamond

Lady, you talk of nothing all this while.

Galatea

'T is very true, sir; I talk of you.

Pharamond

This is a crafty wench. I like in a shower of gold.

Galatea

What have you there, my lord? Gold! now, as I live, 't is fair gold! You would have silver for it, to play with the pages. You could not have taken me in a worse time; but, if you have present use, my lord, I'll send my man with silver and keep your gold safe for you.

[Takes the gold.]

Pharamond

Lady, lady!

Galatea

62She's coming, sir, behind, will take white money. –
63Yet for all this I'll match ye.aparte

She slips behind the arras.

Pharamond

If there be but two such more in this kingdom, and near the court, we may even hang up our harps. Ten such camphor constitutions as this would call the golden age again in question, and teach the old way for every ill-fac'd husband to get his own children; and what a mischief that would breed, let all consider! Enter Megra Here's another: if she be of the same last, the devil shall pluck her on. – Many fair mornings, lady!

Megra

75As many mornings bring as many days,
76Fair, sweet and hopeful to your grace!

Pharamond

77She gives good words yet; sure this wench is free.aparte
78If your more serious business do not call you,
79Let me hold quarter with you; we will talk
80An hour out quickly.

Megra

An hour out quickly. What would your grace talk of?

Pharamond

81Of some such pretty subject as yourself:
82I'll go no further than your eye, or lip;
83There's theme enough for one man for an age.

Megra

84Sir, they stand right, and my lips are yet even,
85Smooth, young enough, ripe enough, and red enough,
86Or my glass wrongs me.

Pharamond

87Oh, they are two twinn'd cherries dy'd in blushes,
88Which those fair suns above with their bright beams
89Reflect upon and ripen. Sweetest beauty,
90Bow down those branches, that the longing taste
91Of the faint looker-on may meet those blessings,
92And taste and live.

They kiss.

Megra

93Oh, delicate sweet prince!aparte
94She that hath snow enough about her heart
95To take the wanton spring of ten such lines off,
96May be a nun without probation.aparte
97You have in such neat poetry gather'd a kiss,
98That if I had but five lines of that number,
99Such pretty begging blanks, I should commend
100Your forehead or your cheeks, and kiss you too.

Pharamond

101Do it in prose; you cannot miss it, madam.

Megra

102I shall, I shall.

Pharamond

I shall, I shall. By my life, but you shall not;
103I'll prompt you first.
104[Kisses her.] Can you do it now?

Megra

105Methinks 't is easy, now you ha' done 't before me;
106But yet I should stick at it.

[Kisses him.]

Pharamond

But yet I should stick at it. Stick till to-morrow;
107I'll ne'er part you, sweetest. But we lose time:
108Can you love me?

Megra

109Love you, my lord! How would you have me love you?

Pharamond

I'll teach you in a short sentence, 'cause I will not load your memory; this is all: love me, and lie with me.

Megra

Was it "lie with me" that you said? 'T is impossible.

Pharamond

Not to a willing mind, that will endeavour. If I do not teach you to do it as easily in one night as you'll go to bed, I'll lose my royal blood for 't.

Megra

119Why, prince, you have a lady of your own
120That yet wants teaching.

Pharamond

I'll sooner teach a mare the old measures than teach her anything belonging to the function. She's afraid to lie with herself if she have but any masculine imaginations about her. I know, when we are married, I must ravish her.

Megra

127By my honour, that's a foul fault, indeed;
128But time and your good help will wear it out, sir.

Pharamond

And for any other I see, excepting your dear self, dearest lady, I had rather be Sir Tim the schoolmaster, and leap a dairy-maid.

Megra

Has your grace seen the court-star, Galatea?

Pharamond

Out upon her! She's as cold of her favour as an apoplex; she sail'd by but now.

Megra

And how do you hold her wit, sir?

Pharamond

I hold her wit? The strength of all the guard cannot hold it, if they were tied to it: she would blow 'em out of the kingdom. They talk of Jupiter; he's but a squib-cracker to her. Look well about you, and you may find a tongue-bolt. But speak, sweet lady, shall I be freely welcome?

Megra

Whither?

Pharamond

To your bed. If you mistrust my faith, you do me the unnoblest wrong.

Megra

I dare not, prince, I dare not.

Pharamond

Make your own conditions: my purse shall seal 'em, and what you dare imagine you can want, I'll furnish you withal. Give two hours to yours thoughts every morning about it.

152Come, I know y' re bashful;
153Speak in my ear, will you be mine? Keep this,
154And with it me: soon I will visit you.

Megra

155My lord, my chamber's most unsafe; but when 't is night,
156I'll find some means to slip into your lodging;
157Till when –

Pharamond

158Till when, this and my heart go with thee!

Exeunt several ways.

Enter Galatea from behind the hangings

Galatea

Oh, thou pernicious petticoat prince! are these your virtues? Well, if I do not lay a train to blow your sport up, I am no woman: and, Lady Dowsabel, I'll fit you for 't.

Exit.

[SCENE III]

[Arethusa's Apartment.]

Enter Arethusa and a Lady

Arethusa

Where's the boy?

Lady

Within, madam.

Arethusa

Gave you him gold to buy him clothes?

Lady

I did.

Arethusa

And has he done't?

Lady

Yes, madam.

Arethusa

'T is a pretty sad-talking boy, is it not? Ask'd you his name?

Lady

No, madam.

Enter Galatea

Arethusa

10Oh, you are welcome. What good news?

Galatea

11As good as any one can tell your grace,
12That says she has done that you would have wish'd.

Arethusa

13Hast thou discovered?

Galatea

14Of modesty for you I have strain'd a point.

Arethusa

15I prithee, how?

Galatea

In list'ning after bawdry. I see, let a lady live never so modestly, she shall be sure to find a lawful time to hearken after bawdry. Your prince, brave Pharamond, was so hot on 't!

Arethusa

With whom?

Galatea

Why, with the lady I suspected. I can tell the time and place.

Arethusa

Oh, when, and where?

Galatea

To-night, his lodging.

Arethusa

26Run thyself into the presence; mingle there again
27With other ladies; leave the rest to me.

[Exit Galatea.]

28If destiny (to whom we dare not say,
29"Why didst thou this?") have not decreed it so,
30In lasting leaves (whose smallest characters
31Were never alter'd yet), this match shall break. –
32Where's the boy?

Lady

33Here, madam.

Enter Bellario

Arethusa

34Sir, you are sad to change your service: is 't not so?

Bellario

35Madam, I have not chang'd; I wait on you,
36To do him service.

Arethusa

To do him service. Thou disclaim'st in me.
37Tell me thy name.

Bellario

38Bellario.

Arethusa

39Thou canst sing and play?

Bellario

40If grief will give me leave, madam, I can.

Arethusa

41Alas, what kind of grief can thy years know?
42Hadst thou a curst master when thou went'st to school?
43Thou art not capable of other grief;
44Thy brows and cheeks are smooth as waters be
45When no breath troubles them. Believe me, boy,
46Care seeks out wrinkled brows and hollow eyes,
47And builds himself caves, to abide in them.
48Come, sir, tell me truly, does your lord love me?

Bellario

49Love, madam! I know not what it is.

Arethusa

50Canst thou know grief, and never yet knew'st love?
51Thou art deceiv'd, boy. Does he speak of me
52As if he wish'd me well?

Bellario

As if he wish'd me well? If it be love
53To forget all respect of his own friends
54With thinking of your face; if it be love
55To sit cross-arm'd and sigh away the day,
56Mingled with starts, crying your name as loud
57And hastily as men i' the streets do fire;
58If it be love to weep himself away
59When he but hears of any lady dead
60Or kill'd, because it might have been your chance;
61If, when he goes to rest (which will not be),
62'Twixt every prayer he says, to name you once,
63As others drop a bead, be to be in love,
64Then, madam, I dare swear he loves you.

Arethusa

65Oh y' are a cunning boy, and taught to lie
66For your lord's credit! But thou know'st a lie
67That bears this sound is welcomer to me.
68Than any truth that says he loves me not.
69Lead the way, boy. –
70[To Lady.] Do you attend me too. –
71'T is thy lord's business hastes me thus. Away!

Exeunt.

[SCENE IV]

[Before Pharamond's Lodging.]

Enter Dion, Cleremont, Thrasiline, Megra, Galatea

Dion

1Come, ladies, shall we talk a round? As men
2Do walk a mile, women should talk an hour
3After supper: 't is their exercise.

Galatea

4'T is late.

Megra

5'T is all
6My eyes will do to lead me to my bed.

Galatea

7I fear, they are so heavy, you'll scarce find
8The way to your own lodging with 'em to-night.

Enter Pharamond

Thrasiline

9The prince!

Pharamond

10Not a-bed, ladies? Y' are good sitters-up.
11What think you of a pleasant dream, to last
12Till morning?

Megra

13I should choose, my lord, a pleasing wake before it.

Enter Arethusa and Bellario

Arethusa

14'T is well, my lord; y' are courting of these ladies. –
15Is 't not late, gentlemen?

Cleremont

16Yes, madam.

Arethusa

17Wait you here.

Exit.

Megra

18She's jealous, as I live.aparte
19The princess has a Hylas, an Adonis.

Pharamond

20His form is angel-like.

Megra

21Why this is he that must, when you are wed,
22Sit by your pillow, like young Apollo, with
23His hand and voice binding your thoughts in sleep.
24The princess does provide him for you and for herself.

Pharamond

25I find no music in these boys.

Megra

I find no music in these boys. Nor I:
26They can do little, and that small they do,
27They have not wit to hide.

Dion

They have not wit to hide. Serves he the princess?

Thrasiline

28Yes.

Dion

Yes. 'T is a sweet boy: how brave she keeps him!

Pharamond

29Ladies all, good rest; I mean to kill a buck
30To-morrow morning ere y' have done your dreams.

Megra

31All happiness attend your grace!

[Exit Pharamond.]

All happiness attend your grace! Gentlemen, good rest. –
32Come, shall we to bed?

Galatea

Come, shall we to bed? Yes. – All, good night.

Dion

33May your dreams be true to you! –

Exeunt Galatea and Megra.

34What shall we do, gallants? 't is late. The king
35Is up still: see, he comes; a guard along
36With him.

Enter King, Arethusa, and Guard

King

With him. Look your intelligence be true.

Arethusa

37Upon my life, it is; and I hope
38Your highness will not tie me to a man
39That in the heat of wooing throws me off,
40And takes another.

Dion

And takes another. What should this mean?

King

41If it be true,
42That lady had been better have embrac'd
43Cureless diseases. Get you to your rest:
44You shall be righted.

Exeunt Arethusa, Bellario.

You shall be righted. – Gentlemen, draw near;
45We shall employ you. Is young Pharamond
46Come to his lodging?

Dion

Come to his lodging? I saw him enter there.

King

47Haste, some of you, ad cunningly discover
48If Megra be in her lodging.

[Exit Dion.]

Cleremont

49Sir,
50She parted hence but now, with other ladies.

King

51If she be there, we shall not need to make
52A vain discovery of our suspicion.
53You gods, I see that who unrighteouslyaparte
54Holds wealth or state from others shall be curs'daparte
55In that which meaner men are blest withal:aparte
56Ages to come shall know no male of himaparte
57Left to inherit, and his name shall beaparte
58Blotted from earth. If he have any child,aparte
59It shall be crossly match'd; the gods themselvesaparte
60Shall sow wild strife betwixt her lord and her.aparte
61Yet, if it be yours wills, forgive the sinaparte
62I have committed; let it not fallaparte
63Upon this underserving child of mine!aparte
64She has not broke your laws. But how can Iaparte
65Look to be heard of gods that must be just,aparte
66Praying upon the ground I hold by wrong?aparte

Enter Dion

Dion

Sir, I have asked, and her women swear she is within; but they, I think, are bawds. I told 'em, I must speak with her; they laugh'd, and said, their lady lay speechless. I said, my business was important; they said, their lady was about it. I grew hot, and cried, my business was a matter that concern'd life and death; they answered, so was sleeping, at which their lady was. I urg'd again, she had scarce time to be so since last I saw her: they smil'd again, and seem'd to instruct me that sleeping was nothing but lying down and winking. Answers more direct I could not get: in short, sir, I think she is not there.

King

81'T is then no time to dally. – You o' the guard,
82Wait at the back door of the prince's lodging,
83And see that none pass thence, upon your lives.

[Exeunt Guards.]

84Knock, gentlemen; knock loud; – louder yet.

[Dion, Cle., &c. knock at the door of Pharamond's Lodging.]

85What, has their pleasure taken off their hearing?
86I'll break your meditations. – Knock again. –
87Not yet? I do not think he sleeps, having this
88'Larum by him. – Once more. – Pharamond! prince!

Pharamond [appears] above.

Pharamond

89What saucy groom knocks at this dead of night?
90Where be our waiters? By my vexed soul,
91He meets is death that meets me, for his boldness.

King

92Prince, prince, you wrong your thoughts; we are your friends:
93Come down.

Pharamond

Come down. The king!

King

Come down. The king! The same, sir. Come down:
94We have cause of present counsel with you.

Pharamond

95If your grace please
96To use me, I'll attend you to your chamber.

Enter Pharamond below

King

97No, 't is too late, prince; I'll make bold with yours.

Pharamond

98I have some private reasons to myself
99Makes me unmannerly, and say you cannot. –

They press to come in.

100Nay, press not forward, gentlemen; he must
101Come through my life that comes here.

King

102Sir, be resolv'd I must and will come. – Enter.

Pharamond

103I will not be dishonour'd.
104He that enters, enters upon his death.
105Sir, 't is a sign you make no stranger of me,
106To bring these renegadoes to my chamber
107At these unseason'd hours.

King

At these unseason'd hours. Why do you
108Chafe yourself so? You are not wrong'd nor shall be;
109Only I'll search your lodging, for some cause
110To ourself known. – Enter, I say.

Pharamond

To ourself known. – Enter, I say. I say, no.

[Enter] Megra above

Megra

111Let 'em enter, prince, let 'em enter;
112I am up and ready: I know their business;
113'T is the poor breaking of a lady's honour
114They hunt so hotly after: let 'em enjoy it. –
115You have your business, gentlemen; I lay here.
116Oh, my lord the king, this is not noble in you
117To make public the weakness of a woman!

King

118Come down.

Megra

119I dare, my lord. Your hootings and your clamours,
120Your private whispers and your broad fleerings,
121Can no more vex my soul than this base carriage.
122But I have vengeance yet in store for some
123Shall, in the most contempt you can have of me,
124Be joy and nourishment.

King

Be joy and nourishment. Will you come down?

Megra

125Yes, to laugh at your worst; but I shall wring you,
126If my skill fail me not.

[Exit above.]

King

127Sir, I must dearly chide you for this looseness;
128You have wrong'd a worthy lady; but, no more. –
129Conduct him to my lodging and to bed.

[Exeunt Pharamond and Attendants.]

Cleremont

Get him another wench, and you bring him to bed indeed.

Dion

132'T is strange a man cannot ride a stage
133Or two, to breathe himself, without a warrant.
134If his gear hold, that lodgings be search'd thus,
135Pray God we may lie with our own wives in safety,
136That they be not by some trick of state mistaken!

Enter [Attendants] with Megra [below]

King

137Now, lady of honour, where's your honour now?
138No man can fit your palate but the prince.
139Thou most ill-shrouded rottenness, thou piece
140Made by a painter and a 'pothecary,
141Thou troubled sea of lust, thou wilderness
142Inhabited by wild thoughts, thou swoln cloud
143Of infection, thou ripe mine of all diseases,
144Thou all-sin, all-hell, and last, all-devils, tell me,
145Had you none to pull on with your courtesies
146But he that must be mine, and wrong my daughter?
147By all the gods, all these, and all the pages,
148And all the court, shall hoot thee through the court,
149Fling rotten oranges, make ribald rhymes,
150And sear thy name with candles upon walls!
151Do you laugh, Lady Venus?

Megra

Do you laugh, Lady Venus? Faith, sir, you must pardon me;
152I cannot choose but laugh to see you merry.
153If you do this, O King! nay, if you dare do it,
154By all those gods you swore by, and as many
155More of my own, I will have fellows, and such
156Fellows in it, as shall make noble mirth!
157The princess, your dear daughter, shall stand by me
158On walls, and sung in ballads, anything.
159Urge me no more; I know her and her haunts,
160Her lays, leaps, and outlays, and will discover all;
161Nay, will dishonour her. I know the boy
162She keeps; a handsome boy, about eighteen;
163Know what she does with him, where, and when.
164Come, sir, you put me to a woman's madness,
165The glory of a fury; and if I do not
166Do 't to the height –

King

Do 't to the height – What boy is this she raves at?

Megra

167Alas! good-minded prince, you know not these things!
168I am loath to reveal 'em. Keep this fault,
169As you would keep your health from the hot air
170Of the corrupted people, or, by Heaven,
171I will not fall alone. What I have known
172Shall be as public as a print; all tongues
173Shall speak it as they do the language they
174Are born in, as free and commonly; I'll set it,
175Like a prodigious star, for all to gaze at,
176And so high and glowing, that other kingdoms far and foreign
177Shall read it there, nay, travel with it, till they find
178No tongue to make it more, nor no more people;
179And then behold the fall of your fair princess!

King

180Has she a boy?

Cleremont

181So please your grace, I have seen a boy wait
182On her, a fair boy.

King

On her, a fair boy. Go, get you to your quarter:
183For this time I will study to forget you.

Megra

184Do you study to forget me, and I'll study
185To forget you.

Exeunt King, Megra, Guard.

Cleremont

Why, here's a male spirit fit for Hercules. If ever there be Nine Worthies of women, this wench shall ride astride and be their captain.

Dion

Sure, she has a garrison of devils in her tongue, she uttered such balls of wild-fire. She has so nettled the king, that all the doctors in the country will scarce cure him. That boy was a strange-found-out antidote to cure her infection: that boy, that princess' boy; that brave, chaste, virtuous lady's boy; and a fair boy, a well-spoken boy! All these considered, can make nothing else – but there I leave you, gentlemen.

Thrasiline

Nay, we'll go wander with you.

Exeunt.
