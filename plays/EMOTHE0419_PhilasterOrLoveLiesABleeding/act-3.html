John Fletcher

Philaster, or Love Lies a-Bleeding

← <<Actus Secundus>> <<Actus Quartus>> →

<<Actus Tertius>>

<<Scena Prima>>

[The Court.]

Enter Cleremont, Dion, and Thrasiline

Cleremont

1Nay, doubtless, 't is true.

Dion

2Ay; and 't is the gods
3That rais'd this punishment, to scourge the king
4With his own issue. Is it not a shame
5For us that should write noble in the land,
6For us that should be freemen, to behold
7A man that is the bravery of his age,
8Philaster, press'd down from his royal right
9By his regardless king? and only look
10And see the sceptre ready to be cast
11Into the hands of that lascivious lady,
12That lives in lust with a smooth boy, now to be married
13To yon strange prince, who, but that people please
14To let him be a prince, is born a slave
15In that which should be his most noble part,
16His mind?

Thrasiline

His mind? That man that would not stir with you
17To aid Philaster, let the gods forget
18That such a creature walks upon the earth!

Cleremont

19Philaster is too backward in 't himself.
20The gentry do await it, and the people,
21Against their nature, are all bent for him,
22And like a field of standing corn, that's mov'd
23With a stiff gale, their heads bow all one way.

Dion

24The only cause that draws Philaster back
25From this attempt is the fair princess' love,
26Which he admires, and we can now confute.

Thrasiline

27Perhaps he'll not believe it.

Dion

28Why, gentlemen, 't is without question so.

Cleremont

29Ay, 't is past speech she lives dishonestly.
30But how shall we, if he be curious, work
31Upon his faith?

Thrasiline

32We all are satisfied within ourselves.

Dion

33Since it is true, and tends to his own good,
34I'll make this new report to be my knowledge.
35I'll say I know it; nay, I'll swear I saw it.

Cleremont

36It will be best.

Thrasiline

It will be best. 'T will move him.

Enter Philaster

Dion

It will be best. 'T will move him. Here he comes.
37Good morrow to your honour: we have spent
38Some time in seeking you.

Philaster

Some time in seeking you. My worthy friends,
39You that can keep your memories to know
40Your friend in miseries, and cannot frown
41On men disgrac'd for virtue, a good day
42Attend you all! What service may I do
43Worthy your acceptation?

Dion

Worthy your acceptation? My good lord,
44We come to urge that virtue, which we know
45Lives in your breast, forth. Rise, and make a head;
46The nobles and the people are all dull'd
47With this usurping king; and not a man,
48That ever heard the word, knows such a thing
49As virtue, but will second your attempts.

Philaster

50How honourable is this love in you
51To me that have deserv'd none! Know, my friends.
52(You, that were born to shame your poor Philaster
53With too much courtesy,) I could afford
54To melt myself in thanks: but my designs
55Are not yet ripe. Suffice it, that ere long
56I shall employ your loves; but yet the time
57Is short of what I would.

Dion

58The time is fuller, sir, than you expect;
59That which hereafter will not, perhaps, be reach'd
60By violence, many now be caught. As for the king,
61You know the people have long hated him;
62But now the princess, whom they lov'd –

Philaster

63Why, what of her?

Dion

Why, what of her? Is loath'd as much as he.

Philaster

64By what strange means?

Dion

By what strange means? She's known a whore.

Philaster

By what strange means? She's known a whore. Thou liest.

Dion

65My lord –

Philaster

66Offers to draw and is held. Thou liest,
67And thou shalt feel it! I had thought thy mind
68Had been of honour. Thus to rob a lady
69Of her good name is an infectious sin
70Not to be pardon'd. Be it false as hell,
71'T will never be redeem'd, if it be sown
72Amongst the people, fruitful to increase
73All evil they shall hear. Let me alone
74That I may cut off falsehood whilst it springs!
75Set hills on hills betwixt me and the man
76That utters this, and I will scale them all,
77And from the utmost top fall on his neck,
78Like thunder from a cloud.

Dion

Like thunder from a cloud. This is most strange:
79Sure, he does love her.

Philaster

Sure, he does love her. I do love fair truth.
80She is my mistress, and who injures her
81Draws vengeance from me. Sirs, let go my arms.

Thrasiline

82Nay, good my lord, be patient.

Cleremont

Nay, good my lord, be patient. Sir, remember,
83This is your honour'd friend,
84That comes to do his service, and will show you
85Why he utter'd this.

Philaster

Why he utter'd this. I ask you pardon, sir;
86My zeal to truth made me unmannerly:
87Should I have heard dishonour spoke of you,
88Behind your back, untruly, I had been
89As much distemper'd and enrag'd as now.

Dion

90But this, my lord, is truth.

Philaster

But this, my lord, is truth. Oh, say not so!
91Good sir, forbear to say so: 't is then truth,
92That womankind is false: urge it no more;
93It is impossible. Why should you think
94The princess light?

Dion

The princess light? Why, she was taken at it.

Philaster

95'T is false! by Heaven, 't is false! It cannot be
96Can it? Speak, gentlemen; for God's love, speak!
97Is 't possible? Can women all be damn'd?

Dion

98Why, no, my lord.

Philaster

Why, no, my lord. Why, then, it cannot be.

Dion

99And she was taken with her boy.

Philaster

And she was taken with her boy. What boy?

Dion

100A page, a boy that serves her.

Philaster

A page, a boy that serves her. Oh, good gods!
101A little boy?

Dion

A little boy? Ay; know you him, my lord?

Philaster

102Hell and sin know him!aparte
103I'll reason it a little coldly with you.
104If she were lustful, would she take a boy,
105That knows not yet desire? She would have one
106Should meet her thoughts and know the sin he acts,
107Which is the great delight of wickedness.
108You are abus'd, and so is she, and I.

Dion

109How you, my lord?

Philaster

How you, my lord? Why, all the world's abus'd
110In an unjust report.

Dion

In an unjust report. Oh, noble sir, your virtues
111Cannot look into the subtle thoughts of woman!
112In short, my lord, I took them; I myself.

Philaster

113Now, all the devils, thou didst! Fly from my rage!
114Would thou hadst ta'en devils engend'ring plagues,
115When thou didst take them! Hide thee from mine eyes!
116Would thou hadst taken thunder on thy breast,
117When thou didst take them; or been strucken dumb
118For ever; that this foul deed might have slept
119In silence!

Thrasiline

120Have you known him so ill-temper'd?

Cleremont

121Never before.

Philaster

Never before. The winds that are let loose
122From the four several corners of the earth,
123And spread themselves all over sea and land,
124Kiss not a chaste one. What friend bears a sword
125To run me through?

Dion

To run me through? Why, my lord, are you
126So mov'd at this?

Philaster

So mov'd at this? When any fall from virtue,
127I am distracted; I have an interest in 't.

Dion

128But, good my lord, recall yourself, and think
129What's best to be done.

Philaster

What's best to be done. I thank you; I will do it.
130Please you to leave me; I'll consider of it.
131To-morrow I will find your lodging forth,
132And give you answer.

Dion

And give you answer. All the gods direct you
133The readiest way!

Thrasiline

The readiest way! He was extreme impatient.

Cleremont

134It was his virtue and his noble mind.

Exeunt Dion, Cleremont, and Thrasiline.

Philaster

135I had forgot to ask him where he took them;
136I'll follow him. Oh that I had a sea
137Within my breast, to quench the fire I feel!
138More circumstances will but fan this fire:
139It more afflicts me now, to know by whom
140This deed is done, than simply that 't is done;
141And he that tells me this is honourable,
142As far from lies as she is far from truth.
143Oh, that, like beasts, we could not grieve ourselves
144With that we see not! Bulls and rams will fight
145To keep their females, standing in their sight;
146But take 'em from them, and you take at once
147Their spleens away; and they will fall again
148Unto their pastures, growing fresh and fat,
149And taste the waters of the springs as sweet
150As 't was before, finding no start in sleep;
151But miserable man –

Enter Bellario

But miserable man – See, see, you gods,
152He walks still; and the face you let him wear
153When he was innocent is still the same,
154Not blasted! Is this justice? Do you mean
155To intrap mortality, that you allow
156Treason so smooth a brow? I cannot now
157Think he is guilty.

Bellario

Think he is guilty. Health to you, my lord!
158The princess doth commend her love, her life,
159And this, unto you.

He gives him a letter.

Philaster

And this, unto you. Oh, Bellario,
160Now I perceive she loves me: she does show it
161In loving thee, my boy. She has made thee brave.

Bellario

162My lord, she has attir'd me past my wish,
163Past my desert; more fit for her attendant,
164Though far unfit for me who do attend.

Philaster

165Thou art grown courtly, boy. – Oh, let all women,
166That love black deeds, learn to dissemble here,
167Here, by this paper! She does write to me
168As if her heart were mines of adamant
169To all the world besides; but, unto me,
170A maiden-snow that melted with my looks. –
171Tell me, my boy, how doth the princess use thee?
172For I shall guess her love to me by that.

Bellario

173Scarce like her servant, but as if I were
174Something allied to her, or had preserv'd
175Her life three time by my fidelity:
176As mothers fond do use their only sons,
177As I'd use one that's left unto my trust,
178For whom my life should pay if he met harm,
179So she does use me.

Philaster

So she does use me. Why, this is wondrous well:
180But what kind language does she feed thee with?

Bellario

181Why, she does tell me she will trust my youth
182With all her loving secrets, and does call me
183Her pretty servant; bids me weep no more
184For leaving you; she'll see my services
185Regarded: and such words of that soft strain
186That I nearer weeping when she ends
187Than ere she spake.

Philaster

Than ere she spake. This is much better still.

Bellario

188Are you not ill, my lord?

Philaster

Are you not ill, my lord? Ill? No, Bellario.

Bellario

189Methinks your words
190Fall not from off your tongue so evenly,
191Nor is there in your looks that quietness
192That I was wont to see.

Philaster

That I was wont to see. Thou art deceiv'd, boy:
193And she strokes thy head?

Bellario

And she strokes thy head? Yes.

Philaster

194And she does clap thy cheeks?

Bellario

And she does clap thy cheeks? She does, my lord.

Philaster

195And she does kiss thee, boy? ha!

Bellario

And she does kiss thee, boy? ha! How, my lord?

Philaster

196She kisses thee?

Bellario

She kisses thee? Never, my lord, by heaven.

Philaster

197That's strange, I know she does.

Bellario

That's strange, I know she does. No, by my life.

Philaster

198Why then she does not love me. Come, she does.
199I bade her do it; I charg'd her, by all charms
200Of love between us, by the hope of peace
201We should enjoy, to yield thee all delights
202Naked as to her bed; I took her oath
203Thou shouldst enjoy her. Tell me, gentle boy,
204Is she not parallelless? Is not her breath
205Sweet as Arabian winds when fruits are ripe?
206Are not her breasts two liquid ivory balls?
207Is she not all a lasting mine of joy?

Bellario

208Ay, now I see why my disturbed thoughts
209Were so perplex'd. When first I went to her,
210My heart held augury. You are abus'd;
211Some villain has abus'd you; I do see
212Whereto you tend. Fall rocks upon his head
213That put this to you! 'T is some subtle train
214To bring that noble frame of yours to nought.

Philaster

215Thou think'st I will be angry with thee. Come,
216Thou shalt know all my drift. I hate her more
217Than I love happiness, and plac'd thee there
218To pry with narrow eyes into her deeds.
219Hast thou discover'd? Is she fall'n to lust,
220As I would wish her? Speak some comfort to me.

Bellario

221My lord, you did mistake the boy you sent.
222Had she the lust of sparrows or of goats,
223Had she a sin that way, hid from the world,
224Beyond the name of lust, I would not aid
225Her base desires; but what I came to know
226As servant to her, I would not reveal,
227To make my life last ages.

Philaster

To make my life last ages. Oh, my heart!
228This is a salve worse than the main disease. –
229Tell me thy thoughts; for I will know the least
230That dwells within thee, or will rip thy heart
231To know it. I will see thy thoughts as plain
232As I do now thy face.

Bellario

As I do now thy face. Why, so you do.
233She is (for aught I know) by all the gods,
234As chaste as ice! But were she foul as hell,
235And I did know it thus, the breath of kings,
236The points of swords, tortures, nor bulls of brass,
237Should draw it from me.

Philaster

Should draw it from me. Then it is no time
238To dally with thee; I will take thy life,
239For I do hate thee. I could curse thee now.

Bellario

240If you do hate, you could not curse me worse;
241The gods have no a punishment in store
242Greater for me than is your hate.

Philaster

Greater for me than is your hate. Fie, fie,
243So young and so dissembling ! Tell me when
244And where thou didst enjoy her, or let plagues
245Fall on me, if I destroy thee not!

Draws his sword.

Bellario

246Heaven knows, I never did; and when I lie
247To save my life, may I live long and loath'd!
248Hew me asunder, and, whilst I can think,
249I'll love those pieces you have cut away
250Better than those that grow, and kiss those limbs
251Because you made 'em so.

Philaster

Because you made 'em so. Fear'st thou not death?
252Can boys contemn that?

Bellario

Can boys contemn that? Oh, what boy is he
253Can be content to live to be a man,
254That sees the best of men thus passionate,
255Thus without reason?

Philaster

Thus without reason? Oh, but thou dost not know
256What 't is to die.

Bellario

What 't is to die. Yes, I do know, my lord:
257'T is less than to be born; a lasting sleep;
258A quiet resting from all jealousy,
259A thing we all pursue. I know, besides,
260It is but giving over a game
261That must be lost.

Philaster

That must be lost. But there are pains, false boy,
262For perjur'd souls. Think but on those, and then
263Thy heart will melt, and thou wilt utter all.

Bellario

264May they fall all upon me whilst I live,
265If I be perjur'd, or have ever thought
266Of that you charge me with! If I be false,
267Send me to suffer in those punishments
268You speak of; kill me!

Philaster

You speak of; kill me! Oh, what should I do?
269Why, who can but believe him? He does swear
270So earnestly, that if it were not true,
271The gods would not endure him. Rise, Bellario:
272Thy protestations are so deep, and thou
273Dost look so truly when thou utter'st them,
274That, though I know 'em false as were my hopes,
275I cannot urge thee further. But thou wert
276To blame to injure me, for I must love
277Thy honest looks, and take no revenge upon
278Thy tender youth. A love from me to thee
279Is firm, whate'er thou dost; it troubles me
280That I have call'd the blood out of thy cheeks,
281That did so well become thee. But, good boy,
282Let me not see thee more: something is done
283That will distract me, that will make me mad,
284If I behold thee. If thou tender'st me,
285Let me not see thee.

Bellario

Let me not see thee. I will fly as far
286As there is morning, ere I give distaste
287To that most honour'd mind. But through these tears,
288Shed at my hopeless parting, I can see
289A world of treason practis'd upon you,
290And her, and me. Farewell for evermore!
291If you shall hear that sorrow struck me dead,
292And after find me loyal, let there be
293A tear shed from you in my memory,
294And I shall rest in peace.

Exit.

Philaster

295Blessing be with thee,
296Whatever thou deserv'st! Oh, where shall I
297Go bathe this body? Nature too unkind;
298That made no medicine for a troubled mind!

Exit.

[SCENE II]

[Arethusa's Apartment.]

Enter Arethusa

Arethusa

1I marvel my boy comes not back again:
2But that I know my love will question him
3Over and over, – how I slept, wak'd, talk'd,
4How I rememb'red him, when his dear name
5Was last spoke, and how, when I sigh'd, wept, sung,
6And then thousand such, – I should be angry at his stay.

Enter King

King

7What, at your meditations! Who attends you?

Arethusa

8None but my single self. I need no guard;
9I do no wrong, nor fear none.

King

10Tell me, have you not a boy?

Arethusa

Tell me, have you not a boy? Yes, sir.

King

11What kind of boy?

Arethusa

What kind of boy? A page, a waiting-boy.

King

12A handsome boy?

Arethusa

A handsome boy? I think he be not ugly:
13Well qualified and dutiful I know him;
14I took him not for beauty.

King

15He speaks and sings and plays?

Arethusa

He speaks and sings and plays? Yes, sir.

King

16About eighteen?

Arethusa

About eighteen? I never ask'd his age.

King

17Is he full of service?

Arethusa

18By your pardon, why do you ask?

King

19Put him away.

Arethusa

Put him away. Sir!

King

Put him away. Sir! Put him away, I say.
20H'as done you that good service shames me to speak of.

Arethusa

21Good, sir, let me understand you.

King

Good, sir, let me understand you. If you fear me,
22Show it in duty; put away that boy.

Arethusa

23Let me have reason for it, sir, and then
24Your will is my command.

King

25Do not you blush to ask it? Cast him off,
26Or I shall do the same to you. Y' are one
27Shame with me, and so near unto myself,
28That, by my life, I dare not tell myself,
29What you, myself, have done.

Arethusa

30What have I done, my lord?

King

31'T is a new language, that all love to learn:
32The common people speak it well already;
33They need no grammar. Understand me well:
34There be foul whispers stirring. Cast him off,
35And suddenly. Do it! Farewell.

Exit King.

Arethusa

36Where may a maiden live securely free,
37Keeping her honour fair? Not with the living.
38They feed upon opinions, errors, dreams,
39And make 'em truths; they draw a nourishment
40Out of defamings, grow upon disgraces,
41And, when they see a virtue fortified
42Strongly above the battery of their tongues,
43Oh, how they cast to sink it! and, defeated,
44(Soul-sick with poison) strike the monuments
45Where noble names lie sleeping, till they sweat,
46And the cold marble melt.

Enter Philaster

Philaster

Peace to your fairest thoughts, dearest mistress!

Arethusa

Oh, my dearest servant, I have a war within me!

Philaster

49He must be more than man that makes these crystals
50Run into rivers. Sweetest fair, the cause?
51And, as I am your slave, tied to your goodness,
52Your creature, made again from what I was
53And newly-spirited, I'll right your honour.

Arethusa

54Oh, my best love, that boy!

Philaster

Oh, my best love, that boy! What boy?

Arethusa

55The pretty boy you gave me –

Philaster

The pretty boy you gave me – What of him?

Arethusa

56Must be no more mine.

Philaster

Must be no more mine. Why?

Arethusa

Must be no more mine. Why? They are jealous of him.

Philaster

57Jealous! Who?

Arethusa

Jealous! Who? The king.

Philaster

Jealous! Who? The king. Oh, my misfortune!aparte

Then t is no idle jealousy.

Arethusa

59Oh, cruel!
60Are you hard-hearted too? Who shall now tell you
61How much I lov'd you? Who shall swear it to you,
62And weep the tears I send? Who shall now bring you
63Letters, rings, bracelets? Lose his health in service?
64Wake tedious nights in stories of your praise?
65Who shall now sing your crying elegies,
66And strike a sad soul into senseless pictures,
67And make them mourn? Who shall take up his lute,
68And touch it till he crown a silent sleep
69Upon my eye-lids, making me dream, and cry,
70"Oh, my dear, dear Philaster!"

Philaster

71Oh, my heart!aparte
72Would he had broken thee, that made thee know
73This lady was not loyal!aparte
74Forget the boy; I'll get thee a far better.

Arethusa

75Oh, never, never such a boy again
76As my Bellario!

Philaster

As my Bellario! 'T is but your fond affection.

Arethusa

77With thee, my boy, farewell for ever
78All secrecy in servants! Farewell, faith,
79And all desire to do well for itself!
80Let all that shall succeed thee for thy wrongs
81Sell and betray chaste love!

Philaster

82And all this passion for a boy?

Arethusa

83He was your boy, and you put him to me,
84And the loss of such must have a mourning for.

Philaster

85Oh, thou forgetful woman!

Arethusa

Oh, thou forgetful woman! How, my lord?

Philaster

86False Arethusa!
87Hast thou a medicine to restore my wits,
88When I have lost 'em? If not, leave to talk,
89And do thus.

Arethusa

And do thus. Do what, sir? Would you sleep?

Philaster

90For ever, Arethusa. Oh, you gods
91Give me a worthy patience! Have I stood,
92Naked, alone, the shock of many fortunes?
93Have I seen mischiefs numberless and mighty
94Grow like a sea upon me? Have I taken
95Danger as stern as death into my bosom,
96And laugh'd upon it, made it but a mirth,
97And flung it by? Do I live now like him,
98Under this tyrant king, that languishing
99Hears his sad bell and sees his mourners? Do I
100Bear all this bravely, and must sink at length
101Under a woman's falsehood? Oh, that boy,
102That cursed boy! None but a villain boy
103To ease your lust?

Arethusa

To ease your lust? Nay, then, I am betray'd:
104I feel the plot cast for my overthrow.
105Oh, I am wretched!

Philaster

106Now you may take that little right I have
107To this poor kingdom. Give it to your joy;
108For I have no joy in it. Some far place,
109Where never womankind durst set her foot
110For bursting with her poisons, must I seek,
111And live to curse you;
112There dig a cave, and preach to birds and beasts
113What woman is, and help to save them from you:
114How heaven is in your eyes, but in your hearts
115More hell than hell has; how your tongues, like scorpions,
116Both heal and poison; how your thoughts are woven
117With thousand changes in one subtle web,
118And worn so by you; how that foolish man,
119That reads the story of a woman's face
120And dies believing it, is lost for ever;
121How all the good you have is but a shadow,
122I' th' morning with you, and at night behind you,
123Past and forgotten; how your vows are frosts,
124Fast for a night, and with the next sun gone;
125How you are, being taken all together,
126A mere confusion, and so dead a chaos,
127That love cannot distinguish. These sad texts,
128Till my last hour, I am bound to utter of you.
129So, farewell all my woe, all my delight!

Exit.

Arethusa

130Be merciful, ye gods, and strike me dead!
131What way have I deserv'd this? Make my breast
132Transparent as pure crystal, that the world,
133Jealous of me, may see the foulest thought
134My heart holds. Where shall a woman turn her eyes,
135To find out constancy?

Enter Bellario

To find out constancy? Save me, how black
136And guiltily, methinks, that boy looks now!
137Oh, thou dissembler, that, before thou spak'st,
138Wert in thy cradle false, sent to make lies
139And betray innocents! Thy lord and thou
140May glory in the ashes of a maid
141Fool'd by her passion; but the conquest is
142Nothing so great as wicked. Fly away!
143Let my command force thee to that which shame
144Would do without it. If thou understood'st
145The loathed office thou hast undergone,
146Why, thou wouldst hide thee under heaps of hills,
147Lest men should dig and find thee.

Bellario

Lest men should dig and find thee. Oh, what god,
148Angry with men, hath sent this strange disease
149Into the noblest minds! Madam, this grief
150You add unto me is no more than drops
151To seas, for which they are not seen to swell.
152My lord hath struck his anger through my heart,
153And let out all the hope of future joys.
154You need not bid me fly; I came to part,
155To take my latest leave. Farewell for ever!
156I durst not run away in honesty
157From such a lady, like a boy that stole
158Or made some grievous fault. The power of gods
159Assist you in your sufferings! Hasty time
160Reveal the truth to your abused lord
161And mine, that he may know your worth; whilst I
162Go seek out some forgotten place to die!

Exit.

Arethusa

163Peace guide thee! Thou hast overthrown me once;
164Yet, if I had another Troy to lose,
165Thou, or another villain with thy looks,
166Might talk me out of it, and send me naked,
167My hair dishevell'd, through the fiery streets.

Enter a Lady

Lady

168Madam, the king would hunt, and calls for you
169With earnestness.

Arethusa

With earnestness. I am in tune to hunt!
170Diana, if thou canst rage with a maid
171As with a man, let me discover thee
172Bathing, and turn me to a fearful hind,
173That I may die pursued by cruel hounds,
174And have my story written in my wounds!

Exeunt.
