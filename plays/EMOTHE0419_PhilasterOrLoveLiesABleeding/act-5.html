[Front Stage. Location indefinite.]

Enter Dion, Cleremont, and Thrasiline

Thrasiline

Has the king sent for him to death?

Dion

Yes; but the king must know 't is not in his power to war with Heaven.

Cleremont

We linger time; the king sent for Philaster and the headsman an hour ago.

Thrasiline

Are all his wounds well?

Dion

All; they were but scratches; but the

8loss of blood made him faint.

Cleremont

We dally, gentlemen.

Thrasiline

Away!

Dion

We'll scuffle hard before he perish.

Exeunt.

[SCENE II]

[A prison.]

Enter Philaster, Arethusa, Bellario

Arethusa

1Nay, faith, Philaster, grieve not; we are well.

Bellario

2Nay, good my lord, forbear; we're wondrous well.

Philaster

3Oh, Arethusa, oh, Bellario,
4Leave to be kind!
5I shall be shut from Heaven, as now from earth,
6If you continue so. I am a man
7False to a pair of the most trusty ones
8That ever earth bore; can it bear us all?
9Forgive, and leave me. But the king hath sent
10To call me to my death: oh, show it me,
11And then forget me! And for thee, my boy,
12I shall deliver words will mollify
13The hearts of beasts to spare thy innocence.

Bellario

14Alas, my lord, my life is not a thing
15Worthy your noble thoughts! 'T is not a life,
16'T is but a piece of childhood thrown away.
17Should I outlive you, I should then outlive
18Virtue and honour; and when that day comes,
19If ever I shall close these eyes but once,
20May I live spotted for my perjury,
21And waste by time to nothing!

Arethusa

22And I ( the woful'st maid that ever was,
23Forc'd with my hands to bring my lord to death)
24Do by the honour of a virgin swear
25To tell no hours beyond it!

Philaster

To tell no hours beyond it! Make me not hated so.

Arethusa

26Come from this prison all joyful to our deaths!

Philaster

27People will tear me, when they find you true
28To such a wretch as I; I shall die loath'd.
29Enjoy your kingdoms peaceably, whilst I
30For ever sleep forgotten with my faults.
31Every just servant, every maid in love,
32Will have a piece of me, if you be true.

Arethusa

33My dear lord, say not so.

Bellario

My dear lord, say not so. A piece of you!
34He was not born of woman that can cut
35It and look on.

Philaster

36Take me in tears betwixt you, for my heart
37Will break with shame and sorrow.

Arethusa

Will break with shame and sorrow. Why 't is well.

Bellario

38Lament no more.

Philaster

Lament no more. Why, what would you have done
39If you had wrong'd me basely, and had found
40My life no price compar'd to yours? For love, sirs,
41Deal with me truly.

Bellario

Deal with me truly. 'T was mistaken, sir.

Philaster

42Why, if it were?

Bellario

Why, if it were? Then, sir, we would have ask'd
43You pardon.

Philaster

You pardon. And have hope to enjoy it?

Arethusa

44Enjoy it! ay.

Philaster

Enjoy it! ay. Would you indeed? Be plain.

Bellario

45We would, my lord.

Philaster

We would, my lord. Forgive me, then.

Arethusa

We would, my lord. Forgive me, then. So, so.

Bellario

46'T is as it should be now.

Philaster

'T is as it should be now. Lead to my death.

Exeunt.

[SCENE III]

[The Palace.]

Enter King, Dion, Cleremont, Thrasiline [with a guard]

King

1Gentlemen, who saw the prince?

Cleremont

2So please you, sir, he's gone to see the city
3And the new platform, with some gentlemen
4Attending on him.

King

Attending on him. Is the princess ready
5To bring her prisoner out?

Thrasiline

To bring her prisoner out? She waits your grace.

King

6Tell her we stay.

[Exit Thrasiline.]

Dion

Tell her we stay. King, you may be deceiv'd yet.aparte
7The head you aim at cost more setting onaparte
8Than to be lost so lightly. If it must off, –aparte
9Like a wild overflow, that swoops before himaparte
10A golden stack, and with it shakes down bridges,aparte
11Cracks the strong hearts of pines, whose cable-rootsaparte
12Held out a thousand storms, a thousand thunders,aparte
13And, so made mightier, takes whole villagesaparte
14Upon his back, and in that heat of prideaparte
15Charges strong towns, towers, castles, palaces,aparte
16And lays them desolate; so shall thy head,aparte
17Thy noble head, bury the lives of thousands,aparte
18That must bleed with thee like a sacrifice,
19In thy red ruins.aparte

Enter Philaster, Arethusa, Bellario in a robe and garland [and Thrasiline]

King

20How now? What masque is this?

Bellario

21Right royal sir, I should
22Sing you an epithalamium of these lovers,
23But having lost my best airs with my fortunes,
24And wanting a celestial harp to strike
25This blessed union on, thus in glad story
26I give you all. These two fair cedar-branches,
27The noblest of the mountain where they grew,
28Straightest and tallest, under whose still shades
29The worthier beasts have made their lairs, and slept
30Free from the fervour of the Sirian star
31And the fell thunder-stroke, free from the clouds
32When they were big with humour and deliver'd
33In thousand spouts their issues to the earth:
34Oh, there was none but silent quiet there!
35Till never-pleased Fortune shot up shrubs,
36Base under-brambles, to divorce these branches;
37And for a while they did so, and did reign
38Over the mountain, and choke up his beauty
39With brakes, rude thorns and thistles, till the sun
40Scorch'd them even to the roots and dried them there.
41And now a gentle gale hath blown again,
42That made these branches meet and twine together,
43Never to be unarm'd. The god that sings
44His holy numbers over marriage-beds
45Hath knit their noble hearts: and here they stand
46Your children, mighty King; and I have done.

King

47How, now?

Arethusa

How, now? Sir, if you love it in plain truth,
48(For now there is no masquing in 't,) this gentleman,
49The prisoner that you gave me, is become
50My keeper, and through all the bitter throes
51Your jealousies and his ill fate have wrought him,
52Thus nobly hath he struggled, and at length
53Arriv'd here my dear husband.

King

Arriv'd here my dear husband. Your dear husband! –
54Call in the captain of the citadel –
55There you shall keep your wedding. I'll provide
56A masque shall make your Hymen turn his saffron
57Into a sullen coat, and sing sad requiems
58To your departing souls.
59Blood shall put out your torches; and, instead
60Of gaudy flowers about your wanton necks,
61An axe shall hang, like a prodigious meteor,
62Ready to crop your loves' sweets. Hear, you gods!
63From this time do I shake all title off
64Of father to this woman, this base woman;
65And what there is of vengeance in a lion
66Chaf'd among dogs or robb'd of his dear young,
67The same, enforc'd more terrible, more mighty,
68Expect from me!

Arethusa

69Sir, by that little life I have left to swear by,
70There's nothing than can stir me from myself.
71What I have done, I have done without repentance,
72For death can be no bugbear unto me,
73So long as Pharamond is not my headsman.

Dion

74Sweet peace upon thy soul, thou worthy maid,aparte
75Whene'er thou diest! For this time I'll excuse thee,aparte
76Or be thy prologue.aparte

Philaster

Or be thy prologue. Sir, let me speak next;
77And let my dying words be better with you
78Than my dull living actions. If you aim
79At the dear life of this sweet innocent,
80You are a tyrant and a savage monster,
81That feeds upon the blood you gave a life to;
82Your memory shall be as foul behind you,
83As you are living; all your better deeds
84Shall be in water writ, but this in marble;
85No chronicle shall speak you, though your own,
86But for the shame of men. No monument,
87Though high and big as Pelion, shall be able
88To cover this base murder: make it rich
89With brass, with purest gold, and shining jasper,
90Like the Pyramids; lay on epitaphs
91Such as make great men gods; my little marble,
92That only clothes my ashes, not my faults,
93Shall far outshine it. And for after-issues,
94Think not so madly of the heavenly wisdoms,
95That they will give you more for your mad rage
96To cut off, unless it be some snake, or something
97Like yourself, that in his birth shall strangle you.
98Remember my father, King! There was a fault.
99But I forgive it. Let that sin persuade you
100To love this lady; if you have a soul,
101Think, save her, and be saved. For myself,
102I have so long expected this glad hour,
103So languish'd under you, and daily wither'd,
104That, Heaven knows, it is a joy to die:
105I find a recreation in 't.

Enter a Messenger

Messenger

106Where's the king ?

King

Where's the king ? Here.

Messenger

Where's the king ? Here. Get to your strength,
107And rescue the Prince Pharamond from danger;
108He's taken prisoner by the citizens,
109Fearing the Lord Philaster.

Dion

Fearing the Lord Philaster. Oh, brave fellows!aparte
110Mutiny, my fine dear countrymen, mutiny!aparte
111Now, my brave valiant foremen, show your weaponsaparte
112In honour of your mistresses!aparte

Enter another Messenger

2 Messenger

113Arm, arm, arm, arm!

King

114A thousand devils take these citizens!

Dion

115A thousand blessings on 'em!aparte

2 Messenger

116Arm, O King! The city is in mutiny,
117Led by an old gray ruffian, who comes on
118In rescue of the Lord Philaster.

King

119Away to the citadel! I'll see them safe,
120And then cope with these burghers. Let the guard
121And all the gentlemen give strong attendance.

Exeunt all except Dion, Cleremont, and Thrasiline.

Cleremont

122The city up! This was above our wishes.

Dion

123Ay, and the marriage too. By my life,
124This noble lady has deceiv'd us all.
125A plague upon myself, a thousand plagues,
126For having such unworthy thoughts of her dear honour!
127Oh, I could beat myself! Or do you beat me,
128And I'll beat you; for we had all one thought.

Cleremont

No, no, 't will but lose time.

Dion

You say true. Are your swords sharp? – Well, my dear countrymen. What-ye-lacks, if you continue, and fall not back upon the first broken shin, I'll have ye chronicled and chronicled, and cut and chronicled, and all-to be-prais'd and sung in sonnets, and bawled in new brave ballads, that all tongues shall troll you in sæcula sæculorum , my kind can-carriers.

Thrasiline

What, if a toy take 'em i' th' heels now, and they run all away, and cry, "the devil take the hindmost"?

Dion

Then the same devil take the foremost too, and souse him for his breakfast! If they all prove cowards, my curses fly among them, and be speeding! May they have murrains reign to keep the gentlemen at home unbound in easy frieze! May the moths branch their velvets, and their silks only be worn before sore eyes! May their false lights undo 'em, and discover presses, holes, stains, and oldness in their stuffs, and make them shop-rid! May they keep whores and horses, and break; and live mewed up with necks of beef and turnips! May they have many children, and none like the father! May they know no language but that gibberish they prattle to their parcels, unless it be the goatish Latin they write in their bonds – and may they write that false, and lose their debts!

Enter the King

King

Now the vengeance of all the gods confound them! How they swarm together! What a hum they raise! – Devils choke your wide throats! – If a man had need to use their valours, he must pay a brokage for it; and then bring 'em on, and they will fight like sheep. 'T is Philaster, none but Philaster, must allay this heat. They will not hear me speak, but fling dirt at me and call me tyrant. Oh, run, dear friend, and bring the Lord Philaster! Speak him fair; call him prince; do him all the courtesy you can; commend me to him. Oh, my wits, my wits!

Exit Cleremont.

Dion

Oh, my brave citizens! as I live, I will not buy a pin out of your walls for this. Nay, you shall cozen me, and I'll thank you, and send you brawn and bacon, and soil you every long vacation a brace of foremen, that at Michaelmas shall come up fat and kicking.

King

What they will do with this poor prince, the gods know, and I fear.

Dion

Why, sir, they'll flay him, and make church-buckets on 's skin, to quench rebellion; then clap a rivet in 's sconce, and hang him up for a sign.

Enter Cleremont with Philaster

King

185Oh, worthy sir, forgive me! Do not make
186Your miseries and my faults meet together,
187To bring a greater danger. Be yourself,
188Still sound amongst diseases. I have wrong'd you;
189And though I find it last, and beaten to it,
190Let first your goodness know it. Calm the people,
191And be what you were born to. Take your love,
192And with her my repentance, all my wishes,
193And all my prayers. By the gods, my heart speaks this;
194And if the least fall from me not perform'd,
195May I be struck with thunder!

Philaster

May I be struck with thunder! Mighty sir,
196I will not do your greatness so much wrong,
197As not to make your word truth. Free the princess
198And the poor boy, and let me stand the shock
199Of this mad sea-breach, which I'll either turn,
200Or perish with it.

King

Or perish with it. Let your own word free them.

Philaster

201Then thus I take my leave, kissing your hand,
202And hanging on your royal word. Be kingly,
203And be not mov'd, sir. I shall bring you peace
204Or never bring myself back.

King

205All the gods go with thee.

Exeunt omnes.

[SCENE IV]

[A Public Place.]

Enter an old Captain and Citizens with Pharamond

Captain

1Come, my brave myrmidons, let us fall on.
2Let your caps swarm, my boys, and your nimble tongues
3Forget your mother-gibberish of "What do you lack?
4And set your mouths ope, children, till your palates
5Fall frighted half a fathom past the cure
6Of bay-salt and gross pepper, and then cry
7"Philaster, brave Philaster!" Let Philaster
8Be deeper in request, my ding-dongs,
9My pairs of dear indentures, kings of clubs,
10Than your cold water-camlets, or your paintings
11Spitted with copper. Let not your hasty silks,
12Or your branch'd cloth of bodkin, or your tissues,
13Dearly belov'd of spiced cake and custards,
14Your Robin Hoods, Scarlets, and Johns, tie your affections
15In darkness to your shops. No, dainty duckers,
16Up with your three-pil'd spirits, your wrought valours;
17And let your uncut cholers make the king feel
18The measure of your mightiness. Philaster!
19Cry, my rose-nobles, cry!

All

Cry, my rose-nobles, cry! Philaster! Philaster!

Captain

20How do you like this, my Lord Prince?
21These are mad boys, I tell you; these are things
22That will not strike their top-sails to a foist,
23And let a man of war, an argosy,
24Hull and cry cockles.

Pharamond

25Why, you rude slave, do you know what you do?

Captain

26My pretty prince of puppets, we do know;
27And give your greatness warning that you talk
28No more such bug's-words, or that solder'd crown
29Shall be scratch'd with a musket. Dear prince Pippin,
30Down with your noble blood, or, as I live,
31I'll have you coddled. – Let him loose, my spirits:
32Make us a round ring with your bills, my Hectors,
33And let us see what this trim man dares do.
34Now, sir, have at you! here I lie;
35And with this swashing blow (do you see, sweet prince?)
36I could hulk your grace, and hang you up cross-legg'd,
37Like a hare at a poulter's, and do this with this wiper.

Pharamond

38You will not see me murder'd, wicked villains?

1 Citizen

39Yes, indeed, will we, sir; we have not seen one
40For a great while.

Captain

41He would have weapons, would he?
42Give him a broadside, my brave boys, with your pikes;
43Branch me his skin in flowers like a satin,
44And between every flower a mortal cut. –
45Your royalty shall ravel! – Jag him, gentlemen;
46I'll have him cut to the kell, then down the seams.
47O for a whip to make him galloon-laces!
48I'll have a coach-whip.

Pharamond

I'll have a coach-whip. Oh, spare me, gentlemen!

Captain

49Hold, hold;
50The man begins to fear and know himself.
51He shall for this time only be seel'd up,
52With a feather through his nose, that he may only
53See heaven, and think whither he is going.
54Nay, my beyond-sea sir, we will proclaim you:
55You would be king!
56Thou tender heir apparent to a church-ale,
57Thou slight prince of single sarcenet,
58Thou royal ring-tail, fit to fly at nothing
59But poor men's poultry, and have every boy
60Beat thee from that too with his bread and butter!

Pharamond

61Gods keep me from these hell-hounds!

1 Citizen

62Shall's geld him, captain?

Captain

63No, you shall spare his dowcets, my dear donsels;
64As you respect the ladies, let them flourish.
65The curses of a longing woman kill
66As speedy as a plague, boys.

1 Citizen

67I'll have a leg, that's certain.

2 Citizen

I'll have a leg, that's certain. I'll have an arm.

3 Citizen

68I'll have his nose, and at mine own charge build
69A college and clap 't upon the gate.

4 Citizen

70I'll have his little gut to string a kit with;
71For certainly a royal gut will sound like silver.

Pharamond

72Would they were in thy belly, and I past
73My pain once!

5 Citizen

74Good captain, let me have his liver to feed ferrets.

Captain

75Who will have parcels else? Speak.

Pharamond

76Good gods, consider me! I shall be tortur'd.

1 Citizen

77Captain, I'll give you the trimming of your two-hand sword,
78And let me have his skin to make false scabbards.

2 Citizen

79He had no horns, sir, had he?

Captain

80No, sir, he's a pollard.
81What wouldst thou do with horns?

2 Citizen

What wouldst thou do with horns? Oh, if he had had,
82I would have made rare hafts and whistles of 'em:
83But is shin-bones, if they be sound, shall serve me.

Enter Philaster

All

84Long live Philaster, the brave Prince Philaster!

Philaster

85I thank you, gentlemen. But why are these
86Rude weapons brought abroad, to teach your hands
87Uncivil trades?

Captain

Uncivil trades? My royal Rosicleer,
88We are thy myrmidons, thy guard, thy roarers;
89And when thy noble body is in durance,
90Thus do we clap our musty morions on,
91And trace the streets in terror. Is it peace,
92Thou Mars of men? Is the king sociable,
93And bids thee live? Art thou above thy foe-men,
94And free as Pœbus? Speak. If not, this stand
95Of royal blood shall be abroach, a-tilt,
96And run even to the lees of honour.

Philaster

97Hold, and be satisfied. I am myself,
98Free as my thoughts are; by the gods, I am!

Captain

99Art thou the dainty darling of the king?
100Art thou the Hylas to our Hercules?
101Do the lords bow, and the regarded scarlets
102Kiss their gumm'd golls, and cry, "We are your servants"?
103Is the court navigable and the presence stuck
104With flags of friendship? If not, we are thy castle,
105And this man sleeps.

Philaster

106I am what I desire to be, your friend;
107I am what I was born to be, your prince.

Pharamond

108Sir, there is some humanity in you;
109You have a noble soul. Forget my name,
110And know my misery; set me safe aboard
111From these wild cannibals, and as I live,
112I'll quit this land for ever. There is nothing, –
113Perpetual prisonment, cold, hunger, sickness
114Of all sorts, of all dangers, and all together,
115The worst company of the worst men, madness, age,
116To be as many creatures as a woman,
117And do as all they do, nay, to despair, –
118But I would rather make it a new nature,
119And live with all these, than endure one hour
120Amongst these wild dogs.

Philaster

121I do pity you. – Friends, discharge your fears;
122Deliver me the prince. I'll warrant you
123I shall be old enough to find my safety.

3 Citizen

124Good sir, take heed he does not hurt you;
125He is a fierce man, I can tell you, sir.

He strives.

Captain

126Prince, by your leave, I'll have a surcingle,
127And mail you like a hawk.

Philaster

128Away, away, there is no danger in him:
129Alas, he had rather sleep to shake his fit off!
130Look you, friends, how gently he leads! Upon my word,
131He's tame enough, he needs no further watching.
132Good my friends, go to your houses,
133And by me have your pardons and my love;
134And know there shall be nothing in my power
135You may deserve, but you shall have your wishes.
136To give you more thanks, were to flatter you:
137Continue still your love; and for an earnest,
138Drink this.

[Gives money.]

All

139Long mayst thou live, brave prince, brave prince, brave prince!

Exeunt Philaster and Pharamond.

Captain

140Go thy ways, thou art the king of courtesy!
141Fall off again, my sweet youths. Come,
142And every man trace to his house again,
143And hang his pewter up; then to the tavern,
144And bring your wives in muffs. We will have music;
145And the red grape shall make us dance and rise, boys.

Exeunt.

[SCENE V]

[The Palace.]

Enter King, Arethusa, Galatea, Megra, Dion, Cleremont, Thrasiline, Bellario, and Attendants

King

1Is it appeas'd?

Dion

2Sir, all is quiet as this dead of night,
3As peaceable as sleep. My lord Philaster
4Brings on the prince himself.

King

Brings on the prince himself. Kind gentleman!
5I will not break the least word I have given
6In promise to him. I have heap'd a world
7Of grief upon his head, which yet I hope
8To wash away.

Enter Philaster and Pharamond

Cleremont

To wash away. My lord is come.

King

To wash away. My lord is come. My son!
9Blest be the time that I have leave to call
10Such virtue mine! Now thou art in mine arms,
11Methinks I have a salve unto my breast
12For all the stings that dwell there. Streams of grief
13That I have wrong'd thee, and as much of joy
14That I repent it, issue from mine eyes;
15Let them appease thee. Take thy right; take her;
16She is thy right too; and forget to urge
17My vexed soul with that I did before.

Philaster

18Sir, it is blotted from my memory,
19Past and forgotten. – For you, prince of Spain,
20Whom I have thus redeem'd, you have full leave
21To make an honourable voyage home.
22And if would go furnish'd to your realm
23With fair provision, I do see a lady,
24Methinks, would gladly bear you company.
25How like you this piece?

Megra

How like you this piece? Sir, he likes it well,
26For he hath tried it, and hath found it worth
27His princely liking. We were ta'en abed;
28I know your meaning. I am not the first
29That nature taught to seek a fellow forth.
30Can shame remain perpetually in me,
31And not in others? Or have princes salves
32To cure ill names, that meaner people want?

Philaster

33What mean you?

Megra

What mean you? You must get another ship,
34To bear the princess and her boy together.

Dion

35How now!

Megra

36Others took me, and I took her and him
37At that all women may be ta'en sometime.
38Ships us all four, my lord; we can endure
39Weather and wind alike.

King

40Clear thou thyself, or know not me for father.

Arethusa

41This earth, how false it is! What means is left for me
42To clear myself? It lies in your belief.
43My lords, believe me; and let all things else
44Struggle together to dishonour me.

Bellario

45Oh, stop your ears, great King, that I may speak
46As freedom would! Then I will call this lady
47As base as are her actions. Here me, sir;
48Believe your heated blood when it rebels
49Against your reason, sooner than this lady.

Megra

50By this good light, he bears it handsomely.

Philaster

51This lady! I will sooner trust the wind
52With feathers, or the troubled sea with pearl,
53Than her with anything. Believe her not.
54Why, think you, if I did believe her words,
55I would outlive 'em? Honour cannot take
56Revenge on you; then what were to be known
57But death?

King

But death? Forget her, sir, since all is knit
58Between us. But I must request of you
59One favour, and will sadly be denied.

Philaster

60Command, whate'er it be.

King

Command, whate'er it be. Swear to be true
61To what you promise.

Philaster

To what you promise. By the powers above,
62Let it not be the death of her or him,
63And it is granted!

King

And it is granted! Bear away that boy
64To torture; I will have her clear'd or buried.

Philaster

65Oh, let me call my word back, worthy sir!
66Ask something else: bury my life and right
67In one poor grave; but do not take away
68My life and fame at once.

King

69Away with him! It stands irrevocable.

Philaster

70Turn all your eyes on me. Here stands a man,
71The falsest and the basest of this world.
72Set swords against this breast, some honest man,
73For I have liv'd till I am pitied!
74My former deeds were hateful; but this last
75Is pitiful, for I unwillingly
76Have given the dear preserver of my life
77Unto his torture. Is it in the power
78Of flesh and blood to carry this, and live?

Offers to kill himself.

Arethusa

79Dear sir, be patient yet! Oh, stay that hand!

King

80Sirs, strip that boy.

Dion

Sirs, strip that boy. Come, sir; your tender flesh
81Will try your constancy.

Bellario

Will try your constancy. Oh, kill me, gentlemen!

Dion

82No. – Help, sirs.

Bellario

No. – Help, sirs. Will you torture me?

King

No. – Help, sirs. Will you torture me? Haste there;
83Why stay you?

Bellario

Why stay you? Then I shall not break my vow,
84You know, just gods, though I discover all.

King

85How's that? Will he confess?

Dion

How's that? Will he confess? Sir, so he says.

King

86Speak then.

Bellario

Speak then. Great King, if you command
87This lord to talk with me alone, my tongue
88Urg'd by my heart, shall utter all the thoughts
89My youth hath known; and stranger things than these
90You hear not often.

King

You hear not often. Walk aside with him.

[Dion and Bellario walk apart.]

Dion

91Why speak'st thou not?

Bellario

Why speak'st thou not? Know you this face, my lord?

Dion

92No.

Bellario

No. Have you not seen it, nor the like?

Dion

93Yes, I have seen the like, but readily
94I know not where.

Bellario

I know not where. I have been often told
95In court of one Euphrasia, a lady,
96And daughter to you; betwixt whom and me
97(They that would flatter my bad face would swear)
98There was such strange resemblance, that we two
99Could not be known asunder, dress'd alike.

Dion

100By Heaven, and so there is!

Bellario

By Heaven, and so there is! For her fair sake,
101Who now doth spend the spring-time of her life
102In holy pilgrimage, move to the king,
103That I may scape this torture.

Dion

That I may scape this torture. But thou speak'st
104As like Euphrasia as thou dost look.
105How came it to thy knowledge that she lives
106In pilgrimage?

Bellario

In pilgrimage? I know it not, my lord;
107But I have heard it, and do scarce believe it.

Dion

108Oh, my shame! is 't possible? Draw near,
109That I may gaze upon thee. Art thou she,
110Or else her murderer? Where wert thou born?

Bellario

111In Syracusa.

Dion

In Syracusa. What's thy name?

Bellario

In Syracusa. What's thy name? Euphrasia.

Dion

112Oh, 't is just, 't is she!
113Now I do know thee. Oh, that thou hadst died,
114And I had never seen thee nor my shame!
115How shall I own thee? Shall this tongue of mine
116E'er call thee daughter more?

Bellario

117Would I had died indeed! I wish it too;
118And so I must have done by vow, ere publish'd
119What I have told, but that there was no means
120To hide it longer. Yet I joy in this,
121The princess is all clear.

King

The princess is all clear. What, have you done?

Dion

122All is discover'd.

Philaster

All is discover'd. Why then hold you me?
123All is discover'd! Pray you, let me go.

He offers to stab himself.

King

124Stay him.

Arethusa

Stay him. What is discover'd?

Dion

Stay him. What is discover'd? Why, my shame.
125It is a woman; let her speak the rest.

Philaster

126How? That again!

Dion

How? That again! It is a woman.

Philaster

Blest be you powers that favour innocence!

King

Lay hold upon that lady.

[Megra is seized.]

Philaster

129It is a woman, sir! – Hark, gentlemen,
130It is a woman! – Arethusa, take
131My soul into thy breast, that would be gone
132With joy. It is a woman! Thou art fair,
133And virtuous still to ages, in despite
134Of malice.

King

135Speak you, where lies his shame?

Bellario

Speak you, where lies his shame? I am his daughter.

Philaster

The gods are just.

Dion

137I dare accuse none; but, before you two,
138The virtue of our age, I bend my knee
139For mercy.

[Kneels.]

Philaster

For mercy. Take it freely; for I know,
140Though what thou didst were undiscreetly done,
141'T was meant well.

Arethusa

'T was meant well. And for me,
142I have a power to pardon sins, as oft
143As any man has power to wrong me.

Cleremont

144Noble and worthy!

Philaster

Noble and worthy! But, Bellario,
145(For I must call thee still so,) tell me why
146Thou didst conceal thy sex. It was a fault,
147A fault, Bellario, though thy other deeds
148Of truth outweigh'd it: all these jealousies
149Had flown to nothing if thou hadst discover'd
150What now we know.

Bellario

What now we know. My father oft would speak
151Your worth and virtue; and, as I did grow
152More and more apprehensive, I did thirst
153To see the man so prais'd. But yet all his
154Was but a maiden-longing, to be lost
155As soon as found; till, sitting in my window,
156Printing my thoughts in lawn, I saw a god,
157I thought, (but it was you,) enter our gates.
158My blood flew out and back again, as fast
159As I had puff'd it forth and suck'd it in
160Like breath. Then was I call'd away in haste
161To entertain you. Never was a man,
162Heav'd from a sheep-cote to a sceptre, rais'd
163So high in thoughts as I. You left a kiss
164Upon these lips then, which I mean to keep
165From you for ever. I did hear you talk,
166Far above singing. After you were gone,
167I grew acquainted with my heart, and search'd
168What stirr'd it so: alas, I found it love!
169Yet far from lust; for, could I but have liv'd
170In presence of you, I had had my end.
171For this I did delude my noble father
172With a feign'd pilgrimage, and dress'd myself
173In habit of a boy; and, for I knew
174My birth no match for you, I was past hope
175Of having you; and, understanding well
176Than when I made discovery of my sex
177I could not stay with you, I made a vow,
178By all the most religious things a maid
179Could call together, never to be known,
180Whilst there was hope to hide me from men's eyes,
181For other than I seem'd, that I might ever
182Abide with you. Then sat I by the fount,
183Where first you took me up.

King

Where first you took me up. Search out a match
184Within our kingdom, where and when thou wilt,
185And I will pay thy dowry; and thyself
186Wilt well deserve him.

Bellario

Wilt well deserve him. Never, sir, will I
187Marry; it is a thing within my vow:
188But, if I may have leave to serve the princess,
189To see the virtues of her lord and her,
190I shall have hope to live.

Arethusa

I shall have hope to live. I, Philaster,
191Cannot be jealous, though you had a lady
192Dress'd like a page to serve you; nor will I
193Suspect her living here. – Come, live with me;
194Live free as I do. She that loves my lord,
195Curs'd be the wife that hates her!

Philaster

196I grieve such virtue should be laid in earth
197Without an heir. – Hear me, my royal father:
198Wrong not the freedom of our souls so much,
199To think to take revenge of that base woman;
200Her malice cannot hurt us. Set her free
201As she was born, saving from shame and sin.

King

202Set her at liberty. – But leave the court;
203This is no place for such. – You, Pharamond,
204Shall have free passage, and a conduct home
205Worthy so great a prince. When you come there,
206Remember 't was your faults that lost you her,
207And not my purpos'd will.

Pharamond

And not my purpos'd will. I do confess,
208Renowed sir.

King

209Last, join your hands in one. Enjoy, Philaster,
210This kingdom, which is yours, and, after me,
211Whatever I call mine. My blessing on you!
212All happy hours be at your marriage-joys,
213That you may grow yourselves over all lands,
214And live to see your plenteous branches spring
215Wherever there is sun! Let princes learn
216By this to rule the passions of their blood;
217For what Heaven wills can never be withstood.

Exeunt omnes.
