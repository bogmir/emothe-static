Philaster, or Love Lies a-Bleeding

<<Actus Primus>>

<<Scena Prima>>

[The Palace.]

Enter Dion, Cleremont, and Thrasiline

Cleremont

Here's nor lords nor ladies.

Dion

Credit me, gentlemen, I wonder at it. They receiv'd strict charge from the King to attend here; besides, it was boldly published that no officer should forbid any gentleman that desired to attend and hear.

Cleremont

Can you guess the cause?

Dion

Sir, it is plain, about the Spanish Prince that's come to marry our kingdom's heir and be our sovereign.

Thrasiline

Many that will seem to know much say she looks not on him like a maid in love.

Dion

Faith, sir, the multitude, that seldom know anything but their own opinions, speak that they would have; but the prince, before his own approach, receiv'd so many confident messages from the state, that I think she's resolv'd to be rul'd.

Cleremont

Sir, it is thought, with her he shall enjoy both these kingdoms of Sicily and Calabria.

Dion

Sir, it is without controversy so meant. But 't will be a troublesome labour for him to enjoy both these kingdoms with safety, the right heir to one of them living, and living so virtuously: especially, the people admiring the bravery of his mind and lamenting his injuries.

Cleremont

Who? Philaster?

Dion

Yes; whose father, we all know, was by our late King of Calabria unrighteously deposed from his fruitful Sicily. Myself drew some blood in those wars, which I would give my hand to be washed from.

Cleremont

Sir, my ignorance in state-policy will not let me know why, Philaster being heir to one of these kingdoms, the King should suffer him to walk abroad with such free liberty.

Dion

Sir, it seems your nature is more constant than to inquire after state-news. But the King, of late, made a hazard of both the kingdoms, of Sicily and his own, with offering but to imprison Philaster; at which the city was in arms, not to be charm'd down by any state-order, or proclamation, till they saw Philaster ride through the streets pleas'd and without a guard; at which they threw their hats and their arms from them; some to make bonfires, some to drink, all for his deliverance: which wise men say is the cause the King labours to bring in the power of a foreign nation to awe his own with.

Enter Galatea, Megra, and a Lady

Thrasiline

See, the ladies! What's the first?

Dion

A wise and modest gentlewoman that attends the princess.

Cleremont

The second?

Dion

She is one that may stand still discreetly enough and ill-favour'dly dance her measure; simper when she is courted by her friend, and slight her husband.

Cleremont

The last?

Dion

Faith, I think she is one whom the state keeps for the agents of our confederate princess; she'll cog and lie with a whole army, before the league shall break. Her name is common through the kingdom, and the trophies of her dishonour advanced beyond Hercules' Pillars. She loves to try the several constitutions of men's bodies; and, indeed, has destroyed the worth of her own body by making experiment upon it for the good of the commonwealth.

Cleremont

She's a profitable member.

Lady

Peace, if you love me! You shall see these gentlemen stand their ground and not court us.

Galatea

What if they should?

Megra

What if they should!

Lady

Nay, let her alone. – What if they should! What, if they should, I say they were never abroad. What foreigner would do so? It writes them directly untravell'd.

Galatea

Why, what if they be?

Megra

What if they be!

Lady

Good madam, let her go on. – What if they be! Why, if they be, I will justify, they cannot maintain discourse with a judicious lady, nor make a leg nor say "Excuse me."

Galatea

Ha, ha, ha!

Lady

Do you laugh, madam?

Dion

You desires upon you, ladies!

Lady

Then you must sit beside us.

Dion

I shall sit near you then, lady.

Lady

Near me, perhaps; but there's a lady endures no stranger; and to me you appear a very strange fellow.

Megra

Methinks he's not so strange; he would quickly be acquainted.

Thrasiline

Peace, the King!

Enter King, Pharamond, Arethusa, and Train

King

97To give a stronger testimony of love
98Than sickly promises (which commonly
99In princes find both birth and burial
100In one breath) we have drawn you, worthy sir,
101To make your fair endearments to our daughter
102And worthy services known to our subjects,
103Now lov'd and wondered at; next, our intent
104To plant you deeply our immediate heir
105Both to our blood and kingdoms. For this lady,
106(The best part of your life, as you confirm me,
107And I believe,) though her few years and sex
108Yet teach her nothing but her fears and blushes,
109Desires without desire, discourse and knowledge
110Only of what herself is to herself,
111Make her feel moderate health; and when she sleeps,
112In making no ill day, knows no ill dreams.
113Think not, dear sir, these undivided parts,
114That must mould up a virgin, are put on
115To show her so, as borrow'd ornaments
116To speak her perfect love to you, or add
117An artificial shadow to her nature, –
118No, sir; I boldly dare proclaim her yet
119No woman. But woo her still, and think her modesty
120A sweeter mistress than the offer'd language
121Of any dame, were she a queen, whose eye
122Speaks common loves and comforts to her servants.
123Last, noble son (for so I now must call you),
124What I have done thus public, is not only
125To add a comfort in particular
126To you or me, but all; and to confirm
127The nobles and the gentry of these kingdoms
128By oath to your succession, which shall be
129Within this month at most.

Thrasiline

130This will be hardly done.

Cleremont

131It must be ill done, if it be done.

Dion

132When 't is at best, 't will be but half done, whilst
133So brave a gentleman's wrong'd and flung off.

Thrasiline

134I fear.

Cleremont

135Who does not?

Dion

136I fear not for myself, and yet I fear too.
137Well, we shall see, we shall see. No more.

Pharamond

138Kissing your white hand, mistress, I take leave
139To thank your royal father; and thus far
140To be my own free trumpet. Understand,
141Great King, and these your subjects, mine that must be,
142(For so deserving you have spoke me, sir,
143And so deserving I dare speak myself,)
144To what a person, of what eminence,
145Ripe expectation, of what faculties,
146Manners and virtues, you should wed your kingdoms:
147You in me have your wishes. Oh, this country!
148By more than all the gods, I hold it happy;
149Happy in their dear memories that have been
150Kings great and good; happy in yours that is;
151And from you (as a chronicle to keep
152Your noble name from eating age) do I
153Opine myself most happy. Gentlemen,
154Believe me in a word, a prince's word,
155There shall be nothing to make up a kingdom
156Mighty and flourishing, defenced, fear'd,
157Equal to be commanded and obeyed,
158But through the travails of my life I'll find it,
159And tie it to this country. By all the gods,
160My reign shall be so easy to the subject,
161That every man shall be his prince himself,
162And his own law – yet I his prince and law.
163And, dearest lady, to your dearest self
164(Dear in the choice of him whose name and lustre
165Must make you more and mightier) let me say,
166You are the blessed'st living; for, sweet princess,
167You shall enjoy a man of men to be
168Your servant; you shall make him yours, for whom
169Great queens must die.

Thrasiline

170Miraculous!

Cleremont

This speech calls him Spaniard, being nothing but a large inventory of his own commendations.

Dion

173I wonder what's his price; for certainly
174He'll sell himself, he has so prais'd his shape.

Enter Philaster

175But here comes one more worthy those large speeches
176Than the large speaker of them.
177Let me be swallow'd quick, if I can find,
178In all the anatomy of yon man's virtues,
179One sinew sound enough to promise for him,
180He shall be constable. By this sun,
181He'll ne'er make king unless it be of trifles,
182In my poor judgment.

Philaster

183[kneeling.] Right noble sir, as low as my obedience
184And with a heart as loyal as my knee,
185I beg your favour.

King

I beg your favour. Rise; you have it, sir.

[Philaster rises.]

Dion

186Mark but the king, how pale he looks with fear!
187Oh, this same whoreson conscience, how it jades us!

King

188Speak your intents, sir.

Philaster

Speak your intents, sir. Shall I speak 'em freely?
189Be still my royal sovereign.

King

Be still my royal sovereign. As a subject,
190We give you freedom.

Dion

We give you freedom. Now it heats.

Philaster

We give you freedom. Now it heats. Then thus I turn
191My language to you, prince, you foreign man!
192Ne'er stare nor put on wonder, for you must
193Endure me, and you shall. This earth you tread upon
194(A dowry, as you hope, with this fair princess),
195By my dead father (oh, I had a father,
196Whose memory I bow to!) was not left
197To your inheritance, and I up and living –
198Having myself about me and my sword,
199The souls of all my name and memories,
200These arms and some few friends besides the gods –
201To part so calmly with it, and sit still
202And say, "I might have been." I tell thee, Pharamond,
203When thou art king, look I be dead and rotten,
204And my name ashes: for, hear me, Pharamond!
205This very ground thou goest on, this fat earth,
206My father's friends made fertile with their faiths,
207Before that day of shame shall gape and swallow
208Thee and thy nation, like a hungry grave,
209Into her hidden bowels. Prince, it shall:
210By the just gods, it shall!

Pharamond

By the just gods, it shall! He's mad beyond cure, mad.

Dion

211Here is a fellow has some fire in 's veins:
212The outlandish prince looks like a tooth-drawer.

Philaster

213Sir Prince of popinjays, I'll make it well
214Appear to you I am not mad.

King

Appear to you I am not mad. You displease us:
215You are too bold.

Philaster

You are too bold. No, sir, I am too tame,
216Too much a turtle, a thing born without passion,
217A faint shadow, that every drunken cloud
218Sails over, and makes nothing.

King

Sails over, and makes nothing. I do not fancy this.
219Call our physicians; sure, he's somewhat tainted.

Thrasiline

220I do not think 't will prove so.

Dion

221H'as given him a general purge already,
222For all the right he has; and now he means
223To let him blood. Be constant, gentlemen:
224By heaven, I'll run his hazard, although I run
225My name out of the kingdom!

Cleremont

My name out of the kingdom! Peace, we are all one soul.

Pharamond

226What you have seen in me to stir offence
227I cannot find, unless it be this lady,
228Offer'd into mine arms with the succession;
229Which I must keep, (though it hath pleas'd your fury
230To mutiny within you,) without disputing
231Your genealogies, or taking knowledge
232Whose branch you are. The king will leave it me,
233And I dare make it mine. You have your answer.

Philaster

234If thou wert sole inheritor to him
235That made the world his, and couldst see no sun
236Shine upon anything but thine; were Pharamond
237As truly valiant as I feel him cold,
238And ring'd among the choicest of his friends,
239(Such as would blush to talk such serious follies,
240Or back such bellied commendations),
241And from this presence, spite of all these bugs,
242You should hear further from me.

King

243Sir, you wrong the prince; I gave you not this freedom
244To brave our best friends. You deserve our frown.
245Go to; be better temper'd.

Philaster

246It must be, sir, when I am nobler us'd.

Galatea

247Ladies,
248This would have been a pattern of succession,
249Had he ne'er met this mischief. By my life,
250He is the worthiest the true name of man
251This day within my knowledge.

Megra

252I cannot tell what you may call your knowledge;
253But the other is the man set in mine eye.
254Oh, 't is a prince of wax!

Galatea

Oh, 't is a prince of wax! A dog it is.

King

255Philaster, tell me
256The injuries you aim at in your riddles.

Philaster

257If you had my eyes, sir, and sufferance,
258My griefs upon you, and my broken fortunes,
259My wants great, and now-nothing hopes and fears,
260My wrongs would make ill riddles to be laugh'd at.
261Dare you be still my king, and right me not?

King

262Give me your wrongs in private.

Philaster

Give me your wrongs in private. Take them,
263And ease me of a load would bow strong Atlas.

They whisper.

Cleremont

He dares not stand the shock.

Dion

I cannot blame him; there's danger in 't. Every man in this age has not a soul of crystal, for all men to read their actions through: men's hearts and faces are so far asunder, that they hold no intelligence. Do but view yon stranger well, and you shall see a fever through all his bravery, and feel him shake like a true tyrant. If he give not back his crown again upon the report of an elder-gun, I have no augury.

King

275Go to;
276Be more yourself, as you respect our favour;
277You'll stir us else. Sir, I must have you know,
278That y' are and shall be, at our pleasure, what
279Fashion we will put upon you. Smooth your brow,
280Or by the gods –

Philaster

281I am dead, sir; y' are my fate. It was not I
282Said I was wrong'd: I carry all about me
283My weak stars lead me to, all my weak fortunes.
284Who dares in all this presence speak, (that is
285But man of flesh, and may be mortal,) tell me
286I do not most entirely love this prince,
287And honour his full virtues!

King

And honour his full virtues! Sure, he's possess'd.

Philaster

288Yes, with my father's spirit. It's here, O King,
289A dangerous spirit! Now he tells me, King,
290I was a king's heir, bids me be a king,
291And whispers to me, these are all my subjects.
292'T is strange he will not let me sleep, but dives
293Into my fancy, and there gives me shapes
294That kneel and do me service, cry me king.
295But I'll suppress him; he's a factious spirit,
296And will undo me. –
297[To Pharamond] Noble sir, your hand;
298I am your servant.

King

I am your servant. Away! I do not like this:
299I'll make you tamer, or I'll dispossess you
300Both of your life and spirit. For this time
301I pardon your wild speech, without so much
302As your imprisonment.

Exeunt King, Pharamond, Arethusa.

Dion

I thank you, sir; you dare not for the people.

Galatea

Ladies, what think you now of this brave fellow?

Megra

A pretty talking fellow, hot at hand. But eye yon stranger: is he not a fine complete gentleman? Oh, these strangers, I do affect them strangely! They do the rarest home-things, and please the fullest! As I live, I could love all the nation over and over for his sake.

Galatea

Gods comfort your poor head-piece, lady! 'T is a weak one, and had need of a night-cap.

Exeunt Ladies.

Dion

314See, how his fancy labours! Has he not
315Spoke home and bravery? What a dangerous train
316Did he give fire to! How he shook the king,
317Made his soul melt within him, and his blood
318Run into whey! It stood upon his brow
319Like a cold winter dew.

Philaster

Like a cold winter dew. Gentlemen,
320You have no suit to me? I am no minion.
321You stand, methinks, like men that would be courtiers,
322If you could well be flatter'd at a price
323Not to undo your children. Y'are all honest:
324Go, get you home again, and make your country
325A virtuous court, to which your great ones may,
326In their diseased age, retire and live recluse.

Cleremont

327How do you, worthy sir?

Philaster

How do you, worthy sir? Well, very well;
328And so well that, if the king please, I find
329I may live many years.

Dion

I may live many years. The king must please,
330Whilst we know what you are and who you are,
331Your wrongs and virtues. Shrink not, worthy sir,
332But add your father to you; in whose name
333We'll waken all the gods, and conjure up
334The rods of vengeance, the abused people,
335Who, like to raging torrents, shall swell high,
336And so begirt the dens of these male-dragons,
337That, through the strongest safety, they shall beg
338For mercy at your sword's point.

Philaster

For mercy at your sword's point. Friends, no more;
339Our ears may be corrupted; 't is an age
340We dare not trust our wills to. Do you love me?

Thrasiline

341Do we love Heaven and honour?

Philaster

342My Lord Dion, you had
343A virtuous gentlewoman call'd you father.
344Is she yet alive?

Dion

Is she yet alive? Most honour'd sir, she is;
345And, for the penance but of an idle dream
346Has undertook a tedious pilgrimage.

Enter a Lady

Philaster

347Is it to me, or any of these gentlemen, you come?

Lady

348To you, brave lord; the princess would entreat
349Your present company.

Philaster

350The princess send for me! Y'are mistaken.

Lady

351If you be call'd Philaster, 't is to you.

Philaster

352Kiss her fair hand, and say I will attend her.

[Exit Lady.]

Dion

353Do you know what you do?

Philaster

354Yes; go to see a woman.

Cleremont

355But do you weigh the danger you are in?

Philaster

356Danger in a sweet face!
357By Jupiter, I must not fear a woman!

Thrasiline

358But are you sure it was the princess sent?
359It may be some foul train to catch your life.

Philaster

360I do not think it, gentlemen; she's noble.
361Her eye may shoot me dead, or those true red
362And white friends in her cheeks may steal my soul out:
363There's all the danger in 't. But, be what may,
364Her single name hath arm'd me.

Exit.

Dion

Her single name hath arm'd me. Go on,
365And be as truly happy as th'art fearless! –
366Come, gentlemen, let's make our friends acquainted,
367Lest the king prove false.

Exeunt.

[SCENE II]

[Arethusa's Apartment.]

Enter Arethusa and a Lady

Arethusa

1Comes he not?

Lady

Comes he not? Madam?

Arethusa

Comes he not? Madam? Will Philaster come?

Lady

2Dear madam, you were wont to credit me
3At first.

Arethusa

4But didst thou tell me so?
5I am forgetful, and my woman's strength
6Is so o'ercharg'd with dangers like to grow
7About my marriage, that these under-things
8Dare not abide in such a troubled sea.
9How look'd he when he told thee he would come?

Lady

10Why, well.

Arethusa

11And not a little fearful?

Lady

12Fear, madam! Sure, he knows not what it is.

Arethusa

13You all are of his faction; the whole court
14Is bold in praise of him; whilst I
15May live neglected, and do noble things,
16As fools in strife throw gold into the sea,
17Drown'd in the doing. But, I know he fears.

Lady

18Fear, madam! Methought, his looks hid more
19Of love than fear.

Arethusa

Of love than fear. Of love! To whom? To you?
20Did you deliver those plain words I sent
21With such a winning gesture and quick look
22That you have caught him?

Lady

That you have caught him? Madam, I mean to you.

Arethusa

23Of love to me! Alas, thy ignorance
24Lets thee not see the crosses of our births!
25Nature, that loves not to be questioned
26Why she did this or that, but has her ends,
27And knows she does well, never gave the world
28Two things so opposite, so contrary,
29As he and I am: if a bowl of blood
30Drawn from this arm of mine would poison thee,
31A draught of his would cure thee. Of love to me!

Lady

32Madam, I think I hear him.

Arethusa

Madam, I think I hear him. Bring him in.

[Exit Lady.]

33You gods, that would not have your dooms withstood,
34Whose holy wisdoms at this time it is
35To make the passion of a feeble maid
36The way unto your justice, I obey.

Enter Philaster [with Lady]

Lady

37Here is my Lord Philaster.

Arethusa

Here is my Lord Philaster. Oh, 't is well.
38Withdraw yourself.

[Exit Lady.]

Philaster

Withdraw yourself. Madam, your messenger
39Made me believe you wish'd to speak with me.

Arethusa

40'T is true, Philaster; but the words are such
41I have to say, and do so ill beseem
42The mouth of woman, that I wish them said,
43And yet am loath to speak them. Have you known
44That I have aught detracted from your worth?
45Have I in person wrong'd you, or have set
46My baser instruments to throw disgrace
47Upon your virtues?

Philaster

Upon your virtues? Never, madam, you.

Arethusa

48Why, then, should you, in such a public place,
49Injure a princess, and a scandal lay
50Upon my fortunes, fam'd to be so great,
51Calling a great part of my dowry in question?

Philaster

52Madam, this truth which I shall speak will be
53Foolish: but, for your fair and virtuous self,
54I could afford myself to have no right
55To anything you wish'd.

Arethusa

To anything you wish'd. Philaster, know,
56I must enjoy these kingdoms.

Philaster

I must enjoy these kingdoms. Madam, both?

Arethusa

57Both, or I die: by heaven, I die, Philaster,
58If I not calmly may enjoy them both.

Philaster

59I would do much to save that noble life;
60Yet would be loath to have posterity
61Find in our stories, that Philaster gave
62His right unto a sceptre and a crown
63To save a lady's longing.

Arethusa

To save a lady's longing. Nay, then, hear:
64I must and will have them, and more –

Philaster

I must and will have them, and more – What more?

Arethusa

65Or lose that little life the gods prepared
66To trouble this poor piece of earth withal.

Philaster

67Madam, what more?

Arethusa

Madam, what more? Turn, then, away thy face.

Philaster

68No.

Arethusa

69No.

Philaster

70I can endure it. Turn away my face!
71I never yet saw enemy that look'd
72So dreadfully, but that I thought myself
73As great a basilisk as he; or spake
74So horrible, but that I thought my tongue
75Bore thunder underneath as much as his;
76Nor beast that I could turn from. Shall I then
77Begin to fear sweet sounds? A lady's voice,
78Whom I do love? Say you would have my life;
79Why, I will give it you; for it is of me
80A thing so loath'd, and unto you that ask
81Of so poor use, that I shall make no price:
82If you entreat, I will unmov'dly hear.

Arethusa

83Yet, for my sake, a little bend thy looks.

Philaster

84I do.

Arethusa

85Then know, I must have them and thee.

Philaster

86And me?

Arethusa

87Thy love; without which, all the land
88Discover'd yet will serve me for no use
89But to be buried in.

Philaster

But to be buried in. Is 't possible?

Arethusa

90With it, it were too little to bestow
91On thee. Now, though thy breath do strike me dead,
92(Which, now, it may,) I have unripp'd my breast.

Philaster

93Madam, you are too full of noble thoughts,
94To lay a train for this contemned life,
95Which you may have for asking. To suspect
96Were base, where I deserve no ill. Love you!
97By all my hopes, I do, above my life!
98But how this passion should proceed from you
99So violently, would amaze a man
100That would be jealous.

Arethusa

101Another soul into my body shot
102Could not have fill'd me with more strength and sprit
103Than this thy breath. But spend not hasty time
104In seeking how I came thus; 't is the gods,
105The gods, that make me so; and, sure, our love
106Will be the nobler and the better blest,
107In that the secret justice of the gods
108Is mingled with it. Let us leave, and kiss;
109Lest some unwelcome guest should fall betwixt us,
110And we should part without it.

Philaster

And we should part without it. 'T will be ill
111I should abide here long.

Arethusa

I should abide here long. 'T is true; and worse
112You should come often. How shall we devise
113To hold intelligence, that our true loves,
114On any new occasion, may agree
115What path is best to tread?

Philaster

What path is best to tread? I have a boy,
116Sent by the gods, I hope, to this intent,
117Not yet seen in the court. Hunting the buck,
118I found him sitting by a fountain's side,
119Of which he borrow'd some to quench his thirst,
120And paid the nymph again as much in tears.
121A garland lay him by, made by himself
122Of many several flowers bred in the vale,
123Stuck in that mystic order that the rareness
124Delighted me: but ever when he turn'd
125His tender eyes upon 'em, he would weep,
126As if he meant to make 'em grow again.
127Seeing such pretty helpless innocence
128Dwell in his face, I ask'd him all his story.
129He told me that his parents gentle died,
130Leaving him to the mercy of the fields,
131Which gave him roots; and of the crystal springs,
132Which did not stop their courses; and the sun,
133Which still, he thank'd him, yielded him his light.
134The took he up his garland, and did show
135What every flower, as country-people hold,
136Did signify, and how all, order'd thus,
137Express'd his grief; and, to my thoughts, did read
138The prettiest lecture of his country-art
139That could be wish'd: so that methought I could
140Have studied it. I gladly entertain'd
141Him, who was glad to follow; and have got
142The trustiest, loving'st, and the gentlest boy
143That ever master kept. Him will I send
144To wait on you, and bear our hidden love.

Enter Lady

Arethusa

145'T is well; no more.

Lady

146Madam, the prince is come to do his service.

Arethusa

147What will you do, Philaster, with yourself?

Philaster

148Why, that which all the gods have pointed out for me.

Arethusa

149Dear, hide thyself. –
150Bring in the prince.

[Exit Lady.]

Philaster

Bring in the prince. Hide me from Pharamond!
151When thunder speaks, which is the voice of Jove,
152Though I do reverence, yet I hide me not;
153And shall a stranger-prince have leave to brag
154Unto a foreign nation, that he made
155Philaster hide himself?

Arethusa

Philaster hide himself? He cannot know it.

Philaster

156Though it should sleep for ever to the world,
157It is a simple sin to hide myself,
158Which will for ever on my conscience lie.

Arethusa

159Then, good Philaster, give him scope and way
160In what he says; for he is apt to speak
161What you are loath to hear. For my sake, do.

Philaster

162I will.

Enter Pharamond

Pharamond

163My princely mistress, as true lovers ought,
164I come to kiss these fair hands, and to show,
165In outward ceremonies, to dear love
166Writ in my heart.

Philaster

167If I shall have an answer no directlier,
168I am gone.

Pharamond

169To what would he have answer?

Arethusa

170To his claim unto the kingdom.

Pharamond

171Sirrah, I forbare you before the king –

Philaster

172Good sir, do so still; I would not talk with you.

Pharamond

173But now the time is fitter. Do but offer
174To make mention of right to any kingdom,
175Though it be scarce habitable –

Philaster

Though it be scarce habitable – Good sir, let me go.

Pharamond

176And by the gods –

Philaster

And by the gods – Peace, Pharamond! if thou –

Arethusa

177Leave us, Philaster.

Philaster

Leave us, Philaster. I have done.

[Going.]

Pharamond

178You are gone! by Heaven I'll fetch you back.

Philaster

179You shall not need.

[Returning.]

Pharamond

You shall not need. What now?

Philaster

You shall not need. What now? Know, Pharamond,
180I loathe to brawl with such a blast as thou,
181Who art nought but a valiant voice; but if
182Thou shalt provoke me further, men shall say,
183Thou wert, and not lament it.

Pharamond

Thou wert, and not lament it. Do you slight
184My greatness so, and in the chamber of
185The princess?

Philaster

186It is a place to which I must confess
187I owe a reverence; but were 't the church,
188Ay, at the altar, there's no place so safe,
189Where thou dar'st injure me, but I dare kill thee.
190And for your greatness, know sir, I can grasp
191You and your greatness thus, thus into nothing.
192Give not a word, not a word back! Farewell.

Exit.

Pharamond

193'T is an odd fellow, madam; we must stop
194His mouth with some office when we are married.

Arethusa

195You were best make him your controller.

Pharamond

196I think he would discharge it well.
197But, madam,
198I hope our hearts are knit; but yet so slow
199The ceremonies of state are, that 't will be long
200Before our hands be so. If then you please,
201Being agreed in heart, let us not wait
202For dreaming form, but take a little stolen
203Delights, and so prevent our joys to come.

Arethusa

204If you dare speak such thoughts,
205I must withdraw in honour.

Exit.

Pharamond

The constitution of my body will never hold out till the wedding; I must seek elsewhere.

Exit.
