<<Actus Quartus>>

<<Scena Prima>>

[The Court.]

Enter King, Pharamond, Arethusa, Galatea, Megra, Dion, Cleremont, Thrasiline, and Attendants

King

1What, are the hounds before and all the woodmen?
2Our horses ready and our bows bent?

Dion

Our horses ready and our bows bent? All, sir.

King

3[to Pharamond.] Y' are cloudy, sir. Come, we have forgotten
4Your venial trespass; let not that sit heavy
5Upon your spirit; here's none dare utter it.

Dion

He looks like an old surfeited stallion after his leaping, dull as a dormouse. See how he sinks! The wench has shot him between wind and water, and, I hope, sprung a leak.

Thrasiline

He needs no teaching, he strikes sure enough. His greatest fault is, he hunts too much in the purlieus; would he would leave off poaching!

Dion

And for his horn, h'as left it at the lodge where he lay late. Oh, he's a precious limehound! Turn him loose upon the pursuit of a lady, and if he lose her, hang him up i' the slip. When my fox-bitch, Beauty, grows proud, I'll borrow him.

King

Is your boy turn'd away?

Arethusa

You did command, sir, and I obey'd you.

King

'T is well done. Hark ye further.

[They talk apart.]

Cleremont

Is 't possible this fellow should repent? Methinks, that were not noble in him; and yet he looks like a mortified member, as if he had a "Sick Man's Salve" in 's mouth. If a worse man had done this fault now, some physical justice or other would presently (without the help of an almanac) have opened the obstructions of his liver, and let him blood with a dog-whip.

Dion

See, see how modestly yon lady looks, as if she came from churching with her neighbours! Why, what a devil can a man see in her face but that she's honest!

Thrasiline

Faith, no great matter to speak of: a foolish twinkling with the eye, that spoils her coat; but he must be a cunning herald that finds it.

Dion

See how they muster one another! Oh, there's a rank regiment where the devil carries the colours and his dam drum-major! Now the world and the flesh come behind with the carriage.

Cleremont

Sure this lady has a good turn done her against her will; before she was common talk, now none dare say cantharides can stir her. Her face looks like a warrant, willing and commanding all tongues, as they will answer it, to be tied up and bolted when this lady means to let herself loose. As I live, she has got her a goodly protection and a gracious; and may use her body discreetly for her health's sake, once a week, excepting Lent and dog-days. Oh, if they were to be got for money, what a great sum would come out of the city for these licences!

King

To horse, to horse! we lose the morning, gentlemen.

Exeunt.

[SCENE II]

[The Forest.]

Enter two Woodmen.

1 Woodman

What, have you lodged the deer?

2 Woodman

Yes, they are ready for the bow.

1 Woodman

Who shoots?

2 Woodman

The princess.

1 Woodman

No, she'll hunt.

2 Woodman

She'll take a stand, I say.

1 Woodman

Who else?

2 Woodman

Why, the young stranger-prince.

1 Woodman

He shall shoot in a stone-bow for me. I never lov'd his beyond-sea-ship since her forsook the say, for paying ten shillings. He was there at the fall of a deer, and would needs (out of his mightiness) give ten groats for the dowcets; marry, his steward would have the velvet-head into the bargain, to turf his hat withal. I think he should love venery; he is an old Sir Tristram; for, if you be rememb'red, he forsook the stag once to strike a rascal miching in a meadow, and her he kill'd in the eye. Who shoots else?

2 Woodman

The Lady Galatea.

1 Woodman

That's a good wench, and she would not chide us for tumbling of her women in the brakes. She's liberal, and by the gods, they say she's honest, and whether that be a fault, I have nothing to do. There's all?

2 Woodman

No, one more; Megra.

1 Woodman

That's a firker, i' faith, boy. There's a wench will ride her haunches as hard after a kennel of hounds as a hunting saddle, and when she comes home, get 'em clapp'd, and all is well again. I have known her lose herself three times in one afternoon (if the woods have been answerable), and it has been work enough for one man to find her, and he has sweat for it. She rides well and she pays well. Hark! let's go.

Exeunt.

Enter Philaster

Philaster

38Oh, that I had been nourish'd in these woods
39With milk of goats and acorns, and not known
40The right of crowns nor the dissembling trains
41Of women's looks; but digg'd myself a cave
42Where I, my fire, my cattle, and my bed
43Might have been shut together in one shed;
44And then had taken me some mountain-girl,
45Beaten with winds, chaste as the harden'd rocks
46Whereon she dwelt, that might have strew'd my bed
47With leaves and reeds, and with the skins of beasts,
48Our neighbours, and have borne at her big breasts
49My large coarse issue! This had been a life
50Free from vexation.

Enter Bellario

Bellario

Free from vexation. Oh, wicked men!
51An innocent may walk safe among beasts;
52Nothing assaults me here. See, my griev'd lord
53Sits as his soul were searching out a way
54To leave his body! – Pardon me, that must
55Break thy last commandment; for I must speak.
56You that are griev'd can pity; hear, my lord!

Philaster

57Is there a creature yet so miserable,
58That I can pity?

Bellario

That I can pity? Oh, my noble lord,
59View my strange fortune, and bestow on me,
60According to your bounty (if my service
61Can merit nothing), so much as may serve
62To keep that little piece I hold of life
63From cold and hunger!

Philaster

From cold and hunger! Is it thou? Be gone!
64Go, sell those misbeseeming clothes thou wear'st,
65And feed thyself with them.

Bellario

66Alas, my lord, I can get nothing for them!
67The silly country-people think 't is treason
68To touch such gay things.

Philaster

To touch such gay things. Now, by the gods, this is
69Unkindly done, to vex me with thy sight.
70Th 'art fallen again to thy dissembling trade;
71How shouldst thou think to cozen me again?
72Remains there yet a plague untried for me?
73Even so thou wept'st, and look'd'st, and spoke'st when first
74I took thee up.
75Curse on the time! If thy commanding tears
76Can work on any other, use thy art;
77I'll not betray it. Which way wilt thou take,
78That I may shun thee, for thine eyes are poison
79To mine, and I am loath to grow in rage?
80This way, or that way?

Bellario

81Any will serve; but I will choose to have
82That path in chase that leads unto my grave.

Exeunt severally.

Enter [on one side] Dion, and [on the other] the Woodmen

Dion

This is the strangest sudden chance! – You, woodmen!

1 Woodman

My lord Dion?

Dion

Saw you a lady come this way on a sable horse studded with stars of white?

2 Woodman

Was she not young and tall?

Dion

Yes. Rode she to the wood or to the plain?

2 Woodman

Faith, my lord, we saw none.

Exeunt Woodmen.

Dion

Pox of your questions then! Enter Cleremont What, is she found?

Cleremont

Nor will be, I think.

Dion

Let him seek his daughter himself. She cannot stray about a little necessary natural business, but the whole court must be in arms. When she has done, we shall have peace.

Cleremont

There's already a thousand fatherless tales amongst us. Some say, her horse ran away with her; some, a wolf pursued her; others, 't was a plot to kill her, and that arm'd men were seen in the wood: but questionless she rode away willingly.

Enter King and Thrasiline

King

105Where is she?

Cleremont

Where is she? Sir, I cannot tell.

King

Where is she? Sir, I cannot tell. How's that?
106Answer me so again!

Cleremont

Answer me so again! Sir, shall I lie?

King

107Yes, lie and damn, rather than tell me that.
108I say again, where is she? Mutter not –
109Sir, speak you; where is she?

Dion

Sir, speak you; where is she? Sir, I do not know.

King

110Speak that again so boldly, and, by Heaven,
111It is thy last! – You, fellows, answer me;
112Where is she? Mark me, all; I am your king:
113I wish to see my daughter; show her me;
114I do command you all, as you are subjects,
115To show her me! What! am I not your king?
116If ay, then am I not to be obeyed?

Dion

117Yes, if you command things possible and honest.

King

118Things possible and honest! Hear me, thou, –
119Thou traitor, that dar'st confine thy king to things
120Possible and honest! Show her me,
121Or, let me perish, if I cover not
122All Sicily with blood!

Dion

All Sicily with blood! Faith, I cannot,
123Unless you tell me where she is.

King

124You have betray'd me; you have let me lose
125The jewel of my life. Go, bring her me.
126And set her here before me. 'T is the king
127Will have it so; whose breath can still the winds,
128Uncloud the sun, charm down the swelling sea,
129And stop the floods of heaven. Speak, can it not?

Dion

130No.

King

No. No! cannot the breath of kings do this?

Dion

131No; nor smell sweet itself, if once the lungs
132Be but corrupted.

King

Be but corrupted. Is it so? Take heed!

Dion

133Sir, take you heed how you dare the powers
134That must be just.

King

That must be just. Alas! what are we kings?
135Why do you gods place us above the rest,
136To be serv'd, flatter'd, and ador'd, till we
137Believe we hold within our hands your thunder?
138And when we come to try the power we have,
139There's not a leaf shakes at our threatenings.
140I have sinn'd, 't is true, and here stand to be punish'd;
141Yet would not thus be punish'd. Let me choose
142My way, and lay it on!

Dion

He articles with the gods. performance of covenants betwixt them!

Enter Pharamond, Galatea, and Megra

King

146What, is she found?

Pharamond

What, is she found? No; we have ta'en her horse;
147He gallop'd empty by. There is some treason.
148You, Galatea, rode with her into
149The wood. Why left you her?

Galatea

The wood. Why left you her? She did command me.

King

Command! you should not.

Galatea

151'T would ill become my fortunes and my birth
152To disobey the daughter of my king.

King

153Y' are all cunning to obey us for our hurt;
154But I will have her.

Pharamond

But I will have her. If I have her not,
155By this hand, there shall be no more Sicily.

Dion

156What, will he carry it to Spain in 's pocket?aparte

Pharamond

157I will not leave one man alive, but the king,
158A cook, and a tailor.

Dion

Yes; you may do well to spare for a spawner.

King

I see the injuries I done must be reveng'd.

Dion

Sir, this is not the way to find her out.

King

166Run all, disperse yourselves. The man that finds her,
167Or (if she be kill'd) the traitor, I'll make him great.

Dion

I know some would give five thousand pounds to find her.

Pharamond

Come, let us seek.

King

170Each man a several way; here I myself.

Dion

Come, gentlemen, we here.

Cleremont

Lady, you must go search too.

Megra

I had rather be search'd myself.

Exeunt omnes.

[SCENE III]

[Another part of the Forest.]

Enter Arethusa

Arethusa

1Where am I now? Feet, find me out a way,
2Without the counsel of my troubled head.
3I'll follow you boldly about these woods,
4O'er mountains, thorough brambles, pits, and floods.
5Heaven, I hope, will ease me: I am sick.

Sits down.

Enter Bellario

Bellario

6Yonder 's my lady. God knows I want nothing,aparte
7Because I do not wish to live; yet I
8Will try her charity. – Oh hear, you that have plenty!
9From that flowing store drop some on dry ground. – See,
10The lively red is gone to guard her heart!
11I fear she faints. She breathes not. –aparte
12Open once more those rosy twins, and send
13Unto my lord your latest farewell! – Oh, she stirs.aparte
14How is it, Madam? Speak comfort.

Arethusa

How is it, Madam? Speak comfort. 'T is not gently done,
15To put me in a miserable life,
16And hold me there. I prithee, let me go;
17I shall do best without thee; I am well.

Enter Philaster

Philaster

18I am to blame to be so much in rage.
19I'll tell her coolly when and where I heard
20This killing truth. I will be temperate
21I speaking, and as just in hearing. –
22Oh, monstrous! Tempt me not, you gods! good gods,
23Tempt not a frail man! What's he, that has a heart,
24But he must ease it here!

Bellario

25My lord, help, help! The princess!

Arethusa

26I am well: forbear.

Philaster

27Let me love lightning, let me be embrac'daparte
28And kiss'd by scorpions, or adore the eyes
29Of basilisks, rather than trust the tongues
30Of hell-bred women! Some good god look down,
31And shrink these veins up! Stick me here a stone,
32Lasting to ages in the memory
33Of this damn'd act!aparte
34You have put hills of fire into this breast,
35Not to be quench'd with tears; for which may guilt
36Sit on your bosoms! At your meals and beds
37Despair await you! What, before my face?
38Poison of asps between your lips! Diseases
39Be your best issues! Nature make a curse,
40And throw it on you!

Arethusa

And throw it on you! Dear Philaster, leave
41To be enrag'd, and hear me.

Philaster

To be enrag'd, and hear me. I have done;
42Forgive my passion. Not the calmed sea,
43When Æolus locks up his windy brood,
44Is less disturb'd than I. I'll make you know't.
45Dear Arethusa, do but take this sword,

Offers his drawn sword.

46And search how temperate a heart I have;
47Then you and this your boy may live and reign
48In lust without control. – Wilt thou, Bellario?
49I prithee kill me; thou art poor, and may'st
50Nourish ambitious thoughts; when I am dead,
51Thy way were freer. Am I raging now?
52If I were mad, I should desire to live.
53Sirs, feel my pulse, whether you have known
54A man in a more equal tune to die.

Bellario

55Alas, my lord, your pulse keeps madman's time!
56So does your tongue.

Philaster

So does your tongue. You will not kill me, then?

Arethusa

57Kill you?

Bellario

Kill you? Not for the world.

Philaster

Kill you? Not for the world. I blame not thee,
58Bellario; thou hast done but that which gods
59Would have transform'd themselves to do. Be gone,
60Leave me without reply; this is the last
61Of all our meetings –

(Exit Bellario.)

Of all our meetings – Kill me with this sword;
62Be wise, or worse will follow: we are two
63Earth cannot bear at once. Resolve to do,
64Or suffer.

Arethusa

65If my fortune be so good to let me fall
66Upon thy hands, I shall have peace in death.
67Yet tell me this, will there be no slanders,
68No jealousy in the other world; no ill there?

Philaster

69No.

Arethusa

70Show me, then, the way.

Philaster

71Then guide my feeble hand,
72You that have power to do it, for I must
73Perform a piece of justice! – If your youth
74Have any way offended Heaven, let prayers
75Short and effectual reconcile you to it.

Arethusa

76I am prepared.

Enter a Country Fellow

Country Fellow

I'll see the king, if he be in the forest; I have hunted him these two hours. If I should come home and not see him, my sisters would laugh at me. I can see nothing but people better hors'd than myself, that outride me; I can hear nothing but shouting. These kings had need of good brains; this whooping is able to put a mean man out of his wits. There's a courtier with his sword drawn; by this hand, upon a woman, I think!

Philaster

87Are you at peace?

Arethusa

Are you at peace? With heaven and earth.

Philaster

May they divide thy soul and body!

Philaster wounds her.

Country Fellow

Hold, dastard! strike a woman! Th' art a craven. I warrant thee, thou wouldst be loath to play half a dozen venies at wasters with a god fellow for a broken head.

Philaster

Leave us, good friend,

Arethusa

94What ill-bred man art thou, to intrude thyself
95Upon our private sports, our recreations?

Country Fellow

96God 'uds me, I understand you not; but
97I know the rogue has hurt you.

Philaster

98Pursue thy own affairs: it will be ill
99To multiply blood upon my head; which thou
100Wilt force me to.

Country Fellow

I know not your rhetoric; but I can lay it on, if you touch the woman.

Philaster

103Slave, take what thou deservest!

They fight.

Arethusa

Slave, take what thou deservest! Heaven guard my lord!

Country Fellow

Oh, do you breathe?

Philaster

105I hear the tread of people. I am hurt.
106The gods take part against me: could this boor
107Have held me thus else? I must shift for life,
108Though I do loathe it. I would find a course
109To lose it rather by my will than force.

Exit.

Country Fellow

I cannot follow the rogue. I pray thee, wench, come and kiss me now.

Enter Pharamond, Dion, Cleremont, Thrasiline, and Woodmen

Pharamond

What art thou?

Country Fellow

Almost kill'd I am for a foolish woman; a knave has hurt her.

Pharamond

The princess, gentlemen! – Where's the wound, madam? Is it dangerous?

Arethusa

He has not hurt me.

Country Fellow

118By God, she lies; h'as hurt her in the breast:
119Look else.

Pharamond

Look else. O sacred spring of innocent blood!

Dion

120'Tis above wonder! Who should dare this?

Arethusa

121I felt it not.

Pharamond

122Speak, villain, who has hurt the princess?

Country Fellow

123Is it the princess?

Dion

124Ay.

Country Fellow

125Then I have seen something yet.

Pharamond

126But who has hurt her?

Country Fellow

127I told you, a rogue; I ne'er saw him before, I.

Pharamond

128Madam, who did it?

Arethusa

Madam, who did it? Some dishonest wretch;
129Alas, I know him not, and do forgive him!

Country Fellow

130He's hurt too; he cannot go far;
131I made my father's old fox fly about his ears.

Pharamond

132How will you have me kill him?

Arethusa

133Not at all; 't is some distracted fellow.

Pharamond

By this hand, I'll leave ne'er a piece of him bigger than a nut, and bring him all to you in my hat.

Arethusa

137Nay, good sir,
138If you do take him, bring him quick to me,
139And I will study for a punishment
140Great as his fault.

Pharamond

141I will.

Arethusa

I will. But swear.

Pharamond

I will. But swear. By all my love, I will. –
142Woodmen, conduct the princess to the king,
143And bear that wounded fellow to dressing. –
144Come, gentlemen, we'll follow the chase close.

Exeunt [on one side] Pharamond, Dion, Cleremont, and Thrasiline; [exit on the other] Arethusa [attended by] 1 Woodman

Country Fellow

I pray you, friend, let me see the king.

2 Woodman

That you shall, and receive thanks.

Country Fellow

If I get clear of this, I'll go see no more gay sights.

Exeunt.

[SCENE IV]

[Another part of the Forest.]

Enter Bellario

Bellario

1A heaviness near death sits on my brow,
2And I must sleep. Bear me, thou gentle bank,
3For ever, if thou wilt. You sweet ones all,

[Lies down.]

4Let me unworthy press you; I could wish
5I rather were a corse strew'd o'er with you
6Than quick above you. Dulness shuts mine eyes,
7And I am giddy; oh, that I could take
8So sound a sleep that I might never wake!

[Sleeps.]

Enter Philaster

Philaster

9I have done ill; my conscience calls me false
10To strike at her that would not strike at me.
11When I did fight, methought I heard her pray
12The gods to guard me. She may be abus'd,
13And I a loathed villain; if she be,
14She will conceal who hurt her. He has wounds
15And cannot follow; neither knows he me.
16Who's this? Bellario sleeping! If thou be'st
17Guilty, there is no justice that thy sleep
18Should be so sound, and mine, whom thou hast wrong'd,
19So broken.
20(Cry within.) Hark! I am pursued. You gods,
21I'll take this offer'd means of my escape.
22They have no mark to know me by my blood,
23If she be true; if false, let mischief light
24On all the world at once! Sword, print my wounds
25Upon this sleeping boy! I ha' none, I think,
26Are mortal, nor would I lay greater on thee.

He wounds him.

Bellario

27Oh, death, I hope, is come! Blest be that hand!
28It meant me well. Again, for pity's sake!

Philaster

29I have caught myself;

Falls.

30The loss of blood hath stay'd my flight. Here, here,
31Is he that struck thee: take thy full revenge;
32Use me, as I did mean thee, worse than death;
33I'll teach thee to revenge. This luckless hand
34Wounded the princess; tell my followers
35Thou didst receive these hurts in staying me,
36And I will second thee; get a reward.

Bellario

37Fly, fly, my lord and save yourself!

Philaster

Fly, fly, my lord and save yourself! How's this?
38Wouldst thou I should be safe?

Bellario

Wouldst thou I should be safe? Else were it vain
39For me to live. These little wounds I have
40Ha' not bled much. Reach me that noble hand;
41I'll help to cover you.

Philaster

I'll help to cover you. Art thou true to me?

Bellario

42Or let me perish loath'd! Come, my good lord,
43Creep in amongst those bushes; who does know
44But that the gods may save your much-lov'd breath?

Philaster

45Then I shall die for grief, if not for this,
46That I have wounded thee. What wilt thou do?

Bellario

Shift for myself well. Peace! I hear 'em come.

[Philaster creeps into a bush.]

[Voices]

[within] Follow, follow, follow! that way they went.

Bellario

51With my own wounds I'll bloody my own sword.
52I need not counterfeit to fall; Heaven knows
53That I can stand no longer.

Falls.

Enter Pharamond, Dion, Cleremont, Thrasiline

Pharamond

54To this place we have track'd him by his blood.

Cleremont

55Yonder, my lord, creeps one away.

Dion

56Stay, sir! what are you?

Bellario

57A wretched creature, wounded in these woods
58By beasts. Relieve me, if your names be me,
59Or I shall perish.

Dion

Or I shall perish. This is he, my lord,
60Upon my soul, that hurt her. 'T is the boy,
61That wicked boy, that serv'd her.

Pharamond

That wicked boy, that serv'd her. Oh, thou damn'd
62In thy creation! What cause couldst thou shape
63To hurt the princess?

Bellario

To hurt the princess? Then I am betray'd.

Dion

64Betray'd! No, apprehended.

Bellario

Betray'd! No, apprehended. I confess
65(Urge it no more) that, big with evil thoughts,
66I set upon her, and did make my aim
67Her death. For charity let fall at once
68The punishment you mean, and do not load
69This weary flesh with tortures.

Pharamond

This weary flesh with tortures. I will know
70Who hir'd thee to this deed.

Bellario

Who hir'd thee to this deed. Mine own revenge.

Pharamond

71Revenge! for what?

Bellario

Revenge! for what? It pleas'd her to receive
72Me as her page and, when my fortunes ebb'd,
73That men strid o'er them careless, she did shower
74Her welcome graces on me, and did swell
75My fortunes till they overflow'd their banks,
76Threat'ning the men that cross'd 'em; when, as swift
77As storms arise at sea, she turn'd her eyes
78To burning suns upon me, and did dry
79The streams she had bestow'd, leaving me worse
80And more contemn'd than other little brooks,
81Because I had been great. In short, I knew
82I could not live, and therefore did desire
83To die reveng'd.

Pharamond

To die reveng'd. If tortures can be found
84Long as thy natural life, resolve to feel
85The utmost rigour.

Philaster creeps out of a bush.

Cleremont

The utmost rigour. Help to lead him hence.

Philaster

86Turn back, you ravishers of innocence!
87Know ye the price of that you bear away
88So rudely?

Pharamond

So rudely? Who's that?

Dion

So rudely? Who's that? 'T is the Lord Philaster.

Philaster

89'T is not the treasure of all kings in one,
90The wealth of Tagus, nor the rocks of pearl
91That pave the court of Neptune, can weigh down
92That virtue. It was I that hurt the princess.
93Place me, some god, upon a pyramis
94Higher than hills of earth, and lend a voice
95Loud as your thunder to me, that from thence
96I may discourse to all the under-world
97The worth that dwells in him!

Pharamond

The worth that dwells in him! How's this?

Bellario

The worth that dwells in him! How's this? My lord, some man
98Weary of life, that would be glad to die.

Philaster

99Leave these untimely courtesies, Bellario.

Bellario

100Alas, he's mad! Come, will you lead me on?

Philaster

101By all the oaths that men ought most to keep,
102And gods to punish most when men do break,
103He touch'd her not. – Take heed, Bellario,
104How thou dost drown the virtues thou hast shown
105With perjury. – By all that's good, 't was I!
106You know she stood betwixt me and my right.

Pharamond

107Thy own tongue be thy judge!

Cleremont

Thy own tongue be thy judge! It was Philaster.

Dion

108Is 't not a brave boy?
109Well, sirs, I fear me we were all deceived.

Philaster

110Have I no friend here?

Dion

Have I no friend here? Yes.

Philaster

Have I no friend here? Yes. Then show it: some
111Good body lend a hand to draw us nearer.
112Would you have tears shed for you when you die?
113Then lay me gently on his neck, that there
114I may weep floods and breathe forth my spirit.
115'T is not the wealth of Plutus, nor the gold.

[Embraces Bellario.]

116Lock'd in the heart of earth, can buy away
117This arm-full from me; this had been a ransom
118To have redeem'd the great Augustus Cæsar,
119Had he been taken. You hard-hearted men,
120More stony than these mountains, can you see
121Such clear pure blood drop, and not cut your flesh
122To stop his life, to bind whose bitter wounds,
123Queens ought to tear their hair, and with their tears
124Bathe 'em? – Forgive me, thou that art the wealth
125Of poor Philaster!

Enter King, Arethusa, and Guard

King

Of poor Philaster! Is the villain ta'en?

Pharamond

126Sir, here be two confess the deed; but sure
127It was Philaster.

Philaster

It was Philaster. Question it no more;
128It was.

King

129The fellow that did fight with him
130Will tell us that.

Arethusa

Will tell us that. Aye me! I know he will.

King

131Did not you know him?

Arethusa

Did not you know him? Sir, if it was he,
132He was disguis'd.

Philaster

He was disguis'd. Oh, my stars,aparte
133That I should live still.aparte

King

That I should live still. Thou ambitious fool,
134Thou that hast laid a train for thy own life! –
135Now I do mean to do, I'll leave to talk.
136Bear them to prison.

Arethusa

137Sir, they did plot together to take hence
138This harmless life; should it pass unreveng'd,
139I should to earth go weeping. Grant me, then,
140By all the love a father bears his child,
141Their custodies, and that I may appoint
142Their tortures and their deaths.

Dion

143Death! Soft; our law will not reach that for this fault.

King

144'T is granted; take 'em to you with a guard. –
145Come, princely Pharamond, this business past,
146We may with more security go on
147To your intended match.

[Exeunt all except Dion, Ceremont, and Thrasiline.]

Cleremont

I pray that this action lose not Philaster the hearts of the people.

Dion

Fear it not; their over-wise heads will think it but a trick.

Exeunt.
