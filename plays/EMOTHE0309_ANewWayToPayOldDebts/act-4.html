4.1

[Enter] Lovell [and] Allworth.

LOVELL

1'Tis well; give me my cloak. I now discharge you
2From further service. Mind your own affairs;
3I hope they will prove successful.

ALLWORTH

I hope they will prove successful. What is blest
4With your good wish, my lord, cannot but prosper.
5Let aftertimes report, and to your honor,
6How much I stand engaged, for I want language
7To speak my debt. Yet if a tear or two
8Of joy for your much goodness can supply
9My tongue's defects, I could—

LOVELL

My tongue's defects, I could— Nay, do not melt.
10This ceremonial thanks to me's superfluous.

OVERREACH

This ceremonial thanks to me's superfluous. (within) Is my lord stirring?

LOVELL

11'Tis he. Oh, here's your letter.
'Tis he. Oh, here's your letter. [He gives Allworth a letter.] Let him in.

Enter Overreach, Greedy, [and] Marall.

OVERREACH

12A good day to my lord!

LOVELL

A good day to my lord! You are an early riser,
13Sir Giles.

OVERREACH

Sir Giles. And reason: to attend Your Lordship.

LOVELL

14And you too, Master Greedy, up so soon?

GREEDY

15In troth, my lord, after the sun is up
16I cannot sleep, for I have a foolish stomach
17That croaks for breakfast. With Your Lordship's favor,
18I have a serious question to demand
19Of my worthy friend Sir Giles.

LOVELL

Of my worthy friend Sir Giles. Pray you use your pleasure.

GREEDY

20How far, Sir Giles—and pray you, answer me
21Upon your credit—hold you it to be
22From your manor house to this of my Lady Allworth's?

OVERREACH

23Why, some four mile.

GREEDY

Why, some four mile. How! Four mile? Good Sir Giles,
24Upon your reputation, think better,
25For, if you do abate but one half-quarter
26Of five, you do yourself the greatest wrong
27That can be in the world; for four miles riding
28Could not have raised so huge an appetite
29As I feel gnawing on me.

MARALL

As I feel gnawing on me. Whether you ride
30Or go afoot, you are that way still provided,
31An it please Your Worship.

OVERREACH

An it please Your Worship. How now, sirrah? Prating
32Before my lord? No difference? Go to my nephew;
33See all his debts discharged, and help His Worship
34To fit on his rich suit.

MARALL

To fit on his rich suit. I may fit you too;aparte
35Tossed like a dog still!aparte

Exit Marall.

LOVELL

Tossed like a dog still! I have writ this morning
36A few lines to my mistress, your fair daughter.

OVERREACH

37'Twill fire her, for she's wholly yours already.—
38[Giving a ring] Sweet Master Allworth, take my ring—'twill carry you
39To her presence, I dare warrant you—and there plead
40For my good lord, if you shall find occasion.
41That done, pray ride to Nottingham; get a license,
42Still by this token.—I'll have it dispatched,
43And suddenly, my lord, that I may say
44My "Honorable," nay, "Right Honorable" daughter.

GREEDY

45[to Allworth] Take my advice, young gentleman; get your breakfast.
46'Tis unwholesome to ride fasting. I'll eat with you,
47And eat to purpose.

OVERREACH

And eat to purpose. Some Fury's in that gut!
48Hungry again! Did you not devour this morning
49A shield of brawn and a barrel of Colchester oysters?

GREEDY

50Why that was, sir, only to scour my stomach—
51A kind of a preparative. [To Allworth] Come, gentleman,
52I will not have you feed like the hangman of Flushing,
53Alone, while I am here.

LOVELL

Alone, while I am here. [to Allworth] Haste your return.

ALLWORTH

54I will not fail, my lord.

GREEDY

I will not fail, my lord. Nor I, to line
55My Christmas coffer.

Exeunt Greedy and Allworth.

OVERREACH

My Christmas coffer. To my wish, we are private.
56I come not to make offer with my daughter
57A certain portion; that were poor and trivial.
58In one word, I pronounce all that is mine,
59In lands or leases, ready coin or goods,
60With her, my lord, comes to you; nor shall you have
61One motive to induce you to believe
62I live too long, since every year I'll add
63Something unto the heap which shall be yours too.

LOVELL

64You are a right kind father.

OVERREACH

You are a right kind father. You shall have reason
65To think me such. How do you like this seat?
66It is well wooded and well watered, the acres
67Fertile and rich. Would it not serve for change
68To entertain your friends in a summer progress?
69What thinks my noble lord?

LOVELL

What thinks my noble lord? 'Tis a wholesome air,
70And well-built pile, and she that's mistress of it
71Worthy the large revenue.

OVERREACH

Worthy the large revenue. She the mistress?
72It may be so for a time; but let my lord
73Say only that he likes it and would have it,
74I say, ere long 'tis his.

LOVELL

I say, ere long 'tis his. Impossible.

OVERREACH

75You do conclude too fast, not knowing me,
76Nor the engines that I work by. 'Tis not alone
77The Lady Allworth's lands, for those once Wellborn's
78(As, by her dotage on him, I know they will be)
79Shall soon be mine; but point out any man's
80In all the shire, and say they lie convenient
81And useful for Your Lordship, and once more
82I say aloud, they are yours.

LOVELL

I say aloud, they are yours. I dare not own
83What's by unjust and cruel means extorted;
84My fame and credit are more dear to me
85Than so to expose 'em to be censured by
86The public voice.

OVERREACH

The public voice. You run, my lord, no hazard.
87Your reputation shall stand as fair
88In all good men's opinions as now;
89Nor can my actions, though condemned for ill,
90Cast any foul aspersion upon yours.
91For, though I do contemn report myself
92As a mere sound, I still will be so tender
93Of what concerns you in all points of honor
94That the immaculate whiteness of your fame
95Nor your unquestioned integrity
96Shall e'er be sullied with one taint or spot
97That may take from your innocence and candor.
98All my ambition is to have my daughter
99Right Honorable, which my lord can make her.
100And, might I live to dance upon my knee
101A young Lord Lovell, born by her unto you,
102I write nil ultra to my proudest hopes.
103As for possessions and annual rents
104Equivalent to maintain you in the port
105Your noble birth and present state requires,
106I do remove that burthen from your shoulders
107And take it on mine own; for, though I ruin
108The country to supply your riotous waste,
109The scourge of prodigals—want—shall never find you.

LOVELL

110Are you not frighted with the imprecations
111And curses of whole families made wretched
112By your sinister practices?

OVERREACH

By your sinister practices? Yes, as rocks are
113When foamy billows split themselves against
114Their flinty ribs, or as the moon is moved
115When wolves, with hunger pined, howl at her brightness.
116I am of a solid temper, and, like these,
117Steer on a constant course. With mine own sword,
118If called into the field, I can make that right
119Which fearful enemies murmured at as wrong.
120Now, for these other piddling complaints
121Breathed out in bitterness, as when they call me
122Extortioner, tyrant, cormorant, or intruder
123On my poor neighbor's right, or grand encloser
124Of what was common to my private use;
125Nay, when my ears are pierced with widows' cries,
126And undone orphans wash with tears my threshold:
127I only think what 'tis to have my daughter
128Right Honorable, and 'tis a powerful charm
129Makes me insensible of remorse, or pity,
130Or the least sting of conscience.

LOVELL

Or the least sting of conscience. I admire
131The toughness of your nature.

OVERREACH

The toughness of your nature. 'Tis for you,
132My lord, and for my daughter, I am marble.
133Nay, more: if you will have my character
134In little, I enjoy more true delight
135In my arrival to my wealth these dark
136And crooked ways than you shall e'er take pleasure
137In spending what my industry hath compassed.
138My haste commands me hence. In one word therefore:
139Is it a match?

LOVELL

Is it a match? I hope that is past doubt now.

OVERREACH

140Then rest secure. Not the hate of all mankind here,
141Nor fear of what can fall on me hereafter,
142Shall make me study aught but your advancement
143One story higher. An earl, if gold can do it!
144Dispute not my religion nor my faith.
145Though I am borne thus headlong by my will,
146You may make choice of what belief you please;
147To me they are equal. So, my lord, good morrow.

Exit.

LOVELL

148He's gone. I wonder how the earth can bear
149Such a portent! I, that have lived a soldier,
150And stood the enemy's violent charge undaunted,
151To hear this blasphemous beast am bathed all over
152In a cold sweat. Yet, like a mountain, he,
153Confirmed in atheistical assertions,
154Is no more shaken than Olympus is
155When angry Boreas loads his double head
156With sudden drifts of snow.

Enter Amble, Lady [Allworth, and Waiting-]woman.

LADY ALLWORTH

With sudden drifts of snow. Save you, my lord!
157Disturb I not your privacy?

LOVELL

Disturb I not your privacy? No, good madam.
158For your own sake I am glad you came no sooner,
159Since this bold, bad man, Sir Giles Overreach,
160Made such a plain discovery of himself,
161And read this morning such a devilish matins
162That I should think it a sin next to his
163But to repeat it.

LADY ALLWORTH

But to repeat it. I ne'er pressed, my lord,
164On others' privacies; yet, against my will,
165Walking for health sake in the gallery
166Adjoining to your lodgings, I was made
167(So vehement and loud he was) partaker
168Of his tempting offers.

LOVELL

Of his tempting offers. Please you to command
169Your servants hence, and I shall gladly hear
170Your wiser counsel.

LADY ALLWORTH

Your wiser counsel. 'Tis, my lord, a woman's,
171But true and hearty. [To her servants] Wait in the next room,
172But be within call, yet not so near to force me
173To whisper my intents.

AMBLE

To whisper my intents. We are taught better
174By you, good madam.

WAITING-WOMAN

By you, good madam. And well know our distance.

LADY ALLWORTH

175Do so, and talk not; 'twill become your breeding.

Exeunt Amble and [Waiting-]woman.

176Now, my good lord—if I may use my freedom
177As to an honored friend?

LOVELL

As to an honored friend? You lessen else
178Your favor to me.

LADY ALLWORTH

Your favor to me. I dare then say thus:
179As you are noble, howe'er common men
180Make sordid wealth the object and sole end
181Of their industrious aims, 'twill not agree
182With those of eminent blood, who are engaged
183More to prefer their honors than to increase
184The state left to 'em by their ancestors,
185To study large additions to their fortunes
186And quite neglect their births; though I must grant
187Riches well got to be a useful servant,
188But a bad master.

LOVELL

But a bad master. Madam, 'tis confessed.
189But what infer you from it?

LADY ALLWORTH

But what infer you from it? This, my lord:
190That as all wrongs, though thrust into one scale,
191Slide of themselves off when right fills the other,
192And cannot bide the trial, so all wealth
193(I mean if ill acquired), cemented to honor
194By virtuous ways achieved and bravely purchased,
195Is but as rubbage poured into a river
196(Howe'er intended to make good the bank),
197Rend'ring the water that was pure before
198Polluted and unwholesome. I allow
199The heir of Sir Giles Overreach, Margaret,
200A maid well qualified, and the richest match
201Our north part can make boast of; yet she cannot,
202With all that she brings with her, fill their mouths
203That never will forget who was her father,
204Or that my husband Allworth's lands and Wellborn's
205(How wrung from both needs now no repetition)
206Were real motives that more worked Your Lordship
207To join your families than her form and virtues.
208You may conceive the rest.

LOVELL

You may conceive the rest. I do, sweet madam,
209And long since have considered it. I know,
210The sum of all that makes a just man happy
211Consists in the well choosing of his wife;
212And there, well to discharge it, does require
213Equality of years, of birth, of fortune.
214For beauty, being poor and not cried up
215By birth or wealth, can truly mix with neither;
216And wealth, where there's such difference in years
217And fair descent, must make the yoke uneasy.
218But I come nearer.

LADY ALLWORTH

But I come nearer. Pray you do, my lord.

LOVELL

219Were Overreach' states thrice centupled, his daughter
220Millions of degrees much fairer than she is,
221Howe'er I might urge precedents to excuse me,
222I would not so adulterate my blood
223By marrying Margaret, and so leave my issue
224Made up of several pieces, one part scarlet
225And the other London blue. In my own tomb
226I will inter my name first.

LADY ALLWORTH

I will inter my name first. I am glad to hear this.aparte
227[Aloud] Why then, my lord, pretend your marriage to her?
228Dissimulation but ties false knots
229On that straight line by which you hitherto
230Have measured all your actions.

LOVELL

Have measured all your actions. I make answer,
231And aptly, with a question. Wherefore have you,
232That since your husband's death have lived a strict
233And chaste nun's life, on the sudden giv'n yourself
234To visits and entertainments? Think you, madam,
235'Tis not grown public conference? Or the favors
236Which you too prodigally have thrown on Wellborn,
237Being too reserved before, incur not censure?

LADY ALLWORTH

238I am innocent here, and on my life I swear
239My ends are good.

LOVELL

My ends are good. On my soul, so are mine
240To Margaret; but leave both to the event.
241And since this friendly privacy does serve
242But as an offered means unto ourselves
243To search each other farther—you having shown
244Your care of me, I my respect to you—
245Deny me not, but still in chaste words, madam,
246An afternoon's discourse.

LADY ALLWORTH

An afternoon's discourse. So I shall hear you.

[Exeunt.]

4.2

[Enter] Tapwell [and] Froth.

TAPWELL

1Undone, undone! This was your counsel, Froth.

FROTH

2Mine! I defy thee. Did not Master Marrall
3(He has marred all, I am sure) strictly command us,
4On pain of Sir Giles Overreach' displeasure,
5To turn the gentleman out of doors?

TAPWELL

To turn the gentleman out of doors? 'Tis true.
6But now he's his uncle's darling, and has got
7Master Justice Greedy (since he filled his belly)
8At his commandment to do anything.
9Woe, woe to us!

FROTH

Woe, woe to us! He may prove merciful.

TAPWELL

10Troth, we do not deserve it at his hands.
11Though he knew all the passages of our house,
12As the receiving of stol'n goods, and bawdry,
13When he was rogue Wellborn no man would believe him,
14And then his information could not hurt us;
15But, now he is "Right Worshipful" again,
16Who dares but doubt his testimony? Methinks
17I see thee, Froth, already in a cart,
18For a close bawd, thine eyes e'en pelted out
19With dirt and rotten eggs, and my hand hissing
20(If I scape the halter) with the letter R
21Printed upon it.

FROTH

Printed upon it. Would that were the worst!
22That were but nine days' wonder, as for credit,
23We have none to lose; but we shall lose the money
24He owes us, and his custom—there's the hell on't.

TAPWELL

25He has summoned all his creditors by the drum,
26And they swarm about him like so many soldiers
27On the payday, and has found out such a new way
28To pay his old debts as 'tis very likely
29He shall be chronicled for it.

FROTH

He shall be chronicled for it. He deserves it,
30More than ten pageants. But are you sure His Worship
31Comes this way to my lady's?

A cry within: "Brave Master Wellborn!"

TAPWELL

Comes this way to my lady's? Yes, I hear him.

FROTH

32Be ready with your petition and present it
33To His good Grace.

Enter Wellborn in a rich habit, [Marall,] Greedy, Order, Furnace, [and] three Creditors. Tapwell, kneeling, delivers his bill of debt.

WELLBORN

To His good Grace. How's this! Petitioned too?
34But note what miracles the payment of
35A little trash and a rich suit of clothes
36Can work upon these rascals! I shall be,
37I think, Prince Wellborn.

MARALL

I think, Prince Wellborn. When Your Worship's married,
38You may be—I know what I hope to see you.

WELLBORN

39Then look thou for advancement.

MARALL

Then look thou for advancement. To be known
40Your Worship's bailiff is the mark I shoot at.

WELLBORN

41And thou shalt hit it.

MARALL

And thou shalt hit it. Pray you, sir, dispatch
42These needy followers, and, for my admittance,
43Provided you'll defend me from Sir Giles,
44Whose service I am weary of, I'll say something
45You shall give thanks for.

WELLBORN

You shall give thanks for. Fear me not Sir Giles.

This interim, Tapwell and Froth flattering and bribing Justice Greedy.

GREEDY

46Who? Tapwell? I remember thy wife brought me,
47Last New Year's tide, a couple of fat turkeys.

TAPWELL

48And shall do every Christmas, let Your Worship
49But stand my friend now.

GREEDY

But stand my friend now. How? With Master Wellborn?
50I can do anything with him, on such terms.—
51[To Wellborn] See you this honest couple? They are good souls
52As ever drew out faucet. Have they not
53A pair of honest faces?

WELLBORN

A pair of honest faces? I o'erheard you,
54And the bribe he promised. You are cozened in 'em,
55For of all the scum that grew rich by my riots,
56This for a most unthankful knave and this
57For a base bawd and whore have worst deserved me,
58And therefore speak not for 'em. By your place
59You are rather to do me justice. Lend me your ear.
60Forget his turkeys, and call in his license,aparte
61And at the next fair I'll give you a yoke of oxenaparte
62Worth all his poultry.aparte

GREEDY

Worth all his poultry. I am changed on the sudden
63In my opinion. [To Tapwell] Come near. Nearer, rascal!
64[To Wellborn] And, now I view him better, did you e'er see
65One look so like an arch-knave? His very countenance,
66Should an understanding judge but look upon him,
67Would hang him, though he were innocent.

TAPWELL  [and] FROTH

Would hang him, though he were innocent. Worshipful sir!

GREEDY

68No, though the great Turk came, instead of turkeys,
69To beg any favor, I am inexorable.
70Thou hast an ill name. Besides thy musty ale,
71That hath destroyed many of the King's liege people,
72Thou never hadst in thy house, to stay mens' stomachs,
73A piece of Suffolk cheese, or gammon of bacon,
74Or any esculent, as the learnèd call it,
75For their emolument, but sheer drink only.
76For which gross fault, I here do damn thy license,
77Forbidding thee ever to tap or draw;
78For instantly I will, in mine own person,
79Command the constable to pull down thy sign,
80And do it before I eat.

FROTH

And do it before I eat. No mercy?

GREEDY

And do it before I eat. No mercy? Vanish!
81If I show any, may my promised oxen gore me!

TAPWELL

82Unthankful knaves are ever so rewarded.

Exeunt Greedy, Tapwell, [and] Froth.

WELLBORN

83[to the First Creditor] Speak; what are you?

FIRST CREDITOR

[to the First Creditor] Speak; what are you? A decayed vintner, sir,
84That might have thrived, but that Your Worship broke me
85With trusting you with muscadine and eggs,
86And five-pound suppers, with your after-drinkings,
87When you lodged upon the Bankside.

WELLBORN

When you lodged upon the Bankside. I remember.

FIRST CREDITOR

88I have not been hasty, nor e'er laid to arrest you;
89And therefore, sir—

WELLBORN

And therefore, sir— Thou art an honest fellow.
90I'll set thee up again. [To Order] See his bill paid.—
91What are you?

SECOND CREDITOR

What are you? A tailor once, but now mere botcher.
92I gave you credit for a suit of clothes,
93Which was all my stock, but, you failing in payment,
94I was removed from the shop board, and confined
95Under a stall.

WELLBORN

Under a stall. [to Order] See him paid.—And botch no more.

SECOND CREDITOR

96I ask no interest, sir.

WELLBORN

I ask no interest, sir. Such tailors need not;
97If their bills are paid in one-and-twenty year,
98They are seldom losers. [To Third Creditor] Oh, I know thy face;
99Thou wert my surgeon. You must tell no tales;
100Those days are done. I will pay you in private.

ORDER

101[to Furnace] A royal gentleman!

FURNACE

[to Furnace] A royal gentleman! [to Order] Royal as an emperor!
102He'll prove a brave master. My good lady knew
103To choose a man.

WELLBORN

To choose a man. [to Order] See all men else discharged;
104And since old debts are cleared by a new way,
105A little bounty will not misbecome me.

[Giving money to Furnace]

106There's something, honest cook, for thy good breakfasts,

[Giving money to Order]

107And this for your respect. Take't; 'tis good gold,
108And I able to spare it.

ORDER

And I able to spare it. You are too munificent.

FURNACE

109[to the others] He was ever so.

WELLBORN

[to the others] He was ever so. Pray you, on before.

THIRD CREDITOR

[to the others] He was ever so. Pray you, on before. Heaven bless you!

MARALL

110At four o'clock the rest know where to meet me.

Exeunt Order, Furnace, [and] Creditors.

WELLBORN

111Now, Master Marrall, what's the weighty secret
112You promised to impart?

MARALL

You promised to impart? Sir, time nor place
113Allow me to relate each circumstance.
114This only in a word: I know Sir Giles
115Will come upon you for security
116For his thousand pounds, which you must not consent to.
117As he grows in heat—as I am sure he will—
118Be you but rough and say he's in your debt
119Ten times the sum, upon sale of your land.
120I had a hand in't (I speak it to my shame)
121When you were defeated of it.

WELLBORN

When you were defeated of it. That's forgiven.

MARALL

122I shall deserve 't, then. Urge him to produce
123The deed in which you passed it over to him—
124Which I know he'll have about him—to deliver
125To the Lord Lovell, with many other writings
126And present moneys. I'll instruct you further,
127As I wait on Your Worship. If I play not my prize
128To your full content and your uncle's much vexation,
129Hang up Jack Marrall.

WELLBORN

Hang up Jack Marrall. I rely upon thee.

Exeunt.

4.3

[Enter] Allworth [and] Margaret.

ALLWORTH

1Whether to yield the first praise to my lord's
2Unequalled temperance or your constant sweetness,
3That I yet live, my weak hands fastened on
4Hope's anchor, spite of all storms of despair,
5I yet rest doubtful.

MARGARET

I yet rest doubtful. Give it to Lord Lovell,
6For what in him was bounty in me's duty.
7I make but payment of a debt to which
8My vows, in that high office registered,
9Are faithful witnesses.

ALLWORTH

Are faithful witnesses. 'Tis true, my dearest.
10Yet, when I call to mind how many fair ones
11Make willful shipwreck of their faiths and oaths
12To God and man to fill the arms of greatness,
13And you rise up no less than a glorious star,
14To the amazement of the world, that hold out
15Against the stern authority of a father
16And spurn at honor when it comes to court you,
17I am so tender of your good that, faintly,
18With your wrong I can wish myself that right
19You yet are pleased to do me.

MARGARET

You yet are pleased to do me. Yet, and ever.
20To me what's title, when content is wanting?
21Or wealth, raked up together with much care
22And to be kept with more, when the heart pines
23In being dispossessed of what it longs for,
24Beyond the Indian mines? Or the smooth brow
25Of a pleased sire, that slaves me to his will,
26And—so his ravenous humor may be feasted
27By my obedience, and he see me great—
28Leaves to my soul nor faculties nor power
29To make her own election?

ALLWORTH

To make her own election? But the dangers
30That follow the repulse—

MARGARET

That follow the repulse— To me they are nothing.
31Let Allworth love, I cannot be unhappy.
32Suppose the worst, that in his rage he kill me;
33A tear or two by you dropped on my hearse
34In sorrow for my fate will call back life
35So far as but to say that I die yours,
36I then shall rest in peace. Or should he prove
37So cruel as one death would not suffice
38His thirst of vengeance, but with ling'ring torments
39In mind and body I must waste to air
40In poverty joined with banishment, so you share
41In my afflictions (which I dare not wish you),
42So high I prize you, I could undergo 'em
43With such a patience as should look down
44With scorn on his worst malice.

ALLWORTH

With scorn on his worst malice. Heaven avert
45Such trials of your true affection to me!
46Nor will it, unto you that are all mercy,
47Show so much rigor. But since we must run
48Such desperate hazards, let us do our best
49To steer between 'em.

MARGARET

To steer between 'em. Your lord's ours, and sure;
50And, though but a young actor, second me
51In doing to the life what he has plotted;
52The end may yet prove happy. Now, my Allworth—

Enter Overreach [behind].

ALLWORTH

53To your letter! And put on a seeming anger.aparte

MARGARET

54[aloud] I'll pay my lord all debts due to his title,
55And when, with terms not taking from his honor,
56He does solicit me, I shall gladly hear him.
57But in this peremptory, nay, commanding way,
58T'appoint a meeting, and, without my knowledge,
59A priest to tie the knot can ne'er be undone
60Till death unloose it, is a confidence
61In His Lordship will deceive him.

ALLWORTH

In His Lordship will deceive him. I hope better,
62Good lady.

MARGARET

Good lady. Hope, sir, what you please. For me,
63I must take a safe and secure course. I have
64A father, and, without his full consent,
65Though all lords of the land kneeled for my favor,
66I can grant nothing.

OVERREACH

I can grant nothing. [coming forward] I like this obedience.
67But whatsoever my lord writes must and shall be
68Accepted and embraced. Sweet Master Allworth,
69You show yourself a true and faithful servant
70To your good lord; he has a jewel of you.—
71How? Frowning, Meg? Are these looks to receive
72A messenger from my lord?
73[Seeing the letter] What's this? Give me it.

MARGARET

74A piece of arrogant paper, like th'inscriptions.

Overreach [takes and] read[s] the letter.

OVERREACH

75"Fair mistress, from your servant learn all joys
76That we can hope for, if deferred, prove toys;
77Therefore this instant, and in private, meet
78A husband that will gladly at your feet
79Lay down his honors, tend'ring them to you
80With all content, the church being paid her due."
81Is this the arrogant piece of paper? Fool,
82Will you still be one? In the name of madness, what
83Could His good Honor write more to content you?
84Is there aught else to be wished after these two
85That are already offered: marriage first,
86And lawful pleasure after? What would you more?

MARGARET

87Why, sir, I would be married like your daughter,
88Not hurried away I'th'night I know not whither,
89Without all ceremony, no friends invited
90To honor the solemnity.

ALLWORTH

To honor the solemnity. An't please Your Honor—
91For so before tomorrow I must style you—aparte
92My lord desires this privacy in respect
93His honorable kinsmen are far off,
94And his desires to have it done brook not
95So long delay as to expect their coming;
96And yet he stands resolved, with all due pomp—
97As running at the ring, plays, masques, and tilting—
98To have his marriage at court celebrated
99When he has brought Your Honor up to London.

OVERREACH

100[to Margaret] He tells you true; 'tis the fashion, on my knowledge.
101Yet the good lord, to please your peevishness,
102Must put it off, forsooth, and lose a night
103In which perhaps he might get two boys on thee.
104Tempt me no farther. If you do, this goad

[Pointing to his sword]

105Shall prick you to him.

MARGARET

Shall prick you to him. I could be contented,
106Were you but by to do a father's part
107And give me in the church.

OVERREACH

And give me in the church. So my lord have you,
108What do I care who gives you? Since my lord
109Does purpose to be private, I'll not cross him.—
110I know not, Master Allworth, how my lord
111May be provided, and therefore
112[giving money] there's a purse
113Of gold 'twill serve this night's expense; tomorrow
114I'll furnish him with any sums. In the meantime,
115Use my ring to my chaplain; he is beneficed
116At my manor of Gotham, and called Parson Willdo.
117'Tis no matter for a license; I'll bear him out in't.

MARGARET

118With your favor, sir, what warrant is your ring?
119He may suppose I got that twenty ways,
120Without your knowledge; and then to be refused
121Were such a stain upon me! If you pleased, sir,
122Your presence would do better.

OVERREACH

Your presence would do better. Still perverse?
123I say again I will not cross my lord;
124Yet I'll prevent you too.
125[Calling] Paper and ink there!

ALLWORTH

126I can furnish you.

OVERREACH

I can furnish you. I thank you; I can write then.

Writes on his book.

ALLWORTH

127You man, if you please, put out the name of my lord,
128In respect he comes disguised, and only write:
129"Marry her to this gentleman."

OVERREACH

"Marry her to this gentleman." Well advised.

[He writes, and gives the note to Allworth.]

130'Tis done. Away! (Margaret kneels.) My blessing, girl? Thou hast it.
131Nay, no reply; begone. [She rises.] Good Master Allworth,
132This shall be the best night's work you ever made.

ALLWORTH

133I hope so, sir.

Exeunt Allworth and Margaret.

OVERREACH

I hope so, sir. Farewell!—Now all's cocksure.
134Methinks I hear already knights and ladies
135Say, "Sir Giles Overreach, how is it with
136Your honorable daughter? Has Her Honor
137Slept well tonight?" Or, "Will Her Honor please
138To accept this monkey? Dog? Or parakeet?"
139(This is state in ladies), or "My eldest son
140To be her page, and wait upon her trencher?"
141My ends, my ends are compassed! Then for Wellborn
142And the lands. Were he once married to the widow,
143I have him here. I can scarce contain myself,
144I am so full of joy, nay, joy all over!

Exit.
