Philip Massinger

A New Way To Pay Old Debts

1.1

[Enter] Wellborn [in ragged clothes], Tapwell, [and] Froth.

WELLBORN

1No booze? Nor no tobacco?

TAPWELL

No booze? Nor no tobacco? Not a suck, sir,
2Nor the remainder of a single can
3Left by a drunken porter, all night palled too.

FROTH

4Not the dropping of the tap for your morning's draft, sir.
5'Tis verity, I assure you.

WELLBORN

'Tis verity, I assure you. Verity, you brach!
6The devil turned precisian? Rogue, what am I?

TAPWELL

7Troth, durst I trust you with a looking glass
8To let you see your trim shape, you would quit me
9And take the name yourself.

WELLBORN

And take the name yourself. How, dog?

TAPWELL

And take the name yourself. How, dog? Even so, sir.
10And I must tell you, if you but advance
11Your Plymouth cloak, you shall be soon instructed
12There dwells—and within call, if it please Your Worship—
13A potent monarch called the constable,
14That does command a citadel called the stocks,
15Whose guards are certain files of rusty billmen,
16Such as with great dexterity will hale
17Your tattered, lousy—

WELLBORN

Your tattered, lousy— Rascal! Slave!

FROTH

Your tattered, lousy— Rascal! Slave! No rage, sir.

TAPWELL

18[to Froth] At his own peril. [To Wellborn] Do not put yourself
19In too much heat, there being no water near
20To quench your thirst; and sure, for other liquor,
21As mighty ale or beer, they are things, I take it,
22You must no more remember—not in a dream, sir.

WELLBORN

23Why, thou unthankful villain, dar'st thou talk thus?
24Is not thy house, and all thou hast, my gift?

TAPWELL

25I find it not in chalk, and Timothy Tapwell
26Does keep no other register.

WELLBORN

Does keep no other register. Am not I he
27Whose riots fed and clothed thee? Wert thou not
28Born on my father's land, and proud to be
29A drudge in his house?

TAPWELL

A drudge in his house? What I was, sir, it skills not.
30What you are is apparent. Now for a farewell:
31Since you talk of father, in my hope it will torment you,
32I'll briefly tell your story. Your dead father,
33My quondam master, was a man of worship,
34Old Sir John Wellborn, Justice of Peace and Quorum,
35And stood fair to be Custos Rotulorum ,
36Bare the whole sway of the shire, kept a great house,
37Relieved the poor, and so forth; but he dying,
38And the twelve hundred a year coming to you,
39Late Master Francis, but now forlorn Wellborn—

WELLBORN

40Slave, stop, or I shall lose myself.

FROTH

Slave, stop, or I shall lose myself. Very hardly;
41You cannot out of your way.

TAPWELL

You cannot out of your way. But to my story.
42You were then a lord of acres, the prime gallant,
43And I your under-butler. Note the change now.
44You had a merry time of't—hawks and hounds,
45With choice of running horses, mistresses
46Of all sort and all sizes—yet so hot
47As their embraces made your lordship melt.
48Which your uncle, Sir Giles Overreach, observing,
49Resolving not to lose a drop of 'em
50On foolish mortgages, statutes, and bonds,
51For a while supplied your looseness, and then left you.

WELLBORN

52Some curate hath penned this invective, mongrel,
53And you have studied it.

TAPWELL

And you have studied it. I have not done yet.
54Your land gone, and your credit not worth a token,
55You grew the common borrower; no man scaped
56Your paper pellets, from the gentleman
57To the beggars on highways, that sold you switches
58In your gallantry.

WELLBORN

In your gallantry. I shall switch your brains out.

TAPWELL

59Where poor Tim Tapwell, with a little stock,
60Some forty pounds or so, bought a small cottage,
61Humbled myself to marriage with my Froth here,
62Gave entertainment—

WELLBORN

Gave entertainment— Yes, to whores and canters,
63Clubbers by night.

TAPWELL

Clubbers by night. True, but they brought in profit,
64And had a gift to pay for what they called for,
65And stuck not like Your Mastership. The poor income
66I gleaned from them hath made me in my parish
67Thought worthy to be scavenger, and in time
68May rise to be overseer of the poor—
69Which if I do, on your petition, Wellborn,
70I may allow you thirteenpence a quarter,
71And you shall thank My Worship.

WELLBORN

And you shall thank My Worship. Thus, you dogbolt!
72And thus!

Beats and kicks him.

TAPWELL

And thus! [to Froth] Cry out for help!

WELLBORN

And thus! [to Froth] Cry out for help! Stir, and thou diest.
73Your potent prince, the constable, shall not save you.
74Hear me, ungrateful hellhound. Did not I
75Make purses for you? Then you licked my boots,
76And thought your holiday cloak too coarse to clean 'em.
77'Twas I that, when I heard thee swear if ever
78Thou couldst arrive at forty pounds thou wouldst
79Live like an emperor, 'twas I that gave it
80In ready gold. Deny this, wretch!

TAPWELL

In ready gold. Deny this, wretch! I must sir,
81For, from the tavern to the taphouse, all,
82On forfeiture of their licenses, stand bound
83Never to remember who their best guests were,
84If they grew poor like you.

WELLBORN

If they grew poor like you. They are well rewarded
85That beggar themselves to make such cuckolds rich.
86Thou viper, thankless viper! Impudent bawd!
87But since you are grown forgetful, I will help
88Your memory, and tread thee into mortar,
89Not leave one bone unbroken.

[He beats Tapwell again.]

TAPWELL

Not leave one bone unbroken. Oh!

FROTH

Not leave one bone unbroken. Oh! Ask mercy!

Enter Allworth.

WELLBORN

90'Twill not be granted.

ALLWORTH

'Twill not be granted. Hold, for my sake, hold!
91Deny me, Frank? They are not worth your anger.

WELLBORN

92For once thou hast redeemed them from this scepter.

[Wellborn puts up] his cudgel.

93But let 'em vanish, creeping on their knees,
94And, if they grumble, I revoke my pardon.

FROTH

95This comes of your prating, husband. You presumed
96On your ambling wit, and must use your glib tongue
97Though you are beaten lame for't.

TAPWELL

Though you are beaten lame for't. Patience, Froth.
98There's law to cure our bruises.

They go off on their hands and knees.

WELLBORN

There's law to cure our bruises. Sent to your mother?

ALLWORTH

99My lady, Frank, my patroness! My all!
100She's such a mourner for my father's death,
101And, in her love to him, so favors me
102That I cannot pay too much observance to her.
103There are few such stepdames.

WELLBORN

There are few such stepdames. 'Tis a noble widow,
104And keeps her reputation pure and clear
105From the least taint of infamy; her life,
106With the splendor of her actions, leaves no tongue
107To envy or detraction. Prithee tell me:
108Has she no suitors?

ALLWORTH

Has she no suitors? Even the best of the shire, Frank,
109My lord excepted—such as sue and send,
110And send and sue again, but to no purpose.
111Their frequent visits have not gained her presence.
112Yet she's so far from sullenness and pride
113That I dare undertake you shall meet from her
114A liberal entertainment. I can give you
115A catalog of her suitors' names.

WELLBORN

A catalog of her suitors' names. Forbear it,
116While I give you good counsel. I am bound to it;
117Thy father was my friend, and that affection
118I bore to him, in right descends to thee.
119Thou art a handsome and a hopeful youth,
120Nor will I have the least affront stick on thee,
121If I with any danger can prevent it.

ALLWORTH

122I thank your noble care. But pray you, in what
123Do I run the hazard?

WELLBORN

Do I run the hazard? Art thou not in love?
124Put it not off with wonder.

ALLWORTH

Put it not off with wonder. In love, at my years?

WELLBORN

125You think you walk in clouds, but are transparent.
126I have heard all, and the choice that you have made,
127And with my finger can point out the north star
128By which the lodestone of your folly's guided.
129And to confirm this true, what think you of
130Fair Margaret, the only child and heir
131Of cormorant Overreach? Does it blush and start
132To hear her only named? Blush at your want
133Of wit and reason.

ALLWORTH

Of wit and reason. You are too bitter, sir.

WELLBORN

134Wounds of this nature are not to be cured
135With balms, but corrosives. I must be plain:
136Art thou scarce manumized from the porter's lodge,
137And yet sworn servant to the pantofle,
138And dar'st thou dream of marriage? I fear
139'Twill be concluded for impossible
140That there is now, nor e'er shall be hereafter,
141A handsome page or players' boy of fourteen
142But either loves a wench, or drabs love him—
143Court-waiters not exempted.

ALLWORTH

Court-waiters not exempted. This is madness.
144Howe'er you have discovered my intents,
145You know my aims are lawful; and if ever
146The queen of flowers, the glory of the spring,
147The sweetest comfort to our smell, the rose,
148Sprang from an envious brier, I may infer
149There's such disparity in their conditions
150Between the goddess of my soul, the daughter,
151And the base churl, her father.

WELLBORN

And the base churl, her father. Grant this true,
152As I believe it, canst thou ever hope
153To enjoy a quiet bed with her whose father
154Ruined thy state?

ALLWORTH

Ruined thy state? And yours too.

WELLBORN

Ruined thy state? And yours too. I confess it.
155True, I must tell you as a friend, and freely,
156That, where impossibilities are apparent,
157'Tis indiscretion to nourish hopes.
158Canst thou imagine—let not self-love blind thee—
159That Sir Giles Overreach, that, to make her great
160In swelling titles, without touch of conscience
161Will cut his neighbor's throat, and I hope his own too,
162Will e'er consent to make her thine? Give o'er,
163And think of some course suitable to thy rank,
164And prosper in it.

ALLWORTH

And prosper in it. You have well advised me.
165But in the meantime, you that are so studious
166Of my affairs wholly neglect your own.
167Remember yourself, and in what plight you are.

WELLBORN

168No matter, no matter.

ALLWORTH

No matter, no matter. Yes, 'tis much material.
169You know my fortune and my means; yet something
170I can spare from myself to help your wants.

[He offers money.]

WELLBORN

171How's this?

ALLWORTH

How's this? Nay, be not angry. There's eight pieces
172To put you in better fashion.

WELLBORN

To put you in better fashion. Money from thee?
173From a boy? A stipendiary? One that lives
174At the devotion of a stepmother
175And the uncertain favor of a lord?
176I'll eat my arms first. Howsoe'er blind Fortune
177Hath spent the utmost of her malice on me—
178Though I am vomited out of an alehouse,
179And, thus accoutred, know not where to eat,
180Or drink, or sleep, but underneath this canopy—
181Although I thank thee, I despise thy offer.
182And, as I in my madness broke my state
183Without th'assistance of another's brain,
184In my right wits I'll piece it; at the worst
185Die thus, and be forgotten.

ALLWORTH

Die thus, and be forgotten. A strange humor!

Exeunt.

1.2

[Enter] Order, Amble, Furnace, [and] Watchall.

ORDER

1Set all things right, or—as my name is Order,
2And by this staff of office that commands you,
3This chain and double ruff, symbols of power—
4Whoever misses in his function,
5For one whole week makes forfeiture of his breakfast
6And privilege in the wine cellar.

AMBLE

And privilege in the wine cellar. You are merry,
7Good Master Steward.

FURNACE

Good Master Steward. Let him; I'll be angry.

AMBLE

8Why, fellow Furnace, 'tis not twelve o'clock yet,
9Nor dinner taking up; then 'tis allowed
10Cooks by their places may be choleric.

FURNACE

11You think you have spoke wisely, goodman Amble,
12My lady's go-before!

ORDER

My lady's go-before! Nay, nay, no wrangling.

FURNACE

13Twit me with the authority of the kitchen?
14At all hours and all places I'll be angry;
15And, thus provoked, when I am at my prayers,
16I will be angry.

AMBLE

I will be angry. There was no hurt meant.

FURNACE

17I am friends with thee, and yet I will be angry.

ORDER

18With whom?

FURNACE

With whom? No matter whom. Yet, now I think on't,
19I am angry with my lady.

WATCHALL

I am angry with my lady. Heaven forbid, man!

ORDER

20What cause has she given thee?

FURNACE

What cause has she given thee? Cause enough, Master Steward.
21I was entertained by her to please her palate,
22And till she forswore eating I performed it.
23Now since our master, noble Allworth, died,
24Though I crack my brains to find out tempting sauces,
25And raise fortifications in the pastry
26Such as might serve for models in the Low Countries,
27Which if they had been practiced at Breda,
28Spinola might have thrown his cap at it and ne'er took it—

AMBLE

29But you had wanted matter there to work on.

FURNACE

30Matter? With six eggs and a strike of rye meal
31I had kept the town till doomsday, perhaps longer.

ORDER

32But, what's this to your pet against my lady?

FURNACE

33What's this? Marry, this: when I am three parts roasted
34And the fourth part parboiled to prepare her viands,
35She keeps her chamber, dines with a panada
36Or water-gruel, my sweat never thought on.

ORDER

37But your art is seen in the dining room.

FURNACE

But your art is seen in the dining room. By whom?
38By such as pretend love to her, but come
39To feed upon her. Yet of all the Harpies
40That do devour her, I am out of charity
41With none so much as the thin-gutted squire
42That's stol'n into commission.

ORDER

That's stol'n into commission. Justice Greedy?

FURNACE

43The same, the same. Meat's cast away upon him;
44It never thrives. He holds this paradox:
45Who eats not well can ne'er do justice well.
46His stomach's as insatiate as the grave,
47Or strumpet's ravenous appetites.

WATCHALL

Or strumpet's ravenous appetites. One knocks.

Allworth knocks, and enters.

ORDER

48Our late young master!

AMBLE

Our late young master! Welcome, sir.

FURNACE

Our late young master! Welcome, sir. Your hand.
49If you have a stomach, a cold bake-meat's ready.

ORDER

50[to the others] His father's picture in little.

FURNACE

[to the others] His father's picture in little. We are all your servants.

AMBLE

51In you he lives.

ALLWORTH

In you he lives. At once, my thanks to all.
52This is yet some comfort. Is my lady stirring?

ORDER

53Her presence answer for us.

Enter the Lady Allworth, Waiting-woman, [and] Chambermaid.

LADY ALLWORTH

Her presence answer for us. [to her women] Sort those silks well.
54I'll take the air alone.

Exeunt Waiting-woman and Chambermaid.

FURNACE

I'll take the air alone. You air and air,
55But will you never taste but spoon-meat more?
56To what use serve I?

LADY ALLWORTH

To what use serve I? Prithee, be not angry;
57I shall, ere long. I'the meantime, there is gold
58To buy thee aprons and a summer suit.

[She gives money.]

FURNACE

59I am appeased, and Furnace now grows cool.

LADY ALLWORTH

60And, as I gave directions, if this morning
61I am visited by any, entertain 'em
62As heretofore, but say, in my excuse,
63I am indisposed.

ORDER

I am indisposed. I shall, madam.

LADY ALLWORTH

I am indisposed. I shall, madam. Do, and leave me.—
64Nay, stay you, Allworth.

ALLWORTH

Nay, stay you, Allworth. I shall gladly grow here
65To wait on your commands.

Exeunt Order, Amble, Furnace, [and] Watchall.

LADY ALLWORTH

To wait on your commands. So soon turned courtier!

ALLWORTH

66Style not that "courtship", madam, which is duty
67Purchased on your part.

LADY ALLWORTH

Purchased on your part. Well, you shall o'ercome;
68I'll not contend in words. How is it with
69Your noble master?

ALLWORTH

Your noble master? Ever like himself,
70No scruple lessened in the full weight of honor.
71He did command me (pardon my presumption)
72As his unworthy deputy to kiss
73Your Ladyship's fair hands.

[He kisses her hands.]

LADY ALLWORTH

Your Ladyship's fair hands. I am honored in
74His favor to me. Does he hold his purpose
75For the Low Countries?

ALLWORTH

For the Low Countries? Constantly, good madam,
76But he will in person first present his service.

LADY ALLWORTH

77And how approve you of his course? You are yet,
78Like virgin parchment, capable of any
79Inscription, vicious or honorable.
80I will not force your will, but leave you free
81To your own election.

ALLWORTH

To your own election. Any form you please
82I will put on; but, might I make my choice,
83With humble emulation I would follow
84The path my lord marks to me.

LADY ALLWORTH

The path my lord marks to me. 'Tis well answered,
85And I commend your spirit. You had a father—
86Blessed be his memory!—that some few hours
87Before the will of heaven took him from me,
88Who did commend you, by the dearest ties
89Of perfect love between us, to my charge;
90And therefore what I speak you are bound to hear
91With such respect as if he lived in me.
92He was my husband, and howe'er you are not
93Son of my womb, you may be of my love,
94Provided you deserve it.

ALLWORTH

Provided you deserve it. I have found you,
95Most honored madam, the best mother to me,
96And, with my utmost strengths of care and service,
97Will labor that you never may repent
98Your bounties showered upon me.

LADY ALLWORTH

Your bounties showered upon me. I much hope it.
99These were your father's words: "If e'er my son
100Follow the war, tell him it is a school
101Where all the principles tending to honor
102Are taught, if truly followed; but for such
103As repair thither as a place in which
104They do presume they may with license practice
105Their lusts and riots, they shall never merit
106The noble name of soldiers. To dare boldly
107In a fair cause, and for the country's safety
108To run upon the cannon's mouth undaunted;
109To obey their leaders, and shun mutinies;
110To bear with patience the winter's cold
111And summer's scorching heat, and not to faint,
112When plenty of provision fails, with hunger,
113Are the essential parts make up a soldier—
114Not swearing, dice, or drinking."

ALLWORTH

Not swearing, dice, or drinking." There's no syllable
115You speak but is to me an oracle,
116Which but to doubt were impious.

LADY ALLWORTH

Which but to doubt were impious. To conclude:
117Beware ill company, for often men
118Are like to those with whom they do conserve.
119And from one man I warned you, and that's Wellborn;
120Not 'cause he's poor—that rather claims your pity—
121But that he's in his manners so debauched,
122And hath to vicious courses sold himself.
123'Tis true your father loved him while he was
124Worthy the loving, but, if he had lived
125To have seen him as he is, he had cast him off
126As you must do.

ALLWORTH

As you must do. I shall obey in all things.

LADY ALLWORTH

127Follow me to my chamber; you shall have gold
128To furnish you like my son, and still supplied
129As I hear from you.

ALLWORTH

As I hear from you. I am still your creature.

Exeunt.

[Enter] Overreach, Greedy, Order, Amble, Furnace, Watchall, [and] Marall.

GREEDY

1Not to be seen?

OVERREACH

Not to be seen? Still cloistered up? Her reason,
2I hope, assures her, though she make herself
3Close prisoner ever for her husband's loss,
4'Twill not recover him.

ORDER

'Twill not recover him. Sir, it is her will,
5Which we that are her servants ought to serve it
6And not dispute. Howe'er, you are nobly welcome,
7And, if you please to stay, that you may think so,
8There came not six days since from Hull a pipe
9Of rich canary, which shall spend itself
10For my lady's honor.

GREEDY

For my lady's honor. Is it of the right race?

ORDER

11Yes, Master Greedy.

AMBLE

Yes, Master Greedy. How his mouth runs o'er!aparte

FURNACE

12I'll make it run, and run.—Save Your good Worship!aparte

GREEDY

13Honest Master Cook, thy hand again. How I love thee!
14Are the good dishes still in being? Speak, boy.

FURNACE

15If you have a mind to feed, there is a chine
16Of beef, well seasoned.

GREEDY

Of beef, well seasoned. Good!

FURNACE

Of beef, well seasoned. Good! A pheasant, larded.

GREEDY

17That I might now give thanks for't?

FURNACE

That I might now give thanks for't? Other kickshaws.
18Besides, there came last night from the Forest of Sherwood
19The fattest stag I ever cooked.

GREEDY

The fattest stag I ever cooked. A stag, man?

FURNACE

20A stag, sir—part of it prepared for dinner,
21And baked in puff paste.

GREEDY

And baked in puff paste. Puff paste too, Sir Giles!
22A ponderous chine of beef! A pheasant larded!
23And red deer too, Sir Giles, and baked in puff paste!
24All business set aside; let us give thanks here.

FURNACE

25How the lean skeleton's rapt!aparte

OVERREACH

How the lean skeleton's rapt! You know we cannot.

MARALL

26Your Worships are to sit on a commission,
27And, if you fail to come, you lose the cause.

GREEDY

28Cause me no causes. I'll prove't, for such a dinner
29We may put off a commission: you shall find it
30Henrici decimo quarto .

OVERREACH

Henrici decimo quarto . Fie, Master Greedy,
31Will you lose me a thousand pounds for a dinner?
32No more, for shame! We must forget the belly
33When we think of profit.

GREEDY

When we think of profit. Well, you shall o'errule me.
34I could ev'n cry now.—Do you hear, Master Cook?
35Send but a corner of that immortal pasty,
36And I, in thankfulness, will by your boy
37Send you a brace of threepences.

FURNACE

38Will you be so prodigal?aparte

Enter Wellborn.

OVERREACH

39Remember me to your lady.—Who have we here?

WELLBORN

40You know me.

OVERREACH

You know me. I did once, but now I will not;
41Thou art no blood of mine. Avaunt, thou beggar!
42If ever thou presume to own me more,
43I'll have thee caged and whipped.

GREEDY

I'll have thee caged and whipped. I'll grant the warrant.—
44Think of Pie Corner, Furnace!

Exeunt Overreach, Greedy, [and] Marall.

WATCHALL

Think of Pie Corner, Furnace! [to Wellborn] Will you out, sir?
45I wonder how you durst creep in.

ORDER

I wonder how you durst creep in. This is rudeness
46And saucy impudence.

AMBLE

And saucy impudence. Cannot you stay
47To be served among your fellows from the basket,
48But you must press into the hall?

FURNACE

But you must press into the hall? Prithee, vanish
49Into some outhouse, though it be the pigsty;
50My scullion shall come to thee.

Enter Allworth.

WELLBORN

My scullion shall come to thee. This is rare.
51Oh, here's Tom Allworth.—Tom!

ALLWORTH

Oh, here's Tom Allworth.—Tom! We must be strangers,
52Nor would I have you seen here for a million.

Exit Allworth.

WELLBORN

53Better and better. He contemns me too?

Enter [Waiting-] woman and Chambermaid.

WAITING-WOMAN

54Foh, what a smell's here! What thing's this?

CHAMBERMAID

Foh, what a smell's here! What thing's this? A creature
55Made out of the privy. Let us hence, for love's sake,
56Or I shall swoon.

WAITING-WOMAN

Or I shall swoon. I begin to faint already.

Exeunt [Waiting-] woman and Chambermaid.

WATCHALL

57[to Wellborn] Will you know your way?

AMBLE

[to Wellborn] Will you know your way? Or shall we teach it you
58By the head and shoulders?

WELLBORN

By the head and shoulders? No. I will not stir.
59Do you mark, I will not. Let me see the wretch
60That dares attempt to force me. Why, you slaves,
61Created only to make legs and cringe,
62To carry in a dish and shift a trencher,
63That have not souls only to hope a blessing
64Beyond blackjacks or flagons—you that were born
65Only to consume meat and drink, and batten
66Upon reversions! Who advances? Who
67Shows me the way?

ORDER

Shows me the way? My lady!

Enter Lady [Allworth, Waiting-] woman, [and] Chambermaid.

CHAMBERMAID

Shows me the way? My lady! Here's the monster.

WAITING-WOMAN

68Sweet madam, keep your glove to your nose.

CHAMBERMAID

Sweet madam, keep your glove to your nose. Or let me
69Fetch some perfumes may be predominant;
70You wrong yourself else.

WELLBORN

You wrong yourself else. Madam, my designs
71Bear me to you.

LADY ALLWORTH

Bear me to you. To me?

WELLBORN

Bear me to you. To me? And though I have met with
72But ragged entertainment from your grooms here,
73I hope from you to receive that noble usage
74As may become the true friend of your husband,
75And then I shall forget these.

LADY ALLWORTH

And then I shall forget these. I am amazed
76To see and hear this rudeness. Dar'st thou think,
77Though sworn, that it can ever find belief
78That I, who to the best men of this country
79Denied my presence since my husband's death,
80Can fall so low as to change words with thee?
81Thou son of infamy, forbear my house,
82And know and keep the distance that's between us,
83Or, though it be against my gentler temper,
84I shall take order you no more shall be
85An eyesore to me.

WELLBORN

An eyesore to me. Scorn me not, good lady;

185Indented, I confess, and labels too,

OVERREACH

Your interest clear? I am o'erwhelmed with wonder!

191Hath razed out the inscription, the wax
192Turned into dust? The rest of my deeds whole
193As when they were delivered, and this only
194Made nothing! Do you deal with witches, rascal?

196Your neck in a hempen circle; yes, there is.
197And now 'tis better thought, for, cheater, know

WELLBORN

OVERREACH

I know no deeds. Wilt thou betray me?

To his no little torment. [as they restrain him] Mine own varlet
214Rebel against me?

MARALL

216The property fit only to be beaten

219Can now anatomize you, and lay open
220All your black plots, and level with the earth

LADY ALLWORTH

OVERREACH
