5.1

[Enter] Lovell, Lady [Allworth, and] Amble [who retires once he has ushered them in].

LADY ALLWORTH

1By this you know how strong the motives were
2That did, my lord, induce me to dispense
3A little with my gravity, to advance,
4In personating some few favors to him,
5The plots and projects of the downtrod Wellborn.
6Nor shall I e'er repent—although I suffer
7In some few men's opinions for't—the action.
8For he that ventured all for my dear husband
9Might justly claim an obligation from me
10To pay him such a courtesy, which, had I
11Coyly or overcuriously denied,
12It might have argued me of little love
13To the deceased.

LOVELL

To the deceased. What you intended, madam,
14For the poor gentleman hath found good success,
15For, as I understand, his debts are paid,
16And he once more furnished for fair employment.
17But all the arts that I have used to raise
18The fortunes of your joy and mine, young Allworth,
19Stand yet in supposition, though I hope well,
20For the young lovers are in wit more pregnant
21Than their years can promise; and for their desires,
22On my knowledge, they are equal.

LADY ALLWORTH

On my knowledge, they are equal. As my wishes
23Are with yours, my lord. Yet give me leave to fear
24The building, though well grounded. To deceive
25Sir Giles, that's both a lion and a fox
26In his proceedings, were a work beyond
27The strongest undertakers, not the trial
28Of two weak innocents.

LOVELL

Of two weak innocents. Despair not, madam.
29Hard things are compassed oft by easy means,
30And judgment, being a gift derived from heaven,
31Though sometimes lodged I'th'hearts of worldly men
32That ne'er consider from whom they receive it,
33Forsakes such as abuse the giver of it.
34Which is the reason that the politic
35And cunning statesman, that believes he fathoms
36The counsels of all kingdoms on the earth,
37Is by simplicity oft overreached.

LADY ALLWORTH

38May he be so! Yet in his name to express it
39Is a good omen.

LOVELL

Is a good omen. May it to myself
40Prove so, good lady, in my suit to you!
41What think you of the motion?

LADY ALLWORTH

What think you of the motion? Troth, my lord,
42My own unworthiness may answer for me;
43For had you, when that I was in my prime
44(My virgin flower uncropped), presented me
45With this great favor, looking on my lowness
46Not in a glass of self-love but of truth,
47I could not but have thought it as a blessing
48Far, far beyond my merit.

LOVELL

Far, far beyond my merit. You are too modest,
49And undervalue that which is above
50My title, or whatever I call mine.
51I grant, were I a Spaniard, to marry
52A widow might disparage me, but, being
53A trueborn Englishman, I cannot find
54How it can taint my honor. Nay, what's more,
55That which you think a blemish is to me
56The fairest lustre. You already, madam,
57Have given sure proofs how dearly you can cherish
58A husband that deserves you, which confirms me
59That, if I am not wanting in my care
60To do you service, you'll be still the same
61That you were to your Allworth. In a word,
62Our years, our states, our births are not unequal,
63You being descended nobly and allied so.
64If then you may be won to make me happy,
65But join your lips to mine, and that shall be
66A solemn contract.

LADY ALLWORTH

A solemn contract. I were blind to my own good,
67Should I refuse it.
68[They kiss.] Yet, my lord, receive me
69As such a one the study of whose whole life
70Shall know no other object but to please you.

LOVELL

71If I return not, with all tenderness,
72Equal respect to you, may I die wretched!

LADY ALLWORTH

73There needs no protestation, my lord.
74To her that cannot doubt—

Enter Wellborn [handsomely dressed].

To her that cannot doubt— You are welcome, sir.
75Now you look like yourself.

WELLBORN

Now you look like yourself. And will continue
76Such, in my free acknowledgment that I am
77Your creature, madam, and will never hold
78My life mine own when you please to command it.

LOVELL

79It is a thankfulness that well becomes you.
80You could not make choice of a better shape
81To dress your mind in.

LADY ALLWORTH

To dress your mind in. For me, I am happy
82That my endeavors prospered. Saw you of late
83Sir Giles, your uncle?

WELLBORN

Sir Giles, your uncle? I heard of him, madam,
84By his minister, Marrall. He's grown into strange passions
85About his daughter. This last night he looked for
86Your Lordship at his house, but, missing you,
87And she not yet appearing, His Wisehead
88Is much perplexed and troubled.

LOVELL

Is much perplexed and troubled. [to Lady Allworth] It may be,
89Sweetheart, my project took.

Enter Overreach [with a box], with distracted looks, driving in Marall before him. [They are unaware at first of the presence of the others already onstage.]

LADY ALLWORTH

Sweetheart, my project took. I strongly hope.

OVERREACH

90[to Marall] Ha! find her, booby, thou huge lump of nothing!
91I'll bore thine eyes out else.

WELLBORN

I'll bore thine eyes out else. May it please Your Lordship,aparte
92For some ends of mine own, but to withdrawaparte
93A little out of sight, though not of hearing,aparte
94You may perhaps have sport.aparte

LOVELL

You may perhaps have sport. You shall direct me.

Steps aside.

OVERREACH

95[to Marall] I shall sol-fa you, rogue.

MARALL

[to Marall] I shall sol-fa you, rogue. Sir, for what cause
96Do you use me thus?

OVERREACH

Do you use me thus? Cause, slave? Why, I am angry,
97And thou a subject only fit for beating,
98And so to cool my choler. Look to the writing;
99Let but the seal be broke upon the box
100That has slept in my cabinet these three years,
101I'll rack thy soul for't.

MARALL

I'll rack thy soul for't. I may yet cry quittance,aparte
102Though now I suffer and dare not resist.aparte

OVERREACH

Though now I suffer and dare not resist. [seeing Lady Allworth] Lady, by your leave, did you see my daughter, lady?
103And the lord her husband? Are they in your house?
104If they are, discover, that I may bid 'em joy;
105And, as an entrance to her place of honor,
106See Your Ladyship on her left hand, and make curtsies
107When she nods on you, which you must receive
108As a special favor.

LADY ALLWORTH

As a special favor. When I know, Sir Giles,
109Her state requires such ceremony, I shall pay it;
110But in the meantime, as I am myself,
111I give you to understand I neither know
112Nor care where Her Honor is.

OVERREACH

Nor care where Her Honor is. When you once see her
113Supported and led by the lord her husband,
114You'll be taught better.
You'll be taught better. [Seeing Wellborn] Nephew!

WELLBORN

You'll be taught better. [Seeing Wellborn] Nephew! Sir.

OVERREACH

You'll be taught better. [Seeing Wellborn] Nephew! Sir. No more?

WELLBORN

115'Tis all I owe you.

OVERREACH

'Tis all I owe you. Have you redeemed rags
116Made you thus insolent?

WELLBORN

Made you thus insolent? (in scorn) Insolent to you?
117Why, what are you, sir, unless in your years,
118At the best, more than myself?

OVERREACH

At the best, more than myself? His fortune swells him.aparte
119'Tis rank he's married.aparte

LADY ALLWORTH

'Tis rank he's married. This is excellent!

OVERREACH

120Sir, in calm language (though I seldom use it),
121I am familiar with the cause that makes you
122Bear up thus bravely. There's a certain buzz
123Of a stol'n marriage—do you hear?—of a stol'n marriage,
124In which, 'tis said, there's somebody hath been cozened.
125I name no parties.

WELLBORN

I name no parties. Well, sir, and what follows?

OVERREACH

126Marry, this, since you are peremptory: remember,
127Upon mere hope of your great match, I lent you
128A thousand pounds. Put me in good security,
129And suddenly, by mortgage or by statute
130Of some of your new possessions, or I'll have you
131Dragged in your lavender robes to the jail. You know me,
132And therefore do not trifle.

WELLBORN

And therefore do not trifle. Can you be
133So cruel to your nephew, now he's in
134The way to rise? Was this the courtesy
135You did me in pure love and no ends else?

OVERREACH

136End me no ends! Engage the whole estate,
137And force you spouse to sign it, you shall have
138Three or four thousand more, to roar and swagger
139And revel in bawdy taverns.

WELLBORN

And revel in bawdy taverns. And beg after;
140Mean you not so?

OVERREACH

Mean you not so? My thoughts are mine, and free.
141Shall I have security?

WELLBORN

Shall I have security? No, indeed you shall not,
142Nor bond, nor bill, nor bare acknowledgment.
143Your great looks fright not me.

OVERREACH

Your great looks fright not me. But my deeds shall.
144Outbraved?

They both draw.

The servants [including Order, Amble, and Furnace] enter.

LADY ALLWORTH

Outbraved? Help! Murder, murder!

WELLBORN

Outbraved? Help! Murder, murder! Let him come on,
145With all his wrongs and injuries about him,
146Armed with his cutthroat practices to guard him;
147The right that I bring with me will defend me,
148And punish his extortion.

OVERREACH

And punish his extortion. That I had thee
149But single in the field!

LADY ALLWORTH

But single in the field! You may; but make not
150My house your quarrelling scene.

OVERREACH

My house your quarrelling scene. Were't in a church,
151By heaven, and hell, I'll do't.

MARALL

By heaven, and hell, I'll do't. Now put him toaparte
152The showing of the deed.aparte

WELLBORN

The showing of the deed. This rage is vain, sir.
153For fighting, fear not; you shall have your hands full
154Upon the least incitement. And whereas
155You charge me with a debt of a thousand pounds,
156If there be law (howe'er you have no conscience),
157Either restore my land, or I'll recover
158A debt that's truly due to me from you
159In value ten times more than what you challenge.

OVERREACH

160I in thy debt? Oh, impudence! Did I not purchase
161The land left by thy father, that rich land,
162That had continued in Wellborn's name
163Twenty descents, which, like a riotous fool,
164Thou didst make sale of? Is not here enclosed
165The deed that does confirm it mine?

MARALL

The deed that does confirm it mine? Now, now!aparte

WELLBORN

166I do acknowledge none. I ne'er passed o'er
167Any such land. I grant for a year or two
168You had it in trust, which if you do discharge,
169Surrend'ring the possession, you shall ease
170Yourself and me of chargeable suits in law—
171Which if you prove not honest (as I doubt it)
172Must of necessity follow.

LADY ALLWORTH

Must of necessity follow. In my judgment
173He does advise you well.

OVERREACH

He does advise you well. Good! Good! Conspire
174With you new husband, lady; second him
175In his dishonest practices. But when
176This manor is extended to my use,
177You'll speak in an humbler key and sue for favor.

LADY ALLWORTH

178Never. Do not hope it.

WELLBORN

Never. Do not hope it. Let despair first seize me!

OVERREACH

179Yet to shut up thy mouth and make thee give
180Thyself the lie, the loud lie, I draw out
181The precious evidence; if thou canst forswear
182Thy hand and seal, and make a forfeit of
183Thy ears to the pillory, see, here's that will make
184My interest clear.
My interest clear. (Opens the box.) Ha!

LADY ALLWORTH

My interest clear. (Opens the box.) Ha! A fair skin of parchment.

WELLBORN

185Indented, I confess, and labels too,
186But neither wax nor words. How! Thunderstruck?
187Not a syllable to insult with? My wise uncle,
188Is this your precious evidence? Is this that makes
189Your interest clear?

OVERREACH

Your interest clear? I am o'erwhelmed with wonder!
190What prodigy is this? What subtle devil
191Hath razed out the inscription, the wax
192Turned into dust? The rest of my deeds whole
193As when they were delivered, and this only
194Made nothing! Do you deal with witches, rascal?
195There is a statute for you, which will bring
196Your neck in a hempen circle; yes, there is.
197And now 'tis better thought, for, cheater, know
198This juggling shall not save you.

WELLBORN

This juggling shall not save you. To save thee
199Would beggar the stock of mercy.

OVERREACH

Would beggar the stock of mercy. Marrall!

MARALL

Would beggar the stock of mercy. Marrall! Sir?

OVERREACH

200(flattering him) Though the witnesses are dead, your testimony—
201Help with an oath or two; and for thy master,
202Thy liberal master, my good, honest servant,
203I know thou wilt swear anything to dash
204This cunning sleight. Besides, I know thou art
205A public notary, and such stand in law
206For a dozen witnesses. The deed being drawn too
207By thee, my careful Marrall, and delivered
208When thou wert present, will make good my title.
209Wilt thou not swear this?

MARALL

Wilt thou not swear this? I? No, I assure you.
210I have a conscience not seared up like yours;
211I know no deeds.

OVERREACH

I know no deeds. Wilt thou betray me?

MARALL

I know no deeds. Wilt thou betray me? [to the others] Keep him
212From using of his hands, I'll use my tongue
213To his no little torment.

OVERREACH

To his no little torment. [as they restrain him] Mine own varlet
214Rebel against me?

MARALL

Rebel against me? Yes, and uncase you, too.
215The idiot, the patch, the slave, the booby,
216The property fit only to be beaten
217For your morning exercise, your football, or
218Th'unprofitable lump of flesh, your drudge,
219Can now anatomize you, and lay open
220All your black plots, and level with the earth
221Your hill of pride, and, with these gabions guarded,
222Unload my great artillery and shake,
223Nay, pulverize the walls you think defend you.

LADY ALLWORTH

224How he foams at the mouth with rage!

WELLBORN

How he foams at the mouth with rage! To him again.

OVERREACH

225Oh, that I had thee in my grip! I would tear thee
226Joint after joint!

MARALL

Joint after joint! I know you are a tearer,
227But I'll have first your fangs pared off, and then
228Come nearer to you, when I have discovered
229And made it good before the judge what ways
230And devilish practices you used to cozen with
231An army of whole families, who, yet alive
232And but enrolled for soldiers, were able
233To take in Dunkirk.

WELLBORN

To take in Dunkirk. All will come out.

LADY ALLWORTH

To take in Dunkirk. All will come out. The better.

OVERREACH

234But that I will live, rogue, to torture thee
235And make thee wish—and kneel in vain—to die,
236These swords that keep thee from me should fix here,
237Although they made my body but one wound,
238But I would reach thee.

LOVELL

But I would reach thee. Heav'n's hand is in this!aparte
239One bandog worry the other!aparte

OVERREACH

One bandog worry the other! I play the fool,
240And make my anger but ridiculous.
241There will be a time and place—there will be, cowards!—
242When you shall feel what I dare do.

WELLBORN

When you shall feel what I dare do. I think so.
243You dare do any ill, yet want true valor
244To be honest and repent.

OVERREACH

To be honest and repent. They are words I know not,
245Nor e'er will learn. Patience, the beggar's virtue,
246Shall find no harbor here.

Enter Greedy and Parson Willdo.

Shall find no harbor here. After these storms,
247At length a calm appears. Welcome, most welcome!
248There's comfort in thy looks. Is the deed done?
249Is my daughter married? Say but so, my chaplain,
250And I am tame.

WILLDO

And I am tame. Married? Yes, I assure you.

OVERREACH

251Then vanish all sad thoughts!
Then vanish all sad thoughts! [Offering money] There's more gold for thee.
252My doubts and fears are in the titles drowned
253Of my Right Honorable, my Right Honorable daughter.

GREEDY

254Here will be feasting! At least for a month
255I am provided. Empty guts, croak no more!
256You shall be stuffed like bagpipes, not with wind
257But bearing dishes.

OVERREACH

But bearing dishes. (whispering to Willdo) Instantly be here?
258To my wish, to my wish! Now you that plot against me,
259And hoped to trip my heels up, that contemned me,
260Think on't, and tremble. (Loud music.) They come! I hear the music.
261A lane there for my lord!

WELLBORN

A lane there for my lord! This sudden heat
262May yet be cooled, sir.

OVERREACH

May yet be cooled, sir. Make way there for my lord!

Enter Allworth and Margaret.

MARGARET

263(kneeling) Sir, first your pardon, then your blessing, with
264Your full allowance of the choice I have made,
265As ever you could make use of your reason.
266Grow not in passion, since you may as well
267Call back the day that's past as untie the knot
268Which is too strongly fastened. Not to dwell
269Too long on words, this's my husband.

OVERREACH

Too long on words, this's my husband. How!

ALLWORTH

270So I assure you. All the rites of marriage,
271With every circumstance, are past. Alas, sir,
272Although I am no lord, but a lord's page,
273Your daughter and my loved wife mourns not for it;
274And, for "Right Honorable son-in-law," you may say
275"Your dutiful daughter."

OVERREACH

"Your dutiful daughter." Devil! Are they married?

WILLDO

276Do a father's part, and say, "Heav'n give 'em joy!"

OVERREACH

277Confusion and ruin! Speak, and speak quickly,
278Or thou art dead.

WILLDO

Or thou art dead. They are married.

OVERREACH

Or thou art dead. They are married. Thou hadst better
279Have made a contract with the king of fiends
280Than these. My brain turns!

WILLDO

Than these. My brain turns! Why this rage to me?
281Is not this your letter, sir, and these the words?
282"Marry her to this gentleman."

OVERREACH

"Marry her to this gentleman." It cannot—
283Nor will I e'er believe it—'sdeath, I will not—
284That I, that in all passages I touched
285At worldly profit have not left a print
286Where I have trod for the most curious search
287To trace my footsteps, should be gulled by children,
288Baffled and fooled, and all my hopes and labors
289Defeated and made void!

WELLBORN

Defeated and made void! As it appears,
290You are so, my grave uncle.

OVERREACH

You are so, my grave uncle. Village nurses
291Revenge their wrongs with curses. I'll not waste
292A syllable, but thus I take the life
293Which wretched I gave to thee.

Offers to kill Margaret.

LOVELL

Which wretched I gave to thee. [intervening] Hold, for your own sake!
294Though charity to your daughter hath quite left you,
295Will you do an act, though in your hopes lost here,
296Can leave no hope for peace or rest hereafter?
297Consider: at the best you are but a man,
298And cannot so create your aims but that
299They may be crossed.

OVERREACH

They may be crossed. Lord, thus I spit at thee
300And at thy counsel, and again desire thee,
301As thou art a soldier, if thy valor
302Dares show itself where multitude and example
303Lead not the way, let's quit the house and change
304Six words in private.

LOVELL

Six words in private. I am ready, sir.

LADY ALLWORTH

Six words in private. I am ready, sir. [to Lovell] Stay, sir!
305Contest with one distracted?

WELLBORN

Contest with one distracted? You'll grow like him,
306Should you answer his vain challenge.

OVERREACH

Should you answer his vain challenge. [to Lovell] Are you pale?
307Borrow his help. Though Hercules call it odds,
308I'll stand against both, as I am, hemmed in thus.
309Since, like a Libyan lion in the toil,
310My fury cannot reach the coward hunters
311And only spends itself, I'll quit the place.
312Alone I can do nothing, but I have servants
313And friends to second me; and, if I make not
314This house a heap of ashes—by my wrongs,
315What I have spoke I will make good!—or leave
316One throat uncut, if it be possible,
317Hell add to my afflictions!

Exit Overreach.

MARALL

Hell add to my afflictions! Is't not brave sport?

GREEDY

318Brave sport? I am sure it has ta'en away my stomach;
319I do not like the sauce.

ALLWORTH

I do not like the sauce. [to Margaret] Nay, weep not, dearest,
320Though it express your pity; what's decreed
321Above, we cannot alter.

LADY ALLWORTH

Above, we cannot alter. His threats move me
322No scruple, madam.

MARALL

No scruple, madam. [to Wellborn] Was it not a rare trick,
323An it please Your Worship, to make the deed nothing?
324I can do twenty neater, if you please
325To purchase and grow rich, for I will be
326Such a solicitor and steward for you
327As never worshipful had.

WELLBORN

As never worshipful had. I do believe thee.
328But first discover the quaint means you used
329To raze out the conveyance.

MARALL

To raze out the conveyance. They are mysteries
330Not to be spoke in public: certain minerals
331Incorporated in the ink and wax.
332Besides, he gave me nothing, but still fed me
333With hopes and blows; and that was the inducement
334To this conundrum. If it please Your Worship
335To call to memory, this mad beast once caused me
336To urge you or to drown or hang yourself;
337I'll do the like to him, if you command me.

WELLBORN

338You are a rascal! He that dares be false
339To a master, though unjust, will ne'er be true
340To any other. Look not for reward
341Or favor from me; I will shun thy sight
342As I would do a basilisk's. Thank my pity
343If thou keep thy ears. Howe'er, I will take order
344Your practice shall be silenced.

GREEDY

Your practice shall be silenced. I'll commit him,
345If you'll have me, sir.

WELLBORN

346That were to little purpose.
347His conscience be his prison.—Not a word,
348But instantly begone!

ORDER

But instantly begone! [kicking Marall] Take this kick with you.

AMBLE

349[kicking Marall] And this.

FURNACE

[kicking Marall] And this. If that I had my cleaver here
350I would divide your knave's head.

MARALL

I would divide your knave's head. This is the haven
351False servants still arrive at.

Exit Marall.

Enter Overreach [distracted in his wits].

LADY ALLWORTH

False servants still arrive at. Come again!

LOVELL

352Fear not; I am your guard.

WELLBORN

Fear not; I am your guard. His looks are ghastly.

WILLDO

353Some little time I have spent, under your favors,
354In physical studies, and, if my judgment err not,
355He's mad beyond recovery. But observe him,
356And look to yourselves.

OVERREACH

And look to yourselves. Why, is not the whole world
357Included in myself? To what use then
358Are friends and servants? Say there were a squadron
359Of pikes, lined through with shot; when I am mounted
360Upon my injuries, shall I fear to charge 'em?

Flourishing his sword ensheathed.

361No: I'll through the battalia, and, that routed,
362I'll fall to execution. Ha! I am feeble:
363Some undone widow sits upon mine arm
364And takes away the use of't, and my sword,
365Glued to my scabbard with wronged orphans' tears,
366Will not be drawn. Ha! What are these? Sure, hangmen,
367That come to bind my hands, and then to drag me
368Before the judgment seat! Now they are new shapes
369And do appear like Furies, with steel whips
370To scourge my ulcerous soul! Shall I then fall
371Ingloriously, and yield? No! Spite of Fate,
372I will be forced to hell like to myself;
373Though you were legions of accursèd spirits,
374Thus would I fly among you.

[He runs at them weakly.]

WELLBORN

Thus would I fly among you. There's no help.
375Disarm him first, then bind him.

GREEDY

Disarm him first, then bind him. Take a mittimus
376And carry him to Bedlam.

LOVELL

And carry him to Bedlam. How he foams!

WELLBORN

377And bites the earth!

WILLDO

378Carry him to some dark room.
379There try what art can do for his recovery.

MARGARET

380O my dear father!

They [Order, Amble, Furnace, etc.] force Overreach off.

ALLWORTH

O my dear father! You must be patient, mistress.

LOVELL

381Here is a precedent to teach wicked men
382That, when they leave religion and turn atheists,
383Their own abilities leave 'em. Pray you, take comfort.
384I will endeavor you shall be his guardians
385In his distractions. And for your land, Master Wellborn,
386Be it good or ill in law, I'll be an umpire
387Between you and this, th'undoubted heir
388Of Sir Giles Overreach. For me, here's the anchor
389That I must fix on.

ALLWORTH

That I must fix on. What you shall determine,
390My lord, I will allow of.

WELLBORN

My lord, I will allow of. 'Tis the language
391That I speak, too. But there is something else
392Beside the repossession of my land
393And payment of my debts that I must practice.
394I had a reputation, but 'twas lost
395In my loose course; and, till I redeem it
396Some noble way, I am but half made up.
397It is a time of action. If Your Lordship
398Will please to confer a company upon me
399In your command, I doubt not in my service
400To my king and country but I shall do something
401That may make me right again.

LOVELL

That may make me right again. Your suit is granted,
402And you loved for the motion.

WELLBORN

And you loved for the motion. Nothing wants then
403But your allowance—

The Epilogue

1But your allowance, and in that our all
2Is comprehended, it being known nor we
3Nor he that wrote the comedy can be free
4Without your manumission—which if you
5Grant willingly, as a fair favor due
6To the poet's and our labors (as you may,
7For we despair not, gentlemen, of the play),
8We jointly shall profess Your Grace hath might
9To teach us action, and him how to write.

[Exeunt.]
