Act IV

[SCENE I]

Enter BEL-IMPERIA and HIERONIMO.

BEL-IMPERIA

1Is this the love thou bear’st Horatio?
2Is this the kindness that thou counterfeits?
3Are these the fruits of thine incessant tears?
4Hieronimo, are these thy passions,
5Thy protestations and thy deep laments,
6That thou wert wont to weary men withal?
7O unkind father! O deceitful world!
8With what excuses canst thou show thyself
9From this dishonour and the hate of men?
10Thus to neglect the loss and life of him
11Whom both my letters and thine own belief
12Assures thee to be causeless slaughtered!
13Hieronimo, for shame, Hieronimo,
14Be not a history to after-times
15Of such ingratitude unto thy son:
16Unhappy mothers of such children then,
17But monstrous fathers to forget so soon
18The death of those, whom they, with care and cost
19Have tender’d so, thus careless should be lost.
20Myself, a stranger in respect of thee,
21So lov’d his life, as still I wish their deaths.
22Nor shall his death be unreveng’d by me,
23Although I bear it out for fashion’s sake:
24For here I swear, in sight of heaven and earth,
25Shouldst thou neglect the love thou shouldst retain,
26And give it over, and devise no more,
27Myself should send their hateful souls to hell,
28That wrought his downfall with extremest death.

HIERONIMO

29But may it be that Bel-imperia
30Vows such revenge as she hath deign’d to say?
31Why, then I see that heaven applies our drift,
32And all the saints do sit soliciting
33For vengeance on those cursèd murderers.
34Madam, ’tis true, and now I find it so,
35I found a letter, written in your name,
36And in that letter, how Horatio died.
37Pardon, O pardon, Bel-imperia,
38My fear and care in not believing it;
39Nor think I thoughtless think upon a mean
40To let his death be unreveng’d at full.
41And here I vow —so you but give consent,
42And will conceal my resolution —
43I will ere long determine of their deaths
44That causeless thus have murderèd my son.

BEL-IMPERIA

45Hieronimo, I will consent, conceal,
46And aught that may effect for thine avail,
47Join with thee to revenge Horatio’s death.

HIERONIMO

48On, then; whatsoever I devise,
49Let me entreat you, grace my practices,
50For why the plot’s already in mine head.
51Here they are.

Enter BALTHAZAR and LORENZO.

BALTHAZAR

Here they are. How now, Hieronimo?
52What, courting Bel-imperia?

HIERONIMO

What, courting Bel-imperia? Ay, my lord;
53Such courting as (I promise you)
54She hath my heart; but you, my lord, have hers.

LORENZO

55But now, Hieronimo, or never,
56We are to entreat your help.

HIERONIMO

We are to entreat your help. My help?
57Why, my good lords, assure yourselves of me;
58For you have giv’n me cause,
59Ay, by my faith, have you!

BALTHAZAR

Ay, by my faith, have you! It pleas’d you,
60At the entertainment of the ambassador,
61To grace the King so much as with a show.
62Now, were your study so well furnished,
63As for the passing of the first night’s sport
64To entertain my father with the like,
65Or any such-like pleasing motion,
66Assure yourself, it would content them well.

HIERONIMO

67Is this all?

BALTHAZAR

Is this all? Ay, this is all.

HIERONIMO

68Why then, I’ll fit you: say no more.
69When I was young, I gave my mind
70And plied myself to fruitless poetry;
71Which though it profit the professor naught,
72Yet is it passing pleasing to the world.

LORENZO

73And how for that?

HIERONIMO

And how for that? Marry, my good lord, thus:
74(And yet methinks you are too quick with us)
75When in Toledo there I studièd,
76It was my chance to write a tragedy:
77See here, my lords –

He shows them a book.

78Which, long forgot, I found this other day.
79Now would your lordships favour me so much
80As but to grace me with your acting it —
81I mean each one of you to play a part —
82Assure you it will prove most passing strange,
83And wondrous plausible to that assembly.

BALTHAZAR

84What, would you have us play a tragedy?

HIERONIMO

85Why, Nero thought it no disparagement,
86And kings and emperors have ta’en delight
87To make experience of their wits in plays.

LORENZO

88Nay, be not angry, good Hieronimo;
89The Prince but ask’d a question.

BALTHAZAR

90In faith, Hieronimo, an you be in earnest,
91I’ll make one.

LORENZO

I’ll make one. And I another.

HIERONIMO

92Now, my good lord, could you entreat
93Your sister Bel-imperia to make one?
94For what’s a play without a woman in it?

BEL-IMPERIA

95Little entreaty shall serve me, Hieronimo;
96For I must needs be employèd in your play.

HIERONIMO

97Why, this is well: I tell you, lordings,
98It was determinèd to have been acted,
99By gentlemen and scholars too,
100Such as could tell what to speak.

BALTHAZAR

101And now it shall be play’d by princes and courtiers,
102Such as can tell how to speak:
103If, as it is our country manner,
104You will but let us know the argument.

HIERONIMO

105That shall I roundly. The chronicles of Spain
106Record this written of a knight of Rhodes:
107He was betroth’d and wedded at the length,
108To one Perseda, an Italian dame,
109Whose beauty ravish’d all that her beheld,
110Especially the soul of Soliman,
111Who at the marriage was the chiefest guest.
112By sundry means sought Soliman to win
113Perseda’s love, and could not gain the same.
114Then ‘gan he break his passions to a friend,
115One of his bashaws, whom he held full dear;
116Her had this bashaw long solicited,
117And saw she was not otherwise to be won,
118But by her husband’s death, this knight of Rhodes,
119Whom presently by treachery he slew.
120She, stirr’d with an exceeding hate therefor,
121As cause of this slew Soliman,
122And, to escape the bashaw’s tyranny.
123Did stab herself: and this the tragedy.

LORENZO

124Oh, excellent!

BEL-IMPERIA

Oh, excellent! But say, Hieronimo,
125What then became of him that was the bashaw?

HIERONIMO

126Marry, thus: mov’d with remorse of his misdeeds,
127Ran to a mountain-top and hung himself.

BALTHAZAR

128But which of us is to perform that part?

HIERONIMO

129O, that will I, my lords; make no doubt of it:
130I’ll play the murderer, I warrant you,
131For I already have conceited that.

BALTHAZAR

132And what shall I?

HIERONIMO

133Great Suleiman, the Turkish emperor.

LORENZO

134And I?

HIERONIMO

And I? Erastus, the Knight of Rhodes.

BEL-IMPERIA

135And I?

HIERONIMO

And I? Perseda, chaste and resolute. —
136And here, my lords, are several abstracts drawn,
137For each of you to note your parts,
138And act it, as occasion’s offer’d you.
139You must provide [you with] a Turkish cap,
140A black mustachio and a falchion;

Gives a paper to Balthazar.

141You with a cross, like to a knight of Rhodes;

Gives another to Lorenzo.

142And, madam, you must attire yourself

He giveth Bel-imperia another.

143Like Phoebe, Flora, or the Huntress,
144Which to your discretion shall seem best.
145And as for me, my lords, I’ll look to one,
146And with the ransom that the Viceroy sent,
147So furnish and perform this tragedy,
148As all the world shall say, Hieronimo
149Was liberal in gracing of it so.

BALTHAZAR

150Hieronimo, methinks a comedy were better.

HIERONIMO

151A comedy?
152Fie! comedies are fit for common wits:
153But to present a kingly troop withal,
154Give me a stately-written tragedy;
155Tragedia cothurnata, fitting kings,
156Containing matter, and not common things.
157My lords, all this must be perform’d
158As fitting for the first night’s revelling.
159The Italian tragedians were so sharp of wit,
160That in one hour’s meditation
161They would perform anything in action.

LORENZO

162And well it may; for I have seen the like
163In Paris ’mongst the French tragedians.

HIERONIMO

164In Paris? mass, and well rememberèd!
165There’s one thing more that rests for us to do.

BALTHAZAR

166What’s that, Hieronimo? Forget not anything.

HIERONIMO

167Each one of us
168Must act his part in unknown languages,
169That it may breed the more variety:
170As you, my lord, in Latin, I in Greek,
171You in Italian, and for because I know
172That Bel-imperia hath practis’d the French,
173In courtly French shall all her phrases be.

BEL-IMPERIA

174You mean to try my cunning, then, Hieronimo?

BALTHAZAR

175But this will be a mere confusion,
176And hardly shall we all be understood.

HIERONIMO

177It must be so, for the conclusion
178Shall prove the invention and all was good:
179And I myself in an oration,
180And with a strange and wondrous show besides,
181That I will have there behind a curtain,
182Assure yourself, shall make the matter known:
183And all shall be concluded in one scene,
184For there’s no pleasure ta’en in tediousness.

BALTHAZAR

185How like you this?

LORENZO

How like you this? Why, thus, my lord:
186We must resolve to soothe his humours up.

BALTHAZAR

187On, then, Hieronimo; Farewell till soon.

HIERONIMO

188You’ll ply this gear?

LORENZO

You’ll ply this gear? I warrant you.

Exeunt all but Hieronimo.

HIERONIMO

You’ll ply this gear? I warrant you. Why so:
189Now shall I see the fall of Babylon,
190Wrought by the heav’ns in this confusion.
191And if the world like not this tragedy,
192Hard is the hap of old Hieronimo.

Exit.

[SCENE II]

Enter ISABELLA with a weapon.

ISABELLA

1Tell me no more! —O monstrous homicides!
2Since neither piety nor pity moves
3The King to justice or compassion,
4I will revenge myself upon this place,
5Where thus they murder’d my belovèd son.

She cuts down the arbour.

6Down with these branches and these loathsome boughs
7Of this unfortunate and fatal pine:
8Down with them, Isabella; rend them up,
9And burn the roots from whence the rest is sprung.
10I will not leave a root, a stalk, a tree,
11A bough, a branch, a blossom, nor a leaf,
12No, not an herb within this garden-plot —
13Accursèd complot of my misery!
14Fruitless for ever may this garden be,
15Barren the earth, and blissless whosoe’er
16Imagines not to keep it unmanur’d!
17An eastern wind commix’d with noisome airs,
18Shall blast the plants and the young saplings;
19The earth with serpents shall be pester’d,
20And passengers, for fear to be infect,
21Shall stand aloof, and, looking at it, tell:
22“There, murder’d, died the son of Isabel.”
23Ay, here he died, and here I him embrace:
24See, where his ghost solicits, with his wounds,
25Revenge on her that should revenge his death.
26Hieronimo, make haste to see thy son;
27For sorrow and despair hath cited me
28To hear Horatio plead with Rhadamanth:
29Make haste, Hieronimo, to hold excus’d
30Thy negligence in pursuit of their deaths
31Whose hateful wrath bereav’d him of his breath. —
32Ah, nay, thou dost delay their deaths,
33Forgives the murderers of thy noble son,
34And none but I bestir me—to no end!
35And as I curse this tree from further fruit,
36So shall my womb be cursèd for his sake,
37And with this weapon will I wound the breast,
38The hapless breast, that gave Horatio suck.

She stabs herself.[Exit]

[SCENE III.]

Enter HIERONIMO; he knocks up the curtain.

Enter the Duke of CASTILE.

CASTILE

1How now, Hieronimo, where’s your fellows,
2That you take all this pain?

HIERONIMO

3O sir, it is for the author’s credit,
4To look that all things may go well.
5But, good my lord, let me entreat your grace
6To give the King the copy of the play:
7This is the argument of what we show.

CASTILE

8I will, Hieronimo.

HIERONIMO

I will, Hieronimo. One thing more, my good lord.

CASTILE

9What’s that?

HIERONIMO

What’s that? Let me entreat your grace
10That, when the train are pass’d into the gallery,
11You would vouchsafe to throw me down the key.

CASTILE

12I will, Hieronimo.

Exit Castile.

HIERONIMO

13What, are you ready, Balthazar?
14Bring a chair and a cushion for the King.

Enter BALTHAZAR, with a chair.

15Well done, Balthazar! Hang up the title:
16Our scene is Rhodes; —what, is your beard on?

BALTHAZAR

17Half on; the other is in my hand.

HIERONIMO

18Despatch, for shame, are you so long?

Exit Balthazar.

19Bethink thyself, Hieronimo,
20Recall thy wits, recount thy former wrongs
21Thou hast receiv’d by murder of thy son,
22And lastly, not least, how Isabel,
23Once his mother and thy dearest wife,
24All woe-begone for him, hath slain herself.
25Behoves thee then, Hieronimo, to be reveng’d:
26The plot is laid of dire revenge:
27On then, Hieronimo, pursue revenge,
28For nothing wants but acting of revenge.

Exit Hieronimo.

[SCENE IV.]

Enter Spanish KING, VICEROY, the Duke of CASTILE, and their train.

KING

1Now, Viceroy, shall we see the tragedy
2Of Soliman, the Turkish emperor,
3Perform’d of pleasure by your son the prince,
4My nephew Don Lorenzo, and my niece.

VICEROY

5Who? Bel-imperia?

KING

6Ay, and Hieronimo, our marshal,
7At whose request they deign to do’t themselves:
8These be our pastimes in the court of Spain.
9Here, brother, you shall be the book-keeper:
10This is the argument of that they show.

He giveth him a book.

Gentlemen, this play of Hieronimo, in sundry languages, was thought good to be set down in English more largely, for the easier understanding to every public reader.

Enter BALTHAZAR, BEL-IMPERIA, and HIERONIMO.

BALTHAZAR

11Bashaw, that Rhodes is ours, yield heavens the honour,
12And holy Mahomet, our sacred prophet!
13And be thou grac’d with every excellence
14That Soliman can give, or thou desire.
15But thy desert in conquering Rhodes is less
16Than in reserving this fair Christian nymph,
17Perseda, blissful lamp of excellence,
18Whose eyes compel, like powerful adamant,
19The warlike heart of Suleiman to wait.

KING

20See, Viceroy, that is Balthazar, your son,
21That represents the emperor Soliman:
22How well he acts his amorous passion!

VICEROY

23Ay, Bel-imperia hath taught him that.

CASTILE

24That’s because his mind runs all on Bel-imperia.

HIERONIMO

25Whatever joy earth yields betide your majesty.

BALTHAZAR

26Earth yields no joy without Perseda’s love.

HIERONIMO

27Let then Perseda on your grace attend.

BALTHAZAR

28She shall not wait on me, but I on her:
29Drawn by the influence of her lights, I yield.
30But let my friend, the Rhodian knight, come forth,
31Erasto, dearer than my life to me,
32That he may see Perseda, my belov’d.

Enter [LORENZO as] Erasto.

KING

33Here comes Lorenzo: look upon the plot,
34And tell me, brother, what part plays he?

BEL-IMPERIA

35Ah, my Erasto, welcome to Perseda.

LORENZO

36Thrice happy is Erasto that thou liv’st;
37Rhodes’ loss is nothing to Erasto’s joy:
38Sith his Perseda lives, his life survives.

BALTHAZAR

39Ah, bashaw, here is love between Erasto
40And fair Perseda, sovereign of my soul.

HIERONIMO

41Remove Erasto, mighty Soliman,
42And then Perseda will be quickly won.

BALTHAZAR

43Erasto is my friend; and while he lives,
44Perseda never will remove her love.

HIERONIMO

45Let not Erasto live to grieve great Soliman.

BALTHAZAR

46Dear is Erasto in our princely eye.

HIERONIMO

47But if he be your rival, let him die.

BALTHAZAR

48Why, let him die! —so love commandeth me.
49Yet grieve I that Erasto should so die.

HIERONIMO

50Erasto, Soliman saluteth thee,
51And lets thee wit by me his highness’ will,
52Which is, thou shouldst be thus employ’d.

Stabs him.

BEL-IMPERIA

Which is, thou shouldst be thus employ’d. Ay me!
53Erasto! see, Soliman, Erasto’s slain!

BALTHAZAR

54Yet liveth Soliman to comfort thee.
55Fair queen of beauty, let not favour die,
56But with a gracious eye behold his grief,
57That with Perseda’s beauty is increas’d
58If by Perseda grief be not releas’d.

BEL-IMPERIA

59Tyrant, desist soliciting vain suits;
60Relentless are mine ears to thy laments,
61As thy butcher is pitiless and base,
62Which seiz’d on my Erasto, harmless knight.
63Yet by thy power thou thinkest to command,
64And to thy power Perseda doth obey:
65But, were she able, thus she would revenge
66Thy treacheries on thee, ignoble Prince:

Stabs him.

67And on herself she would be thus reveng’d.

Stabs herself.

KING

68Well said, old Marshal, this was bravely done!

HIERONIMO

69But Bel-imperia plays Perseda well!

VICEROY

70Were this in earnest, Bel-imperia,
71You would be better to my son than so.

KING

72But now what follows for Hieronimo?

HIERONIMO

73Marry, this follows for Hieronimo:
74Here break we off our sundry languages,
75And thus conclude I in our vulgar tongue.
76Haply you think —but bootless are your thoughts —
77That this is fabulously counterfeit,
78And that we do as all tragedians do:
79To die to-day (for fashioning our scene)
80The death of Ajax, or some Roman peer,
81And in a minute starting up again,
82Revive to please to-morrow’s audience.
83No, princes; know I am Hieronimo,
84The hopeless father of a hapless son,
85Whose tongue is tun’d to tell his latest tale,
86Not to excuse gross errors in the play.
87I see your looks urge instance of these words;
88Behold the reason urging me to this:

Shows his dead son.

89See here my show, look on this spectacle:
90Here lay my hope, and here my hope hath end;
91Here lay my heart, and here my heart was slain;
92Here lay my treasure, here my treasure lost;
93Here lay my bliss, and here my bliss bereft:
94But hope, heart, treasure, joy and bliss,
95All fled, fail’d, died, yea, all decay’d with this.
96From forth these wounds came breath that gave me life;
97They murder’d me that made these fatal marks.
98The cause was love, whence grew this mortal hate;
99The hate, Lorenzo and young Balthazar;
100The love, my son to Bel-imperia.
101But night, the coverer of accursèd crimes,
102With pitchy silence hush’d these traitors’ harms,
103And lent them leave, for they had sorted leisure
104To take advantage in my garden-plot
105Upon my son, my dear Horatio:
106There merciless, they butcher’d up my boy,
107In black, dark night, to pale, dim, cruel death.
108He shrieks: I heard (and yet, methinks, I hear)
109His dismal outcry echo in the air.
110With soonest speed I hasted to the noise,
111Where hanging on a tree I found my son,
112Through-girt with wounds, and slaughter’d as you see.
113And griev’d I, think you, at this spectacle?
114Speak, Portuguese, whose loss resembles mine:
115If thou canst weep upon thy Balthazar,
116’Tis like I wail’d for my Horatio.
117And you, my lord, whose reconcilèd son
118March’d in a net, and thought himself unseen,
119And rated me for brainsick lunacy,
120With “God amend that mad Hieronimo” —
121How can you brook our play’s catastrophe?
122And here behold this bloody handkercher,
123Which at Horatio’s death I weeping dipp’d
124Within the river of his bleeding wounds:
125It as propitious, see, I have reserv’d,
126And never hath it left my bloody heart,
127Soliciting remembrance of my vow
128With these, O, these accurs’d murderers:
129Which now perform’d, my heart is satisfied.
130And to this end the bashaw I became
131That might revenge me on Lorenzo’s life,
132Who therefore was appointed to the part,
133And was to represent the Knight of Rhodes,
134That I might kill him more conveniently.
135So, Viceroy, was this Balthazar, thy son,
136That Soliman which Bel-imperia,
137In person of Perseda murderèd,
138Solely appointed to that tragic part
139That she might slay him that offended her.
140Poor Bel-imperia miss’d her part in this:
141For though the story saith she should have died,
142Yet I of kindness and of care to her,
143Did otherwise determine of her end;
144But love of him whom they did hate too much
145Did urge her resolution to be such. —
146And, princes, now behold Hieronimo,
147Author and actor in this tragedy,
148Bearing his latest fortune in his fist;
149And will as resolute conclude his part,
150As any of the actors gone before.
151And, gentles, thus I end my play;
152Urge no more words: I have no more to say.

He runs to hang himself.

KING

153O hearken, Viceroy! Hold, Hieronimo!
154Brother, my nephew and thy son are slain!

VICEROY

155We are betray’d; My Balthazar is slain!
156Break ope the doors; run, save Hieronimo!

[They break in and hold Hieronimo.]

157Hieronimo,
158Do but inform the King of these events;
159Upon mine honour, thou shalt have no harm.

HIERONIMO

160Viceroy, I will not trust thee with my life,
161Which I this day have offer’d to my son.
162Accursèd wretch!
163Why stay’st thou him that was resolv’d to die?

KING

164Speak, traitor! damned, bloody murderer, speak!
165For now I have thee, I will make thee speak.
166Why hast thou done this undeserving deed?

VICEROY

167Why hast thou murderèd my Balthazar?

CASTILE

168Why hast thou butcher’d both my children thus?

HIERONIMO

169§ Oh, good words!
170As dear to me was my Horatio,
171As yours, or yours, or yours, my lord, to you.
172My guiltless son was by Lorenzo slain,
173And by Lorenzo and that Balthazar
174Am I at last revenged thoroughly,
175Upon whose souls may heavens be yet avenged
176With greater far than these afflictions.

CASTILE

177But who were thy confederates in this?

VICEROY

178That was thy daughter Bel-imperia,
179For by her hand my Balthazar was slain.
180I saw her stab him.

KING

181Why speak’st thou not?

HIERONIMO

182What lesser liberty can kings afford
183Than harmless silence? Then afford it me.
184Sufficeth I may not, nor I will not, tell thee.

KING

185Fetch forth the tortures! —Traitor as thou art,
186I’ll make thee tell.

HIERONIMO

I’ll make thee tell. Indeed
187Thou mayst torment me as his wretched son
188Hath done in murdering my Horatio.
189But never shalt thou force me to reveal
190The thing which I have vowed inviolate.
191And therefore, in despite of all thy threats,
192Pleased with their deaths, and eased with their revenge,
193First take my tongue and afterwards my heart. §
194<sup> Q4 "Q4" «[The previous twenty-four lines, signposted between § signs, are replaced, in all the quartos from 1602 onwards, by the following “addition”.]» </sup> But are you sure they are dead?

CASTILE

<sup> Q4 "Q4" «[The previous twenty-four lines, signposted between § signs, are replaced, in all the quartos from 1602 onwards, by the following “addition”.]» </sup> But are you sure they are dead? Ay, slave, too sure.

HIERONIMO

195What, and yours too?

VICEROY

196Ay, all are dead; not one of them survive.

HIERONIMO

197Nay, then I care not; come, and we shall be friends,
198Let us lay our heads together.
199See, here’s a goodly noose will hold them all.

VICEROY

200O damnèd devil, how secure he is!

HIERONIMO

201Secure? Why, dost thou wonder at it?
202I tell thee, Viceroy, this day I have seen revenge,
203And in that sight am grown a prouder monarch,
204Than ever sat under the crown of Spain.
205Had I as many lives as there be stars,
206As many heavens to go, to as those lives,
207I’d give them all, ay, and my soul to boot,
208But I would see thee ride in this red pool.

CASTILE

209Speak! Who were thy confederates in this?

VICEROY

210That was thy daughter Bel-imperia,
211For by her hand my Balthazar was slain.
212I saw her stab him.

HIERONIMO

213Oh, good words!
214As dear to me was my Horatio,
215As yours, or yours, or yours, my lord, to you.
216My guiltless son was by Lorenzo slain,
217And by Lorenzo and that Balthazar
218Am I at last reveng’d thoroughly,
219Upon whose souls may heavens be yet aveng’d
220With greater far than these afflictions.
221Methinks since I grew inward with revenge,
222I cannot look with scorn enough on death.

KING

223What, dost thou mock us, slave? bring tortures forth.

HIERONIMO

224Do, do, do: and meantime I’ll torture you.
225You had a son, as I take it; and your son
226Should ha’ been married to your daughter:
227Ha, was’t not so? —You had a son too,
228He was my liege’s nephew, he was proud
229And politic; had he liv’d, he might have come
230To wear the crown of Spain (I think ’twas so):
231’Twas I that kill’d him; look you, this same hand,
232’Twas it that stabb’d his heart — do you see this hand?
233For one Horatio, if you ever knew him: a youth,
234One that they hang’d up in his father’s garden:
235One that did force your valiant son to yield,
236While your more valiant son did take him prisoner.

VICEROY

237Be deaf, my senses; I can hear no more.

KING

238Fall, heav’n, and cover us with thy sad ruins.

CASTILE

239Roll all the world within thy pitchy cloud.

HIERONIMO

240Now do I applaud what I have acted.
241Nunc iners cadat manus!
242Now to express the rupture of my part,
243First take my tongue, and afterward my heart. </sup> Q4 <sup>

[He bites out his tongue.]

KING

244O monstrous resolution of a wretch!
245See, Viceroy, he hath bitten forth his tongue,
246Rather than to reveal what we requir’d.

CASTILE

247Yet can he write.

KING

248And if in this he satisfy us not,
249We will devise th’ extremest kind of death
250That ever was invented for a wretch.

Then he makes signs for a knife to mend his pen.

CASTILE

251O he would have a knife to mend his pen.

VICEROY

252Here, and advise thee that thou write the troth.

KING

253Look to my brother! Save Hieronimo!

Hieronimo with a knife stabs the duke and himself.

254What age hath ever heard such monstrous deeds?
255My brother, and the whole succeeding hope
256That Spain expected after my decease! —
257Go, bear his body hence, that we may mourn
258The loss of our belovèd brother’s death,
259That he may be entomb’d whate’er befall;
260I am the next, the nearest, last of all.

VICEROY

261And thou, Don Pedro, do the like for us:
262Take up our hapless son, untimely slain;
263Set me with him, and he with woeful me,
264Upon the main-mast of a ship unmann’d,
265And let the wind and tide haul me along
266To Scylla’s barking and untamèd gulf,
267Or to the loathsome pool of Acheron,
268To weep my want for my sweet Balthazar:
269Spain hath no refuge for a Portingale.

The trumpets sound a dead march; the King of Spain

270mourning after his brother’s body, and the King of
271Portingale bearing the body of his son. [Exeunt.]

[SCENE V.]

GHOST and REVENGE.

GHOST

1Ay, now my hopes have end in their effects,
2When blood and sorrow finish my desires:
3Horatio murder’d in his father’s bower,
4Vile Serberine by Pedringano slain;
5False Pedringano hang’d by quaint device;
6Fair Isabella by herself misdone;
7Prince Balthazar by Bel-imperia stabb’d;
8The Duke of Castile and his wicked son
9Both done to death by old Hieronimo;
10My Bel-imperia fall’n as Dido fell,
11And good Hieronimo slain by himself:
12Ay, these were spectacles to please my soul! —
13Now will I beg at lovely Proserpine
14That, by the virtue of her princely doom,
15I may consort my friends in pleasing sort,
16And on my foes work just and sharp revenge.
17I’ll lead my friend Horatio through those fields,
18Where never-dying wars are still inur’d;
19I’ll lead fair Isabella to that train,
20Where pity weeps, but never feeleth pain;
21I’ll lead my Bel-imperia to those joys,
22That vestal virgins and fair queens possess;
23I’ll lead Hieronimo where Orpheus plays,
24Adding sweet pleasure to eternal days.
25But say, Revenge —for thou must help, or none —
26Against the rest how shall my hate be shown?

REVENGE

27This hand shall hale them down to deepest hell
28Where none but Furies, bugs and tortures dwell.

GHOST

29Then, sweet Revenge, do this at my request:
30Let me be judge, and doom them to unrest.
31Let loose poor Tityus from the vulture’s gripe,
32And let Don Cyprian supply his room;
33Place Don Lorenzo on Ixion’s wheel,
34And let the lover’s endless pains surcease
35(Juno forgets old wrath, and grants him ease);
36Hang Balthazar about Chimaera’s neck,
37And let him there bewail his bloody love,
38Repining at our joys that are above;
39Let Serberine go roll the fatal stone,
40And take from Sisyphus his endless moan;
41False Pedringano, for his treachery,
42Let him be dragg’d through boiling Acheron,
43And there live, dying still in endless flames,
44Blaspheming gods and all their holy names.

REVENGE

45Then haste we down to meet thy friends and foes:
46To place thy friends in ease, the rest in woes;
47For here though death hath end their misery,
48I’ll there begin their endless tragedy.

Exeunt.
