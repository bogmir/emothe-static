The Spanish Tragedy

Act I

[SCENE I]

Enter the Ghost of Andrea, and with him Revenge.

GHOST

1When this eternal substance of my soul
2Did live imprison’d in my wanton flesh,
3Each in their function serving other’s need,
4I was a courtier in the Spanish court.
5My name was Don Andrea; my descent,
6Though not ignoble, yet inferior far
7To gracious fortunes of my tender youth:
8For there in prime and pride of all my years,
9By duteous service and deserving love,
10In secret I possess’d a worthy dame,
11Which hight sweet Bel-imperia by name.
12But in the harvest of my summer joys
13Death’s winter nipp’d the blossoms of my bliss,
14Forcing divorce betwixt my love and me.
15For in the late conflict with Portingale
16My valour drew me into danger’s mouth
17Till life to death made passage through my wounds.
18When I was slain, my soul descended straight
19To pass the flowing stream of Acheron;
20But churlish Charon, only boatman there,
21Said that, my rites of burial not perform’d,
22I might not sit amongst his passengers.
23Ere Sol had slept three nights in Thetis’ lap
24And slaked his smoking chariot in her flood,
25By Don Horatio, our Knight Marshal’s son,
26My funerals and obsequies were done.
27Then was the ferryman of hell content
28To pass me over to the slimy strand
29That leads to fell Avernus’ ugly waves.
30There, pleasing Cerberus with honey’d speech,
31I pass’d the perils of the foremost porch.
32Not far from hence, amidst ten thousand souls,
33Sat Minos, Aeacus and Rhadamanth,
34To whom no sooner gan I make approach
35To crave a passport for my wandering ghost,
36But Minos’ engraven leaves of lottery
37Drew forth the manner of my life and death.
38‘This knight’, quoth he, ‘both liv’d and died in love,
39And for his love tried fortune of the wars,
40And by war’s fortune lost both love and life’.
41‘Why then’, said Aeacus, ‘convey him hence
42To walk with lovers in our fields of love,
43And spend the course of everlasting time
44Under green myrtle trees and cypress shades’.
45‘No, no’, said Rhadamanth, ‘it were not well
46With loving souls to place a martialist.
47He died in war, and must to martial fields,
48Where wounded Hector lives in lasting pain,
49And Achilles’ Myrmidons do scour the plain’.
50Then Minos, mildest censor of the three,
51Made this device to end the difference:
52‘Send him’, quoth he, ‘to our infernal king
53To doom him as best seems his majesty’.
54To this effect my passport straight was drawn.
55In keeping on my way to Pluto’s court
56Through dreadful shades of ever-glooming night,
57I saw more sights than thousand tongues can tell,
58Or pens can write, or mortal hearts can think.
59Three ways there were. That on the right hand side
60Was ready way unto the foresaid fields
61Where lovers live and bloody martialists,
62But either sort contain’d within his bounds.
63The left hand path, declining fearfully,
64Was ready downfall to the deepest hell,
65Where bloody Furies shakes their whips of steel,
66And poor Ixion turns an endless wheel;
67Where usurers are chok’d with melting gold,
68And wantons are embrac’d with ugly snakes,
69And murderers groan with never-killing wounds,
70And perjur’d wights scalded in boiling lead,
71And all foul sins with torments overwhelm’d.
72’Twixt these two ways I trod the middle path,
73Which brought me to the fair Elysian green,
74In midst whereof there stands a stately tower,
75The walls of brass, the gates of adamant.
76Here finding Pluto with his Proserpine,
77I show’d my passport, humbled on my knee,
78Whereat fair Proserpine began to smile,
79And begg’d that only she might give my doom.
80Pluto was pleas’d and seal’d it with a kiss.
81Forthwith, Revenge, she rounded thee in th’ear,
82And bade thee lead me through the gates of horn,
83Where dreams have passage in the silent night.
84No sooner had she spoke but we were here —
85I wot not how —in twinkling of an eye.

REVENGE

Then know, Andrea, that thou art arriv’d

87Where thou shalt see the author of thy death,
88Don Balthazar, the Prince of Portingale,
89Depriv’d of life by Bel-imperia.
90Here sit we down to see the mystery,
91And serve for chorus in this tragedy.

[SCENE II]

Enter SPANISH KING, GENERAL, CASTILE, and HIERONIMO.

KING

1Now say, Lord General, how fares our camp?

GENERAL

2All well, my sovereign liege, except some few
3That are deceas'd by fortune of the war.

KING

4But what portends thy cheerful countenance
5And posting to our presence thus in haste?
6Speak, man, hath fortune given us victory?

GENERAL

7Victory, my liege, and that with little loss.

KING

8Our Portingales will pay us tribute then?

GENERAL

9Tribute and wonted homage therewithal.

KING

10Then bless'd be heaven and guider of the heavens,
11From whose fair influence such justice flows.

CASTILE

12O multum dilecte Deo, tibi militat aether,
13Et conjuratae curvato poplite gentes
14Succumbunt: recti soror est victoria juris.

KING

15Thanks to my loving brother of Castile.
16But, General, unfold in brief discourse
17Your form of battle and your war’s success,
18That, adding all the pleasure of thy news
19Unto the height of former happiness,
20With deeper wage and greater dignity
21We may reward thy blissful chivalry.

GENERAL

22Where Spain and Portingale do jointly knit
23Their frontiers, leaning on each other’s bound,
24There met our armies in their proud array;
25Both furnish'd well, both full of hope and fear,
26Both menacing alike with daring shows,
27Both vaunting sundry colours of device,
28Both cheerly sounding trumpets, drums and fifes,
29Both raising dreadful clamours to the sky,
30That valleys, hills, and rivers made rebound,
31And heaven itself was frighted with the sound.
32Our battles both were pitch'd in squadron form,
33Each corner strongly fenc'd with wings of shot;
34But ere we join'd and came to push of pike,
35I brought a squadron of our readiest shot
36From out our rearward to begin the fight;
37They brought another wing to encounter us.
38Meanwhile, our ordnance play'd on either side,
39And captains strove to have their valours tried.
40Don Pedro, their chief horsemen’s colonel,
41Did with his cornet bravely make attempt
42To break the order of our battle ranks.
43But Don Rogero, worthy man of war,
44March'd forth against him with our musketeers,
45And stopp'd the malice of his fell approach
46While they maintain hot skirmish too and fro,
47Both battles join and fall to handy blows,
48Their violent shot resembling th’ocean’s rage
49When, roaring loud and with a swelling tide,
50It beats upon the rampiers of huge rocks
51And gapes to swallow neighbour-bounding lands.
52Now while Bellona rageth here and there,
53Thick storms of bullets rain like winter’s hail,
54And shivered lances dark the troubled air.
55Pede pes et cuspide cuspis;
56Arma sonant armis, vir petiturque viro.
57On every side drop captains to the ground,
58And soldiers, some ill-maim’d, some slain outright;
59Here falls a body sundered from his head,
60There legs and arms lie bleeding on the grass,
61Mingled with weapons and unbowell’d steeds
62That, scattering, overspread the purple plain.
63In all this turmoil, three long hours and more
64The victory to neither part inclined,
65Till Don Andrea with his brave lancers
66In their main battle made so great a breach
67That, half dismayed, the multitude retired.
68But Balthazar, the Portingales’ young prince,
69Brought rescue and encouraged them to stay.
70Here-hence the fight was eagerly renewed,
71And in that conflict was Andrea slain,
72Brave man-at-arms, but weak to Balthazar.
73Yet while the Prince, insulting over him,
74Breathed out proud vaunts sounding to our reproach,
75Friendship and hardy valour, joined in one,
76Pricked forth Horatio, our Knight Marshal’s son,
77To challenge forth that Prince in single fight.
78Not long between these twain the fight endured,
79But straight the Prince was beaten from his horse
80And forced to yield him prisoner to his foe.
81When he was taken, all the rest they fled,
82And our carbines pursued them to the death
83Till, Phoebus waning to the western deep,
84Our trumpeters were charged to sound retreat.

KING

85Thanks, good Lord General, for these good news,
86And, for some argument of more to come,
87Take this and wear it for thy sovereign’s sake.

Gives him his chain.

88But tell me now, hast thou confirmed a peace?

GENERAL

89No peace, my liege, but peace conditional,
90That if with homage tribute be well paid,
91The fury of your forces will be stayed.
92And to this peace their Viceroy hath subscribed,

Gives the King a paper.

93And made a solemn vow that during life
94His tribute shall be truly paid to Spain.

KING

95These words, these deeds, become thy person well. —
96But now, Knight Marshal, frolic with thy King,
97For ’tis thy son that wins this battle’s prize.

HIERONIMO

98Long may he live to serve my sovereign liege,
99And soon decay unless he serve my liege.

KING

100Nor thou nor he shall die without reward.

A tucket afar off.

101What means this warning of this trumpet’s sound?

GENERAL

102This tells me that your grace’s men of war,
103Such as war’s fortune hath reserved from death,
104Come marching on towards your royal seat
105To show themselves before your majesty —
106For so I gave in charge at my depart —
107Whereby by demonstration shall appear
108That all, except three hundred or few more,
109Are safe returned and by their foes enriched.

The army enters; BALTHAZAR, between LORENZO and HORATIO, captive.

KING

110A gladsome sight! I long to see them here!

They enter and pass by.

111Was that the warlike Prince of Portingale,
112That by our nephew was in triumph led?

GENERAL

113It was, my liege, the Prince of Portingale.

KING

114But what was he that on the other side
115Held him by th’ arm as partner of the prize?

HIERONIMO

116That was my son, my gracious sovereign,
117Of whom though from his tender infancy
118My loving thoughts did never hope but well,
119He never pleas’d his father’s eyes till now,
120Nor fill’d my heart with over-cloying joys.

KING

121Go, let them march once more about these walls,
122That, staying them, we may confer and talk
123With our brave prisoner and his double guard.
124Hieronimo, it greatly pleaseth us
125That in our victory thou have a share
126By virtue of thy worthy son’s exploit.

Enter again.

127Bring hither the young Prince of Portingale:
128The rest march on, but ere they be dismiss’d,
129We will bestow on every soldier
130Two ducats, and on every leader ten,
131That they may know our largess welcomes them.

Exeunt all [the army] but Balthazar, Lorenzo, and Horatio.

132Welcome, Don Balthazar; welcome, nephew;
133And thou, Horatio, thou art welcome too.
134Young Prince, although thy father’s hard misdeeds
135In keeping back the tribute that he owes
136Deserve but evil measure at our hands,
137Yet shalt thou know that Spain is honourable.

BALTHAZAR

138The trespass that my father made in peace
139Is now controll’d by fortune of the wars;
140And cards once dealt, it boots not ask why so.
141His men are slain, a weakening to his realm;
142His colours seiz’d, a blot unto his name;
143His son distress’d, a corsive to his heart:
144These punishments may clear his late offence.

KING

145Ay, Balthazar, if he observe this truce,
146Our peace will grow the stronger for these wars.
147Meanwhile, live thou, though not in liberty,
148Yet free from bearing any servile yoke;
149For, in our hearing, thy deserts were great,
150And, in our sight, thyself art gracious.

BALTHAZAR

151And I shall study to deserve this grace.

KING

152But tell me, —for their holding makes me doubt—
153To which of these twain art thou prisoner?

LORENZO

154To me, my liege.

HORATIO

To me, my liege. To me, my sovereign.

LORENZO

155This hand first took his courser by the reins.

HORATIO

156But first my lance did put him from his horse.

LORENZO

157I seiz’d his weapon and enjoy’d it first.

HORATIO

158But first I forc’d him lay his weapons down.

KING

159Let go his arm, upon our privilege.

Let him go.

160Say, worthy Prince, to whether did’st thou yield?

BALTHAZAR

161To him in courtesy, to this perforce:
162He spake me fair, this other gave me strokes;
163He promis’d life, this other threaten’d death;
164He won my love, this other conquer’d me;
165And truth to say, I yield myself to both.

HIERONIMO

166But that I know your grace for just and wise,
167And might seem partial in this difference,
168Enforc’d by nature and by law of arms
169My tongue should plead for young Horatio’s right.
170He hunted well that was a lion’s death,
171Not he that in a garment wore his skin;
172So hares may pull dead lions by the beard.

KING

173Content thee, Marshal, thou shalt have no wrong,
174And, for thy sake, thy son shall want no right.
175Will both abide the censure of my doom?

LORENZO

176I crave no better than your grace awards.

HORATIO

177Nor I, although I sit beside my right.

KING

178Then by my judgement, thus your strife shall end:
179You both deserve and both shall have reward.
180Nephew, thou took’st his weapon and his horse:
181His weapons and his horse are thy reward.
182Horatio, thou did’st force him first to yield:
183His ransom therefore is thy valour’s fee;
184Appoint the sum as you shall both agree.
185But, nephew, thou shalt have the Prince in guard,
186For thine estate best fitteth such a guest:
187Horatio’s house were small for all his train.
188Yet, in regard thy substance passeth his,
189And that just guerdon may befall desert,
190To him we yield the armour of the Prince.
191How likes Don Balthazar of this device?

BALTHAZAR

192Right well, my liege, if this provison were,
193That Don Horatio bear us company,
194Whom I admire and love for chivalry.

KING

195Horatio, leave him not that loves thee so.
196Now let us hence to see our soldiers paid,
197And feast our prisoner as our friendly guest.

Exeunt.

[SCENE III]

Enter VICEROY, ALEXANDRO, VILLUPPO [and Attendants].

VICEROY

1Is our Ambassador dispatch’d for Spain?

ALEXANDRO

2Two days, my liege, are pass’d since his depart.

VICEROY

3And tribute-payment gone along with him?

ALEXANDRO

4Ay, my good lord.

VICEROY

5Then rest we here awhile in our unrest,
6And feed our sorrows with some inward sighs,
7For deepest cares break never into tears.
8But wherefore sit I in a regal throne?
9This better fits a wretch’s endless moan.

Falls to the ground.

10Yet this is higher than my fortunes reach,
11And therefore better than my state deserves.
12Ay, ay, this earth, image of melancholy,
13Seeks him whom fates adjudge to misery.
14Here let me lie; now am I at the lowest.
15Qui jacet in terra, non habet unde cadat.
16In me consumpsit vires fortuna nocendo:
17Nil superest ut jam possit obesse magis.
18Yes, Fortune may bereave me of my crown:
19Here, take it now; — let Fortune do her worst,
20She will not rob me of this sable weed:
21O no, she envies none but pleasant things.
22Such is the folly of despiteful chance!
23Fortune is blind, and sees not my deserts;
24So is she deaf, and hears not my laments;
25And could she hear, yet is she wilful mad,
26And therefore will not pity my distress.
27Suppose that she could pity me, what then?
28What help can be expected at her hands
29Whose foot is standing on a rolling stone,
30And mind more mutable than fickle winds?
31Why wail I then, where’s hope of no redress?
32O yes, complaining makes my grief seem less.
33My late ambition hath distain’d my faith,
34My breach of faith occasion’d bloody wars,
35Those bloody wars have spent my treasure,
36And with my treasure my people’s blood,
37And with their blood, my joy and best belov’d,
38My best belov’d, my sweet and only son.
39Oh, wherefore went I not to war myself?
40The cause was mine; I might have died for both:
41My years were mellow, his but young and green;
42My death were natural, but his was forc’d.

ALEXANDRO

43No doubt, my liege, but still the Prince survives.

VICEROY

44Survives! ay, where?

ALEXANDRO

45In Spain, a prisoner by mischance of war.

VICEROY

46Then they have slain him for his father’s fault.

ALEXANDRO

47That were a breach to common law of arms.

VICEROY

48They reck no laws that meditate revenge.

ALEXANDRO

49His ransom’s worth will stay from foul revenge.

VICEROY

50No, if he liv’d the news would soon be here.

ALEXANDRO

51Nay, evil news fly faster still than good.

VICEROY

52Tell me no more of news, for he is dead.

VILLUPPO

53My sovereign, pardon the author of ill news,
54And I’ll bewray the fortune of thy son.

VICEROY

55Speak on, I’ll guerdon thee whate’er it be:
56Mine ear is ready to receive ill news,
57My heart grown hard ’gainst mischief’s battery.
58Stand up, I say, and tell thy tale at large.

VILLUPPO

59[Rises.] Then hear that truth which these mine eyes have seen:
60When both the armies were in battle join’d,
61Don Balthazar, amidst the thickest troops,
62To win renown, did wondrous feats of arms:
63Amongst the rest I saw him hand to hand
64In single fight with their Lord general;
65Till Alexandro, that here counterfeits
66Under the colour of a duteous friend,
67Discharg’d his pistol at the Prince’s back,
68As though he would have slain their general:
69But therewithal Don Balthazar fell down;
70And when he fell, then we began to fly:
71But had he liv’d, the day had sure been ours.

ALEXANDRO

72O wicked forgery! O traitorous miscreant!

VICEROY

73Hold thou thy peace! But now, Villuppo, say,
74Where then became the carcase of my son?

VILLUPPO

75I saw them drag it to the Spanish tents.

VICEROY

76Ay, ay, my nightly dreams have told me this. —
77Thou false, unkind, unthankful traitorous beast,
78Wherein had Balthazar offended thee
79That thou shouldst thus betray him to our foes?
80Was’t Spanish gold that bleared so thine eyes
81That thou couldst see no part of our deserts?
82Perchance because thou art Terceira’s lord,
83Thou hadst some hope to wear this diadem,
84If first my son and then myself were slain;
85But thy ambitious thought shall break thy neck.
86Ay, this was it that made thee spill his blood:

Takes the crown and puts it on again.

87But I’ll now wear it till thy blood be spilt.

ALEXANDRO

88Vouchsafe, dread sovereign, to hear me speak.

VICEROY

89Away with him; his sight is second hell.
90Keep him till we determine of his death.

[Exit Alexandro, guarded.]

91If Balthazar be dead, he shall not live.
92Villuppo, follow us for thy reward.

Exit Viceroy.

VILLUPPO

93Thus have I with an envious, forged tale
94Deceiv’d the King, betray’d mine enemy,
95And hope for guerdon of my villainy.

Exit.

[SCENE IV]

Enter HORATIO and BEL-IMPERIA.

BEL-IMPERIA

1Signor Horatio, this is the place and hour
2Wherein I must entreat thee to relate
3The circumstance of Don Andrea’s death,
4Who, living, was my garland’s sweetest flower,
5And in his death hath buried my delights.

HORATIO

6For love of him and service to yourself,
7I nill refuse this heavy doleful charge;
8Yet tears and sighs, I fear, will hinder me.
9When both our armies were enjoin’d in fight,
10Your worthy chevalier amidst the thick’st,
11For glorious cause still aiming at the fairest,
12Was at the last by young Don Balthazar
13Encounter’d hand to hand: Their fight was long,
14Their hearts were great, their clamours menacing,
15Their strength alike, their strokes both dangerous.
16But wrathful Nemesis, that wicked power,
17Envying at Andrea’s praise and worth,
18Cut short his life to end his praise and worth.
19She, she herself, disguis’d in armour’s mask —
20As Pallas was before proud Pergamus —
21Brought in a fresh supply of halberdiers,
22Which paunch’d his horse and ding’d him to the ground.
23Then young Don Balthazar with ruthless rage,
24Taking advantage of his foe’s distress,
25Did finish what his halberdiers begun,
26And left not, till Andrea’s life was done.
27Then, though too late, incens’d with just remorse,
28I with my band set forth against the prince,
29And brought him prisoner from his halberdiers.

BEL-IMPERIA

30Would thou hadst slain him that so slew my love!
31But then was Don Andrea’s carcase lost?

HORATIO

32No, that was it for which I chiefly strove,
33Nor stepp’d I back till I recover’d him:
34I took him up, and wound him in mine arms,
35And, wielding him unto my private tent,
36There laid him down, and dew’d him with my tears,
37And sigh’d and sorrow’d as became a friend.
38But neither friendly sorrow, sighs, nor tears
39Could win pale death from his usurpèd right.
40Yet this I did, and less I could not do:
41I saw him honour’d with due funeral.
42This scarf I pluck’d from off his lifeless arm,
43And wear it in remembrance of my friend.

BEL-IMPERIA

44I know the scarf: would he had kept it still;
45For, had he liv’d, he would have kept it still,
46And worn it for his Bel-imperia’s sake:
47For ’twas my favour at his last depart.
48But now wear thou it both for him and me;
49For, after him, thou hast deserv’d it best.
50But, for thy kindness in his life and death,
51Be sure, while Bel-imperia’s life endures,
52She will be Don Horatio’s thankful friend.

HORATIO

53And, madam, Don Horatio will not slack
54Humbly to serve fair Bel-imperia.
55But now, if your good liking stand thereto,
56I’ll crave your pardon to go seek the prince;
57For so the Duke, your father, gave me charge.Exit.

BEL-IMPERIA

58Ay, go, Horatio, leave me here alone;
59For solitude best fits my cheerless mood.
60Yet what avails to wail Andrea’s death,
61From whence Horatio proves my second love?
62Had he not lov’d Andrea as he did,
63He could not sit in Bel-imperia’s thoughts.
64But how can love find harbour in my breast,
65Till I revenge the death of my belov’d?
66Yes, second love shall further my revenge.
67I’ll love Horatio, my Andrea’s friend,
68The more to spite the Prince that wrought his end;
69And where Don Balthazar, that slew my love,
70Himself now pleads for favour at my hands,
71He shall, in rigour of my just disdain,
72Reap long repentance for his murderous deed.
73For what was’t else but murderous cowardice,
74So many to oppress one valiant knight,
75Without respect of honour in the fight?
76And here he comes that murder’d my delight.

Enter LORENZO and BALTHAZAR.

LORENZO

77Sister, what means this melancholy walk?

BEL-IMPERIA

78That for a while I wish no company.

LORENZO

79But here the Prince is come to visit you.

BEL-IMPERIA

80That argues that he lives in liberty.

BALTHAZAR

81No, madam, but in pleasing servitude.

BEL-IMPERIA

82Your prison then belike is your conceit.

BALTHAZAR

83Ay, by conceit my freedom is enthrall’d.

BEL-IMPERIA

84Then with conceit enlarge yourself again.

BALTHAZAR

85What if conceit have laid my heart to gage?

BEL-IMPERIA

86Pay that you borrow’d and recover it.

BALTHAZAR

87I die, if it return from whence it lies.

BEL-IMPERIA

88A heartless man, and live? A miracle!

BALTHAZAR

89Ay, lady, love can work such miracles.

LORENZO

90Tush, tush, my lord! let go these ambages,
91And in plain terms acquaint her with your love.

BEL-IMPERIA

92What boots complaint when there’s no remedy?

BALTHAZAR

93Yes, to your gracious self must I complain,
94In whose fair answer lies my remedy,
95On whose perfection all my thoughts attend,
96In whose aspect mine eyes find beauty’s bower,
97In whose translucent breast my heart is lodg’d.

BEL-IMPERIA

98Alas, my lord, these are but words of course,
99And but devise to drive me from this place.

She, in going in, lets fall her glove, which Horatio, coming out, takes up.

HORATIO

100Madam, your glove.

BEL-IMPERIA

101Thanks, good Horatio; take it for thy pains.

BALTHAZAR

102Signior Horatio stoop’d in happy time.

HORATIO

103I reap’d more grace than I deserv’d or hop’d.

LORENZO

104My lord, be not dismay’d for what is past:
105You know that women oft are humorous;
106These clouds will overblow with little wind;
107Let me alone, I’ll scatter them myself.
108Meanwhile let us devise to spend the time
109In some delightful sports and revelling.

HORATIO

110The King, my lords, is coming hither straight
111To feast the Portingale ambassador;
112Things were in readiness before I came.

BALTHAZAR

113Then here it fits us to attend the King,
114To welcome hither our ambassador,
115And learn my father and my country’s health.

Enter the Banquet, Trumpets, the KING and Ambassador.

KING

116See, lord Ambassador, how Spain entreats
117Their prisoner Balthazar, thy Viceroy’s son:
118We pleasure more in kindness than in wars.

AMBASSADOR

119Sad is our King, and Portingale laments,
120Supposing that Don Balthazar is slain.

BALTHAZAR

121So am I slain, by beauty’s tyranny. —aparte
122You see, my lord, how Balthazar is slain:
123I frolic with the Duke of Castile’s son,
124Wrapp’d every hour in pleasures of the court,
125And grac’d with favours of his majesty.

KING

126Put off your greetings till our feast be done.
127Now come and sit with us, and taste our cheer.

Sit to the banquet.

128Sit down, young Prince, you are our second guest;
129Brother, sit down; and, nephew, take your place.
130Signior Horatio, wait thou upon our cup,
131For well thou hast deserved to be honour’d.
132Now, lordings, fall to. Spain is Portugal,
133And Portugal is Spain; we both are friends,
134Tribute is paid, and we enjoy our right.
135But where is old Hieronimo, our Marshal?
136He promis’d us in honour of our guest,
137To grace our banquet with some pompous jest.

Enter HIERONIMO, with a Drum, three Knights, each [hangs up] his scutcheon; Then he fetches three Kings, they take their crowns and them captive.

138Hieronimo, this masque contents mine eye,
139Although I sound not well the mystery.

HIERONIMO

140The first arm’d knight that hung his scutcheon up,

He takes the scutcheon and gives it to the King.

141Was English Robert, Earl of Gloucester,
142Who, when King Stephen bore sway in Albion,
143Arriv’d with five and twenty thousand men
144In Portingale and by success of war
145Enforc’d the king, then but a Saracen,
146To bear the yoke of the English monarchy.

KING

147My lord of Portingale, by this you see
148That which may comfort both your King and you,
149And make your late discomfort seem the less.
150But say, Hieronimo, what was the next?

HIERONIMO

151The second knight that hung his scutcheon up

He doth as he did before.

152Was Edmund, Earl of Kent in Albion,
153When English Richard wore the diadem.
154He came likewise and razed Lisbon walls,
155And took the king of Portingale in fight;
156For which, and other such-like service done,
157He after was created Duke of York.

KING

158This is another special argument
159That Portingale may deign to bear our yoke,
160When it by little England hath been yok’d.
161But now, Hieronimo, what were the last?

HIERONIMO

162The third and last, not least in our account,

Doing as before

163Was, as the rest, a valiant Englishman,
164Brave John of Gaunt, the Duke of Lancaster,
165As by his scutcheon plainly may appear.
166He with a puissant army came to Spain,
167And took our King of Castile prisoner.

AMBASSADOR

168This is an argument for our Viceroy
169That Spain may not insult for her success,
170Since English warriors likewise conquer’d Spain
171And made them bow their knees to Albion.

KING

172Hieronimo, I drink to thee for this device,
173Which hath pleas’d both the ambassador and me:
174Pledge me, Hieronimo, if thou love thy King.

Takes the cup of Horatio.

175My lord, I fear we sit but over-long,
176Unless our dainties were more delicate;
177But welcome are you to the best we have.
178Now let us in, that you may be dispatch’d:
179I think our council is already set.

Exeunt omnes.

[SCENE V]

Ghost of Andrea, Revenge.

ANDREA

1Come we for this from depth of underground,
2To see him feast that gave me my death’s wound?
3These pleasant sights are sorrow to my soul:
4Nothing but league, and love, and banqueting?

REVENGE

5Be still, Andrea; ere we go from hence,
6I’ll turn their friendship into fell despite,
7Their love to mortal hate, their day to night,
8Their hope into despair, their peace to war,
9Their joys to pain, their bliss to misery.
