Act II

[SCENE I]

Enter LORENZO and BALTHAZAR.

LORENZO

1My lord, though Bel-imperia seem thus coy,
2Let reason hold you in your wonted joy:
3In time the savage bull sustains the yoke,
4In time all haggard hawks will stoop to lure,
5In time small wedges cleave the hardest oak,
6In time the flint is pierc’d with softest shower,
7And she in time will fall from her disdain,
8And rue the suff’rance of your friendly pain.

BALTHAZAR

9No, she is wilder and more hard withal.
10Than beast, or bird, or tree, or stony wall.
11But wherefore blot I Bel-imperia’s name?
12It is my fault, not she, that merits blame.
13My feature is not to content her sight,
14My words are rude and work her no delight.
15The lines I send her are but harsh and ill,
16Such as do drop from Pan and Marsyas’ quill.
17My presents are not of sufficient cost,
18And being worthless, all my labour’s lost.
19Yet might she love me for my valiancy:
20Ay, but that’s slandered by captivity.
21Yet might she love me to content her sire:
22Ay, but her reason masters his desire.
23Yet might she love me as her brother’s friend:
24Ay, but her hopes aim at some other end.
25Yet might she love me to uprear her state:
26Ay, but perhaps she hopes some nobler mate.
27Yet might she love me as her beauty’s thrall:
28Ay, but I fear she cannot love at all.

LORENZO

29My lord, for my sake, leave these ecstasies,
30And doubt not but we’ll find some remedy.
31Some cause there is that lets you not be lov’d;
32First that must needs be known and then remov’d.
33What if my sister love some other knight?

BALTHAZAR

34My summer’s day will turn to winter’s night.

LORENZO

35I have already found a stratagem,
36To sound the bottom of this doubtful theme.
37My lord, for once you shall be rul’d by me;
38Hinder me not, whate’er you hear or see.
39By force or fair means will I cast about
40To find the truth of all this question out.
41Ho, Pedringano!

PEDRINGANO

42Signior!

LORENZO

43Vien qui presto.

Enter PEDRINGANO.

PEDRINGANO

44Hath your lordship any service to command me?

LORENZO

45Ay, Pedringano, service of import;
46And, not to spend the time in trifling words,
47Thus stands the case: it is not long, thou know’st,
48Since I did shield thee from my father’s wrath,
49For thy conveyance in Andrea’s love,
50For which thou wert adjudg’d to punishment:
51I stood betwixt thee and thy punishment,
52And since, thou know’st how I have favour’d thee.
53Now to these favours will I add reward,
54Not with fair words, but store of golden coin,
55And lands and living join’d with dignities,
56If thou but satisfy my just demand:
57Tell truth and have me for thy lasting friend.

PEDRINGANO

58Whate’er it be your lordship shall demand,
59My bounden duty bids me tell the truth.
60If case it lie in me to tell the truth.

LORENZO

61Then, Pedringano, this is my demand:
62Whom loves my sister Bel-imperia?
63For she reposeth all her trust in thee.
64Speak, man, and gain both friendship and reward:
65I mean, whom loves she in Andrea’s place?

PEDRINGANO

66Alas, my lord, since Don Andrea’s death
67I have no credit with her as before;
68And therefore know not if she love or no.

LORENZO

69Nay, if thou dally, then I am thy foe,

<sup> Q4 </sup> [Draws his sword.] </sup> Q4 <sup>

70And fear shall force what friendship cannot win:
71Thy death shall bury what thy life conceals;
72Thou diest for more esteeming her than me.

PEDRINGANO

73Oh, stay, my lord!

LORENZO

74Yet speak the truth and I will guerdon thee,
75And shield thee from whatever can ensue,
76And will conceal whate’er proceeds from thee.
77But if thou dally once again, thou diest.

PEDRINGANO

78If Madam Bel-imperia be in love —

LORENZO

79What, villain! ifs and ands?

<sup> Q4 </sup> [Offers to kill him.] </sup> Q4 <sup>

PEDRINGANO

80Oh, stay, my lord! She loves Horatio.

Balthazar starts back.

LORENZO

81What? Don Horatio, our Knight Marshal’s son?

PEDRINGANO

82Even him, my lord.

LORENZO

83Now say but how tohu know’st he is her love,
84And thou shalt find me kind and liberal:
85Stand up, I say, and fearless tell the truth.

PEDRINGANO

86She sent him letters, which myself perus’d,
87Full-fraught with lines and arguments of love,
88Preferring him before Prince Balthazar.

LORENZO

89Swear on this cross that what thou say’st is true,
90And that thou wilt conceal what thou hast told.

PEDRINGANO

91I swear to both, by him that made us all.

LORENZO

92In hope thine oath is true, here’s thy reward:
93But if I prove thee perjur’d and unjust,
94This very sword whereon thou took’st thine oath,
95Shall be the worker of thy tragedy.

PEDRINGANO

96What I have said is true, and shall, for me,
97Be still conceal’d from Bel-imperia.
98Besides, your honour’s liberality
99Deserves my duteous service, even till death.

LORENZO

100Let this be all that thou shalt do for me:
101Be watchful when and where these lovers meet,
102And give me notice in some secret sort.

PEDRINGANO

103I will, my lord.

LORENZO

104Then shalt thou find that I am liberal.
105Thou know’st that I can more advance thy state
106Than she; be therefore wise and fail me not.
107Go and attend her as thy custom is,
108Lest absence make her think thou dost amiss.

Exit Pedringano.

109Why so, tam armis quam ingenio :
110Where words prevail not, violence prevails;
111But gold doth more than either of them both.
112How likes Prince Balthazar this stratagem?

BALTHAZAR

113Both well and ill; it makes me glad and sad:
114Glad, that I know the hinderer of my love;
115Sad, that I fear she hates me whom I love.
116Glad, that I know on whom to be reveng’d;
117Sad, that she’ll fly me if I take revenge.
118Yet must I take revenge or die myself,
119For love resisted grows impatient.
120I think Horatio be my destin’d plague:
121First in his hand he brandished a sword,
122And with that sword he fiercely waged war,
123And in that war he gave me dangerous wounds,
124And by those wounds he forced me to yield,
125And by my yielding I became his slave.
126Now in his mouth he carries pleasing words,
127Which pleasing words do harbour sweet conceits,
128Which sweet conceits are lim’d with sly deceits,
129Which sly deceits smooth Bel-imperia’s ears,
130And through her ears dive down into her heart,
131And in her heart set him where I should stand.
132Thus hath he ta’en my body by his force,
133And now by sleight would captivate my soul:
134But in his fall I’ll tempt the destinies,
135And either lose my life, or win my love.

LORENZO

136Let’s go, my lord; your staying stays revenge.
137Do you but follow me, and gain your love:
138Her favour must be won by his remove.

Exeunt.

[SCENE II]

Enter HORATIO and BEL-IMPERIA.

HORATIO

1Now, madam, since by favour of your love
2Our hidden smoke is turn’d to open flame,
3And that with looks and words we feed our thoughts
4(Two chief contents, where more cannot be had),
5Thus, in the midst of love’s fair blandishments,
6Why show you sign of inward languishments?

PEDRINGANO showeth all to the Prince and LORENZO, placing them in secret [above].

BEL-IMPERIA

7My heart, sweet friend, is like a ship at sea:
8She wisheth port, where, riding all at ease,
9She may repair what stormy times have worn,
10And leaning on the shore, may sing with joy
11That pleasure follows pain, and bliss annoy.
12Possession of thy love is th’ only port
13Wherein my heart, with fears and hopes long toss’d,
14Each hour doth wish and long to make resort,
15There to repair the joys that it hath lost,
16And, sitting safe, to sing in Cupid’s quire
17That sweetest bliss is crown of love’s desire.

BALTHAZAR above.

BALTHAZAR

18O sleep, mine eyes, see not my love profan’d;
19Be deaf, my ears, hear not my discontent;
20Die, heart: another joys what thou deserv’st.

LORENZO

21Watch still, mine eyes, to see this love disjoin’d;
22Hear still, mine ears, to hear them both lament;
23Live, heart, to joy at fond Horatio’s fall.

BEL-IMPERIA

24Why stands Horatio speechless all this while?

HORATIO

25The less I speak, the more I meditate.

BEL-IMPERIA

26But whereon dost thou chiefly meditate?

HORATIO

27On dangers past, and pleasures to ensue.

BALTHAZAR

28On pleasures past, and dangers to ensue.

BEL-IMPERIA

29What dangers and what pleasures dost thou mean?

HORATIO

30Dangers of war, and pleasures of our love.

LORENZO

31Dangers of death, but pleasures none at all.

BEL-IMPERIA

32Let dangers go, thy war shall be with me:
33But such a war as breaks no bond of peace.
34Speak thou fair words, I’ll cross them with fair words;
35Send thou sweet looks, I’ll meet them with sweet looks;
36Write loving lines, I’ll answer loving lines;
37Give me a kiss, I’ll countercheck thy kiss:
38Be this our warring peace, or peaceful war.

HORATIO

39But, gracious madam, then appoint the field,
40Where trial of this war shall first be made.

BALTHAZAR

41Ambitious villain, how his boldness grows!

BEL-IMPERIA

42Then be thy father’s pleasant bower the field,
43Where first we vow’d a mutual amity;
44The court were dangerous; that place is safe.
45Our hour shall be when Vesper ‘gins to rise,
46That summons home distressful travellers:
47There none shall hear us but the harmless birds;
48Haply the gentle nightingale
49Shall carol us asleep ere we be ware,
50And, singing with the prickle at her breast,
51Tell our delight and mirthful dalliance.
52Till then each hour will seem a year and more.

HORATIO

53But, honey sweet and honourable love,
54Return we now into your father’s sight:
55Dangerous suspicion waits on our delight.

LORENZO

56Ay, danger mixed with jealous despite
57Shall send thy soul into eternal night.

Exeunt.

[SCENE III]

Enter KING OF SPAIN, Portingale Ambassador, DON CYPRIAN, etc.

KING

1Brother of Castile, to the Prince’s love
2What says your daughter Bel-imperia?

CYPRIAN

3Although she coy it as becomes her kind,
4And yet dissemble that she loves the prince,
5I doubt not, I, but she will stoop in time.
6And were she froward, which she will not be,
7Yet herein shall she follow my advice,
8Which is to love him or forgo my love.

KING

9Then, lord Ambassador of Portingale,
10Advise thy King to make this marriage up,
11For strengthening of our late-confirmed league;
12I know no better means to make us friends.
13Her dowry shall be large and liberal:
14Besides that she is daughter and half-heir
15Unto our brother here, Don Cyprian,
16And shall enjoy the moiety of his land,
17I’ll grace her marriage with an uncle’s gift,
18And this it is: in case the match go forward,
19The tribute which you pay shall be releas’d,
20And if by Balthazar she have a son,
21He shall enjoy the kingdom after us.

AMBASSADOR

22I’ll make the motion to my sovereign liege,
23And work it, if my counsel may prevail.

KING

24Do so, my lord, and, if he give consent,
25I hope his presence here will honour us,
26In celebration of the nuptial day;
27And let himself determine of the time.

AMBASSADOR

28Will’t please your grace command me ought beside?

KING

29Commend me to the King, and so farewell.
30But where’s Prince Balthazar to take his leave?

AMBASSADOR

31That is perform’d already, my good lord.

KING

32Amongst the rest of what you have in charge,
33The prince’s ransom must not be forgot:
34That’s none of mine, but his that took him prisoner,
35And well his forwardness deserves reward:
36It was Horatio, our Knight Marshal’s son.

AMBASSADOR

37Between us there’s a price already pitch’d,
38And shall be sent with all convenient speed.

KING

39Then once again farewell, my lord.

AMBASSADOR

40Farewell, my lord of Castile and the rest.

Exit.

KING

41Now, brother, you must take some little pains
42To win fair Bel-imperia from her will:
43Young virgins must be ruled by their friends.
44The prince is amiable and loves her well;
45If she neglect him and forgo his love,
46She both will wrong her own estate and ours.
47Therefore, whiles I do entertain the prince
48With greatest pleasure that our court affords,
49Endeavour you to win your daughter’s thought:
50If she give back, all this will come to naught.

Exeunt.

[SCENE IV]

Enter HORATIO, BEL-IMPERIA, and PEDRINGANO.

HORATIO

1Now that the night begins with sable wings
2To overcloud the brightness of the sun,
3And that in darkness pleasures may be done,
4Come, Bel-imperia, let us to the bower,
5And there in safety pass a pleasant hour.

BEL-IMPERIA

6I follow thee, my love, and will not back,
7Although my fainting heart controls my soul.

HORATIO

8Why, make you doubt of Pedringano’s faith?

BEL-IMPERIA

9No, he is as trusty as my second self. —
10Go, Pedringano, watch without the gate,
11And let us know if any make approach.

PEDRINGANO

12Instead of watching, I’ll deserve more goldaparte
13By fetching Don Lorenzo to this match.aparte

Exit Pedringano.

HORATIO

14What means my love?

BEL-IMPERIA

What means my love? I know not what myself;
15And yet my heart foretells me some mischance.

HORATIO

16Sweet, say not so; fair fortune is our friend,
17And heavens have shut up day to pleasure us.
18The stars, thou see’st, hold back their twinkling shine,
19And Luna hides herself to pleasure us.

BEL-IMPERIA

20Thou hast prevail’d; I’ll conquer my misdoubt,
21And in thy love and counsel drown my fear.
22I fear no more; love now is all my thoughts.
23Why sit we not? for pleasure asketh ease.

HORATIO

24The more thou sitt’st within these leavy bowers,
25The more will Flora deck it with her flowers.

BEL-IMPERIA

26Ay, but if Flora spy Horatio here,
27Her jealous eye will think I sit too near.

HORATIO

28Hark, madam, how the birds record by night,
29For joy that Bel-imperia sits in sight.

BEL-IMPERIA

30No, Cupid counterfeits the nightingale,
31To frame sweet music to Horatio’s tale.

HORATIO

32If Cupid sing, then Venus is not far:
33Ay, thou art Venus, or some fairer star.

BEL-IMPERIA

34If I be Venus, thou must needs be Mars;
35And where Mars reigneth, there must needs be wars.

HORATIO

36Then thus begin our wars: put forth thy hand,
37That it may combat with my ruder hand.

BEL-IMPERIA

38Set forth thy foot to try the push of mine.

HORATIO

39But first my looks shall combat against thine.

BEL-IMPERIA

40Then ward thyself: I dart this kiss at thee.

HORATIO

41Thus I retort the dart thou threw’st at me.

BEL-IMPERIA

42Nay then, to gain the glory of the field,
43My twining arms shall yoke and make thee yield.

HORATIO

44Nay, then, my arms are large and strong withal:
45Thus elms by vines are compass’d till they fall.

BEL-IMPERIA

46O, let me go; for in my troubled eyes
47Now may’st thou read that life in passion dies.

HORATIO

48O, stay a while, and I will die with thee;
49So shalt thou yield, and yet have conquer’d me.

BEL-IMPERIA

50Who’s there? Pedringano! we are betray’d!

Enter LORENZO, BALTHAZAR, SERBERINE, PEDRINGANO, disguised.

LORENZO

51My lord, away with her, take her aside. —
52O sir, forbear: your valour is already tried.
53Quickly dispatch, my masters.

They hang him in the arbour.

HORATIO

54What, will you murder me?

LORENZO

55Ay, thus, and thus: these are the fruits of love.

They stab him.

BEL-IMPERIA

56O, save his life, and let me die for him!
57O, save him, brother, Save him, Balthazar:
58I lov’d Horatio, but he lov’d not me.

BALTHAZAR

59But Balthazar loves Bel-imperia.

LORENZO

60Although his life were still ambitious-proud,
61Yet is he at the highest now he is dead.

BEL-IMPERIA

62Murder! Murder! Help, Hieronimo, help!

LORENZO

63Come, stop her mouth, away with her.

Exeunt [leaving Horatio’s body].

Enter HIERONIMO in his shirt, etc.

HIERONIMO

64What outcries pluck me from my naked bed,
65And chill my throbbing heart with trembling fear,
66Which never danger yet could daunt before?
67Who calls Hieronimo? Speak, here I am.
68I did not slumber, therefore ’twas no dream.
69No, no, it was some woman cried for help,
70And there within this garden did she cry,
71And in this garden must I rescue her. —
72But stay, what murd’rous spectacle is this?
73A man hang’d up, and all the murderers gone!
74And in my bower, to lay the guilt on me!
75This place was made for pleasure, not for death.

He cuts him down.

76Those garments that he wears I oft have seen.—
77Alas, it is Horatio, my sweet son!
78O no, but he that whilom was my son!
79O, was it thou that call’dst me from my bed?
80O speak, if any spark of life remain:
81I am thy father; who hath slain my son?
82What savage monster, not of human kind,
83Hath here been glutted with thy harmless blood,
84And left thy bloody corpse dishonour’d here,
85For me, amidst these dark and deathful shades,
86To drown thee with an ocean of my tears?
87O heavens, why made you night to cover sin?
88By day this deed of darkness had not been.
89O earth, why didst thou not in time devour
90The vild profaner of this sacred bower?
91O poor Horatio, what hadst thou misdone,
92To leese thy life, ere life was new begun?
93O wicked butcher, whatsoe’er thou wert,
94How could thou strangle virtue and desert?
95Ay me most wretched, that have lost my joy,
96In leesing my Horatio, my sweet boy!

Enter ISABELLA.

ISABELLA

97My husband’s absence makes my heart to throb. —
98Hieronimo!

HIERONIMO

99Here, Isabella, help me to lament;
100For sighs are stopp’d and all my tears are spent.

ISABELLA

101What world of grief — My son Horatio!
102O, where’s the author of this endless woe?

HIERONIMO

103To know the author were some ease of grief;
104For in revenge my heart would find relief.

ISABELLA

105Then is he gone? And is my son gone too?
106O, gush out, tears, fountains and floods of tears;
107Blow, sighs, and raise an everlasting storm;
108For outrage fits our cursed wretchedness.
109<sup> Q4 </sup> Ay me, Hieronimo, sweet husband, speak!

HIERONIMO

110He supp’d with us to-night, frolic and merry,
111And said he would go visit Balthazar
112At the Duke’s palace: there the Prince doth lodge.
113He had no custom to stay out so late;
114He may be in his chamber; Some go see.
115Rodorigo, ho!

Enter PEDRO and JAQUES.

ISABELLA

116Ay me, he raves! Sweet Hieronimo!

HIERONIMO

117True, all Spain takes note of it.
118Besides, he is so generally belov’d;
119His majesty the other day did grace him
120With waiting on his cup: these be favours,
121Which do assure he cannot be short-liv’d.

ISABELLA

122Sweet Hieronimo!

HIERONIMO

123I wonder how this fellow got his clothes!
124Sirrah, sirrah, I’ll know the truth of all:
125Jaques, run to the Duke of Castile’s presently,
126And bid my son Horatio to come home:
127I and his mother have had strange dreams to-night.
128Do ye hear me, sir?

JAQUES

Do ye hear me, sir? Ay, sir.

HIERONIMO

Do ye hear me, sir? Ay, sir. Well, sir, begone.
129Pedro, come hither; Know’st thou who this is?

PEDRO

130Too well, sir.

HIERONIMO

Too well, sir. Too well, who? Who is it? Peace, Isabella!
131Nay, blush not, man.

PEDRO

Nay, blush not, man. It is my lord Horatio.

HIERONIMO

132Ha, ha! Saint James, but this doth make me laugh,
133That there are more deluded than myself.

PEDRO

134Deluded?

HIERONIMO

Deluded? Ay:
135I would have sworn myself, within this hour,
136That this had been my son Horatio,
137His garments are so like.
138Ha! are they not great persuasions?

ISABELLA

139O, would to God it were not so!

HIERONIMO

140Were not, Isabella? Dost thou dream it is?
141Can thy soft bosom entertain a thought,
142That such a black deed of mischief should be done
143On one so pure and spotless as our son?
144Away, I am ashamèd.

ISABELLA

Away, I am ashamèd. Dear Hieronimo,
145Cast a more serious eye upon thy grief:
146Weak apprehension gives but weak belief.

HIERONIMO

147It was a man, sure, that was hanged up here;
148A youth, as I remember: I cut him down.
149If it should prove my son now after all —
150Say you? say you? — Light! Lend me a taper;
151Let me look again. —O God!
152Confusion, mischief, torment, death and hell,
153Drop all your stings at once in my cold bosom,
154That now is stiff with horror: Kill me quickly!
155Be gracious to me, thou infective night,
156And drop this deed of murder down on me;
157Gird in my waste of grief with thy large darkness,
158And let me not survive to see the light
159May put me in the mind I had a son.

ISABELLA

160O sweet Horatio! O my dearest son!

HIERONIMO

161How strangely had I lost my way to grief! </sup> Q4 <sup>
162Sweet lovely rose, ill-pluck’d before thy time,
163Fair worthy son, not conquer’d but betray’d,
164I’ll kiss thee now, for words with tears are stay’d.

ISABELLA

165And I’ll close up the glasses of his sight,
166For once these eyes were only my delight.

HIERONIMO

167See’st thou this handkerchief besmear’d with blood?
168It shall not from me, till I take revenge.
169See’st thou those wounds that yet are bleeding fresh?
170I’ll not entomb them, till I have reveng’d.
171Then will I joy amidst my discontent;
172Till then my sorrow never shall be spent.

ISABELLA

173The heavens are just; murder cannot be hid:
174Time is the author both of truth and right,
175And time will bring this treachery to light.

HIERONIMO

176Meanwhile, good Isabella, cease thy plaints,
177Or, at the least, dissemble them a while:
178So shall we sooner find the practice out,
179And learn by whom all this was brought about.
180Come, Isabel, now let us take him up,

They take him up.

182I’ll say his dirge; singing fits not this case.

[SCENE V]

Ghost of ANDREA, REVENGE.

ANDREA

1Brought’st thou me hither to increase my pain?
2I look’d that Balthazar should have been slain:
3But ’tis my friend Horatio that is slain,
4And they abuse fair Bel-imperia,
5On whom I doted more than all the world,
6Because she lov’d me more than all the world.

REVENGE

7Thou talk’st of harvest when the corn is green:
8The end is crown of every work well done;
9The sickle comes not till the corn be ripe.
10Be still, and ere I lead thee from this place,
11I’ll show thee Balthazar in heavy case.
