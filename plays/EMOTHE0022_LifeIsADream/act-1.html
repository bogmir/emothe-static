Act I

At one side a craggy mountain, at the other a tower, the lower part of which serves as the prison of Sigismund. The door facing the spectators is half open. The action commences at nightfall.

SCENE I.

ROSAURA, CLARIN.

ROSAURA in man's attire appears on the rocky heights and descends to the plain. She is followed by CLARIN.

ROSAURA.

1Wild hippogriff swift speeding,
2Thou that dost run, the winged winds exceeding,
3Bolt which no flash illumes,
4Fish without scales, bird without shifting plumes,
5And brute awhile bereft
6Of natural instinct, why to this wild cleft,
7This labyrinth of naked rocks, dost sweep
8Unreined, uncurbed, to plunge thee down the steep?
9Stay in this mountain wold,
10And let the beasts their Phaeton behold.
11For I, without a guide,
12Save what the laws of destiny decide,
13Benighted, desperate, blind.
14Take any path whatever that doth wind
15Down this rough mountain to its base,
16Whose wrinkled brow in heaven frowns in the sun's bright face.
17Ah, Poland! in ill mood
18Hast thou received a stranger, since in blood
19The name thou writest on thy sands
20Of her who hardly here fares hardly at thy hands.
21My fate may well say so:—
22But where shall one poor wretch find pity in her woe?

CLARIN.

23Say two, if you please;
24Don't leave me out when making plaints like these.
25For if we are the two
26Who left our native country with the view
27Of seeking strange adventures, if we be
28The two who, madly and in misery,
29Have got so far as this, and if we still
30Are the same two who tumbled down this hill,
31Does it not plainly to a wrong amount,
32To put me in the pain and not in the account?

ROSAURA.

33I do not wish to impart,
34Clarin, to thee, the sorrows of my heart;
35Mourning for thee would spoil the consolation
36Of making for thyself thy lamentation;
37For there is such a pleasure in complaining,
38That a philosopher I've heard maintaining
39One ought to seek a sorrow and be vain of it,
40In order to be privileged to complain of it.

CLARIN.

41That same philosopher
42Was an old drunken fool, unless I err:
43Oh, that I could a thousand thumps present him,
44In order for complaining to content him!
45But what, my lady, say,
46Are we to do, on foot, alone, our way
47Lost in the shades of night?
48For see, the sun descends another sphere to light.

ROSAURA.

49So strange a misadventure who has seen?
50But if my sight deceives me not, between
51These rugged rocks, half-lit by the moon's ray
52And the declining day,
53It seems, or is it fancy? that I see
54A human dwelling?

CLARIN.

55So it seems to me,
56Unless my wish the longed-for lodging mocks.

ROSAURA.

57A rustic little palace 'mid the rocks
58Uplifts its lowly roof,
59Scarce seen by the far sun that shines aloof.
60Of such a rude device
61Is the whole structure of this edifice,
62That lying at the feet
63Of these gigantic crags that rise to greet
64The sun's first beams of gold,
65It seems a rock that down the mountain rolled.

CLARIN.

66Let us approach more near,
67For long enough we've looked at it from here;
68Then better we shall see
69If those who dwell therein will generously
70A welcome give us.

ROSAURA.

71See an open door
72(Funereal mouth 'twere best the name it bore),
73From which as from a womb
74The night is born, engendered in its gloom.

[The sound of chains is heard within.]

CLARIN.

75Heavens! what is this I hear?

ROSAURA.

76Half ice, half fire, I stand transfixed with fear.

CLARIN.

77A sound of chains, is it not?
78Some galley-slave his sentence here hath got;
79My fear may well suggest it so may be.

SCENE II.

SIGISMUND, [in the tower.] ROSAURA, CLARIN.

SIGISMUND.

80[within]. Alas! Ah, wretched me! Ah, wretched me!

ROSAURA.

81Oh what a mournful wail!
82Again my pains, again my fears prevail.

CLARIN.

83Again with fear I die.

ROSAURA.

84Clarin!

CLARIN.

85My lady!

ROSAURA.

86Let us turn and fly
87The risks of this enchanted tower.

CLARIN.

88For one,
89I scarce have strength to stand, much less to run.

ROSAURA.

90Is not that glimmer there afar —
91That dying exhalation — that pale star —
92A tiny taper, which, with trembling blaze
93Flickering 'twixt struggling flames and dying rays,
94With ineffectual spark
95Makes the dark dwelling place appear more dark?
96Yes, for its distant light,
97Reflected dimly, brings before my sight
98A dungeon's awful gloom,
99Say rather of a living corse, a living tomb;
100And to increase my terror and surprise,
101Drest in the skins of beasts a man there lies:
102A piteous sight,
103Chained, and his sole companion this poor light.
104Since then we cannot fly,
105Let us attentive to his words draw nigh,
106Whatever they may be.

[The doors of the tower open wide, and SIGISMUND is discovered in chains and clad in the skins of beasts. The light in the tower increases.]

SIGISMUND.

107Alas! Ah, wretched me! Ah, wretched me!
108Heaven, here lying all forlorn,
109I desire from thee to know,
110Since thou thus dost treat me so,
111Why have I provoked thy scorn
112By the crime of being born?—
113Though for being born I feel
114Heaven with me must harshly deal,
115Since man's greatest crime on earth
116Is the fatal fact of birth —
117Sin supreme without appeal.
118This alone I ponder o'er,
119My strange mystery to pierce through;
120Leaving wholly out of view
121Germs my hapless birthday bore,
122How have I offended more,
123That the more you punish me?
124Must not other creatures be
125Born? If born, what privilege
126Can they over me allege
127Of which I should not be free?
128Birds are born, the bird that sings,
129Richly robed by Nature's dower,
130Scarcely floats — a feathered flower,
131Or a bunch of blooms with wings —
132When to heaven's high halls it springs,
133Cuts the blue air fast and free,
134And no longer bound will be
135By the nest's secure control:—
136And with so much more of soul,
137Must I have less liberty?
138Beasts are born, the beast whose skin
139Dappled o'er with beauteous spots,
140As when the great pencil dots
141Heaven with stars, doth scarce begin
142From its impulses within—
143Nature's stern necessity,
144To be schooled in cruelty,—
145Monster, waging ruthless war:—
146And with instincts better far
147Must I have less liberty?
148Fish are born, the spawn that breeds
149Where the oozy sea-weeds float,
150Scarce perceives itself a boat,
151Scaled and plated for its needs,
152When from wave to wave it speeds,
153Measuring all the mighty sea,
154Testing its profundity
155To its depths so dark and chill:—
156And with so much freer will,
157Must I have less liberty?
158Streams are born, a coiled-up snake
159When its path the streamlet finds,
160Scarce a silver serpent winds
161'Mong the flowers it must forsake,
162But a song of praise doth wake,
163Mournful though its music be,
164To the plain that courteously
165Opes a path through which it flies:—
166And with life that never dies,
167Must I have less liberty?
168When I think of this I start,
169Aetna-like in wild unrest
170I would pluck from out my breast
171Bit by bit my burning heart:—
172For what law can so depart
173From all right, as to deny
174One lone man that liberty —
175That sweet gift which God bestows
176On the crystal stream that flows,
177Birds and fish that float or fly?

ROSAURA.

178Fear and deepest sympathy
179Do I feel at every word.

SIGISMUND.

180Who my sad lament has heard?
181What! Clotaldo!

CLARIN

182Say 'tis he.aparte

ROSAURA.

183No, 'tis but a wretch (ah, me!)
184Who in these dark caves and cold
185Hears the tale your lips unfold.

SIGISMUND.

186Then you'll die for listening so,
187That you may not know I know
188That you know the tale I told.

[Seizes her.]

189Yes, you'll die for loitering near:
190In these strong arms gaunt and grim
191I will tear you limb from limb.

CLARIN.

192I am deaf and couldn't hear:—
193No!

ROSAURA.

194If human heart you bear,
195'Tis enough that I prostrate me.
196At thy feet, to liberate me!

SIGISMUND.

197Strange thy voice can so unbend me,
198Strange thy sight can so suspend me,
199And respect so penetrate me!
200Who art thou? for though I see
201Little from this lonely room,
202This, my cradle and my tomb.
203Being all the world to me,
204And if birthday it could be,
205Since my birthday I have known
206But this desert wild and lone,
207Where throughout my life's sad course
208I have lived, a breathing corse,
209I have moved, a skeleton;
210And though I address or see
211Never but one man alone,
212Who my sorrows all hath known,
213And through whom have come to me
214Notions of earth, sky, and sea;
215And though harrowing thee again,
216Since thou'lt call me in this den,
217Monster fit for bestial feasts,
218I'm a man among wild beasts,
219And a wild beast amongst men.
220But though round me has been wrought
221All this woe, from beasts I've learned
222Polity, the same discerned
223Heeding what the birds had taught,
224And have measured in my thought
225The fair orbits of the spheres;
226You alone, 'midst doubts and fears,
227Wake my wonder and surprise —
228Give amazement to my eyes,
229Admiration to my ears.
230Every time your face I see
231You produce a new amaze:
232After the most steadfast gaze,
233I again would gazer be.
234I believe some hydropsy
235Must affect my sight, I think
236Death must hover on the brink
237Of those wells of light, your eyes,
238For I look with fresh surprise,
239And though death result, I drink.
240Let me see and die: forgive me;
241For I do not know, in faith,
242If to see you gives me death,
243What to see you not would give me;
244Something worse than death would grieve me,
245Anger, rage, corroding care,
246Death, but double death it were,
247Death with tenfold terrors rife,
248Since what gives the wretched life,
249Gives the happy death, despair!

ROSAURA.

250Thee to see wakes such dismay,
251Thee to hear I so admire,
252That I'm powerless to inquire,
253That I know not what to say:
254Only this, that I to-day,
255Guided by a wiser will,
256Have here come to cure my ill,
257Here consoled my grief to see,
258If a wretch consoled can be
259Seeing one more wretched still.
260Of a sage, who roamed dejected,
261Poor, and wretched, it is said,
262That one day, his wants being fed
263By the herbs which he collected,
264"Is there one" (he thus reflected)
265"Poorer than I am to-day?"
266Turning round him to survey,
267He his answer got, detecting
268A still poorer sage collecting
269Even the leaves he threw away.
270Thus complaining to excess,
271Mourning fate, my life I led,
272And when thoughtlessly I said
273To myself, "Does earth possess
274One more steeped in wretchedness?"
275I in thee the answer find.
276Since revolving in my mind,
277I perceive that all my pains
278To become thy joyful gains
279Thou hast gathered and entwined.
280And if haply some slight solace

SCENE IV.

SCENE V.

A HALL IN THE ROYAL PALACE.

492At thy starry eyes outshining,

630The bright marbles of Lysippus,
631Universally proclaim me
632Through earth's bounds the great Basilius.
633You already know the sciences
634That I feel my mind most given to
635Are the subtle mathematics,
636By whose means my clear prevision
637Takes from rumour its slow office,
638Takes from time its jurisdiction
639Of, each day, new facts disclosing;
640Since in algebraic symbols
641When the fate of future ages
642On my tablets I see written,
643I anticipate time in telling
644What my science hath predicted.
645All those circles of pure snow,
646All those canopies of crystal,
647Which the sun with rays illumines,
648Which the moon cuts in its circles,
649All those orbs of twinkling diamond,
650All those crystal globes that glisten,
651All that azure field of stars
652Where the zodiac signs are pictured,
653Are the study of my life,
654Are the books where heaven has written
655Upon diamond-dotted paper,
656Upon leaves by sapphires tinted,
657With light luminous lines of gold,
658In clear characters distinctly
659All the events of human life,
660Whether adverse or benignant.
661These so rapidly I read
662That I follow with the quickness
663Of my thoughts the swiftest movements
664Of their orbits and their circles.
665Would to heaven, that ere my mind
666To those mystic books addicted
667Was the comment of their margins
668And of all their leaves the index,
669Would to heaven, I say, my life
670Had been offered the first victim
671Of its anger, that my death-stroke
672Had in this way have been given me,
673Since the unhappy find even merit
674Is the fatal knife that kills them,
675And his own self-murderer
676Is the man whom knowledge injures!—
677I may say so, but my story
678So will say with more distinctness,
679And to win your admiration
680Once again I pray you listen.—
681Clorilene, my wife, a son
682Bore me, so by fate afflicted
683That on his unhappy birthday
684All Heaven's prodigies assisted.
685Nay, ere yet to life's sweet life
686Gave him forth her womb, that living
687Sepulchre (for death and life
688Have like ending and beginning),
689Many a time his mother saw
690In her dreams' delirious dimness
691From her side a monster break,
692Fashioned like a man, but sprinkled
693With her blood, who gave her death,
694By that human viper bitten.
695Round his birthday came at last,
696All its auguries fulfilling
697(For the presages of evil
698Seldom fail or even linger):
699Came with such a horoscope,
700That the sun rushed blood-red tinted
701Into a terrific combat
702With the dark moon that resisted;
703Earth its mighty lists outspread
704As with lessening lights diminished
705Strove the twin-lamps of the sky.
706'Twas of all the sun's eclipses
707The most dreadful that it suffered
708Since the hour its bloody visage
709Wept the awful death of Christ.
710For o'erwhelmed in glowing cinders
711The great orb appeared to suffer
712Nature's final paroxysm.
713Gloom the glowing noontide darkened,
714Earthquake shook the mightiest buildings,
715Stones the angry clouds rained down,
716And with blood ran red the rivers.
717In this frenzy of the sun,
718In its madness and delirium,
719Sigismund was born, thus early
720Giving proofs of his condition,
721Since his birth his mother slew,
722Just as if these words had killed her,
723"I am a man, since good with evil
724I repay here from the beginning,"—
725I, applying to my studies,
726Saw in them as 'twere forewritten
727This, that Sigismund would be
728The most cruel of all princes,
729Of all men the most audacious,
730Of all monarchs the most wicked;
731That his kingdom through his means
732Would be broken and partitioned,
733The academy of the vices,
734And the high school of sedition;
735And that he himself, borne onward
736By his crimes' wild course resistless,
737Would even place his feet on me;
738For I saw myself down-stricken,
739Lying on the ground before him
740(To say this what shame it gives me!)
741While his feet on my white hairs
742As a carpet were imprinted.
743Who discredits threatened ill,
744Specially an ill previsioned
745By one's study, when self-love
746Makes it his peculiar business?—
747Thus then crediting the fates
748Which far off my science witnessed,
749All these fatal auguries
750Seen though dimly in the distance,
751I resolved to chain the monster
752That unhappily life was given to,
753To find out if yet the stars
754Owned the wise man's weird dominion.
755It was publicly proclaimed
756That the sad ill-omened infant
757Was stillborn. I then a tower
758Caused by forethought to be builded
759'Mid the rocks of these wild mountains
760Where the sunlight scarce can gild it,
761Its glad entrance being barred
762By these rude shafts obeliscal.
763All the laws of which you know,
764All the edicts that prohibit
765Anyone on pain of death
766That secluded part to visit
767Of the mountain, were occasioned
768By this cause, so long well hidden.
769There still lives Prince Sigismund,
770Miserable, poor, in prison.
771Him alone Clotaldo sees,
772Only tends to and speaks with him;
773He the sciences has taught him,
774He the Catholic religion
775Has imparted to him, being
776Of his miseries the sole witness.
777Here there are three things: the first
778I rate highest, since my wishes
779Are, O Poland, thee to save
780From the oppression, the affliction
781Of a tyrant King, because
782Of his country and his kingdom
783He were no benignant father
784Who to such a risk could give it.
785Secondly, the thought occurs
786That to take from mine own issue
787The plain right that every law
788Human and divine hath given him
789Is not Christian charity;
790For by no law am I bidden
791To prevent another proving,
792Say, a tyrant, or a villain,
793To be one myself: supposing
794Even my son should be so guilty,
795That he should not crimes commit
796I myself should first commit them.
797Then the third and last point is,
798That perhaps I erred in giving
799Too implicit a belief
800To the facts foreseen so dimly;
801For although his inclination
802Well might find its precipices,
803He might possibly escape them:
804For the fate the most fastidious,
805For the impulse the most powerful.
806Even the planets most malicious
807Only make free will incline,
808But can force not human wishes.
809And thus 'twist these different causes
810Vacillating and unfixed,
811I a remedy have thought of
812Which will with new wonder fill you.
813I to-morrow morning purpose,
814Without letting it be hinted
815That he is my son, and therefore
816Your true King, at once to fix him
817As King Sigismund (for the name
818Still he bears that first was given him)
819'Neath my canopy, on my throne,
820And in fine in my position,
821There to govern and command you,
822Where in dutiful submission
823You will swear to him allegiance.
824My resources thus are triple,
825As the causes of disquiet
826Were which I revealed this instant.
827The first is; that he being prudent,
828Careful, cautious and benignant,
829Falsifying the wild actions
830That of him had been predicted,
831You'll enjoy your natural prince,
832He who has so long been living
833Holding court amid these mountains,
834With the wild beasts for his circle.
835Then my next resource is this:
836If he, daring, wild, and wicked,
837Proudly runs with loosened rein
838O'er the broad plain of the vicious,
839I will have fulfilled the duty
840Of my natural love and pity;
841Then his righteous deposition
842Will but prove my royal firmness,
843Chastisement and not revenge
844Leading him once more to the prison.
845My third course is this: the Prince
846Being what my words have pictured,
847From the love I owe you, vassals,
848I will give you other princes
849Worthier of the crown and sceptre;
850Namely, my two sisters' children,
851Who their separate pretensions
852Having happily commingled
853By the holy bonds of marriage,
854Will then fill their fit position.
855This is what a king commands you,
856This is what a father bids you,
857This is what a sage entreats you,
858This is what an old man wishes;
859And as Seneca, the Spaniard,
860Says, a king for all his riches
861Is but slave of his Republic,
862This is what a slave petitions.

ASTOLFO.

863If on me devolves the answer,
864As being in this weighty business
865The most interested party,
866I, of all, express the opinion:—
867Let Prince Sigismund appear;
868He's thy son, that's all-sufficient.

ALL.

869Give to us our natural prince,
870We proclaim him king this instant!

BASILIUS.

871Vassals, from my heart I thank you
872For this deference to my wishes:—
873Go, conduct to their apartments
874These two columns of my kingdom,
875On tomorrow you shall see him.

ALL.

876Live, long live great King Basilius!

[Exeunt all, accompanying ESTRELLA and ASTOLFO; The King remains.]
