Arden of Faversham

[SCENE I]

Enter ARDEN and FRANKLIN.

FRANKLIN.

1Arden, cheer up thy spirits, and droop no more.
2My gracious Lord, the Duke of Somerset,
3Hath freely given to thee and to thy heirs,
4By letters patents from his Majesty,
5All the lands of the Abbey of Feversham.
6Here are the deeds,
7Seal'd and subscrib'd with his name and the king's:
8Read them, and leave this melancholy mood.

ARDEN.

9Franklin, thy love prolongs my weary life;
10And but for thee how odious were this life,
11That shows me nothing but torments my soul,
12And those foul objects that offend mine eyes;
13Which makes me wish that for this veil of heaven
14The earth hung over my head and cover'd me.
15Love-letters pass 'twixt Mosbie and my wife,
16And they have privy meetings in the town:
17Nay, on his finger did I spy the ring
18Which at our marriage-day the priest put on.
19Can any grief be half so great as this?

FRANKLIN.

20Comfort thyself, sweet friend; it is not strange
21That women will be false and wavering.

ARDEN.

22Ay, but to dote on such a one as he
23Is monstrous, Franklin, and intolerable.

FRANKLIN.

24Why, what is he?

ARDEN.

25A botcher, and no better at the first;
26Who, by base brokage getting some small stock,
27Crept into service of a nobleman,
28And by his servile flattery and fawning
29Is now become the steward of his house,
30And bravely jets it in his silken gown.

FRANKLIN.

31No nobleman will countenance such a peasant.

ARDEN.

32Yes, the Lord Clifford, he that loves not me.
33But through his favour let not him grow proud;
34For were he by the Lord Protector back'd,
35He should not make me to be pointed at.
36I am by birth a gentleman of blood,
37And that injurious ribald, that attempts
38To violate my dear wife's chastity
39(For dear I hold her love, as dear as heaven)
40Shall on the bed which he thinks to defile
41See his dissever'd joints and sinews torn,
42Whilst on the planchers pants his weary body,
43Smear'd in the channels of his lustful blood.

FRANKLIN.

44Be patient, gentle friend, and learn of me
45To ease thy grief and save her chastity:
46Intreat her fair; sweet words are fittest engines
47To raze the flint walls of a woman's breast.
48In any case be not too jealious,
49Nor make no question of her love to thee;
50But, as securely, presently take horse,
51And lie with me at London all this term;
52For women, when they may, will not,
53But, being kept back, straight grow outrageous.

ARDEN.

54Though this abhors from reason, yet I'll try it,
55And call her forth, and presently take leave.
56How, Alice!

Here enters ALICE.

ALICE.

57Husband, what mean you to get up so early?
58Summer nights are short, and yet you rise ere day.
59Had I been wake, you had not risen so soon.

ARDEN.

60Sweet love, thou know'st that we two, Ovid-like,
61Have chid the morning when it 'gan to peep,
62And often wish'd that dark night's purblind steeds
63Would pull her by the purple mantle back,
64And cast her in the ocean to her love.
65But this night, sweet Alice, thou hast kill'd my heart:
66I heard thee call on Mosbie in thy sleep.

ALICE.

67'Tis like I was asleep when I nam'd him,
68For being awake he comes not in my thoughts.

ARDEN.

69Ay, but you started up, and suddenly,
70Instead of him, caught me about the neck.

ALICE.

71Instead of him? why, who was there but you?
72And where but one is, how can I mistake?

FRANKLIN.

73Arden, leave to urge her over-far.

ARDEN.

74Nay, love, there is no credit in a dream;
75Let it suffice I know thou lovest me well.

ALICE.

76Now I remember whereupon it came:
77Had we no talk of Mosbie yesternight?

FRANKLIN.

78Mistress Alice, I heard you name him once or twice.

ALICE.

79And thereof came it, and therefore blame not me.

ARDEN.

80I know it did, and therefore let it pass.
81I must to London, sweet Alice, presently.

ALICE.

82But tell me, do you mean to stay there long?

ARDEN.

83No longer there till my affairs be done.

FRANKLIN.

84He will not stay above a month at most.

ALICE.

85A month? ay me! Sweet Arden, come again
86Within a day or two, or else I die.

ARDEN.

87I cannot long be from thee, gentle Alice.
88Whilst Michael fetch our horses from the field,
89Franklin and I will down unto the quay;
90For I have certain goods there to unload.
91Meanwhile prepare our breakfast, gentle Alice;
92For yet ere noon we'll take horse and away.

Exeunt Arden and Franklin.

ALICE.

93Ere noon he means to take horse and away!
94Sweet news is this. O that some airy spirit
95Would in the shape and likeness of a horse
96Gallop with Arden 'cross the ocean,
97And throw him from his back into the waves!
98Sweet Mosbie is the man that hath my heart:
99And he usurps it, having nought but this,
100That I am tied to him by marriage.
101Love is a god, and marriage is but words;
102And therefore Mosbie's title is the best.
103Tush! whether it be or no, he shall be mine,
104In spite of him, of Hymen, and of rites.

Here enters ADAM of the Flower-de-luce.

105And here comes Adam of the Flower-de-luce;
106I hope he brings me tidings of my love.
107-How now, Adam, what is the news with you?
108Be not afraid; my husband is now from home.

ADAM.

109He whom you wot of, Mosbie, Mistress Alice,
110Is come to town, and sends you word by me
111In any case you may not visit him.

ALICE.

112Not visit him?

ADAM.

113No, nor take no knowledge of his being here.

ALICE.

114But tell me, is he angry or displeas'd?

ADAM.

115Should seem so, for he is wondrous sad.

ALICE.

116Were he as mad as raving Hercules,
117I'll see him, I; and were thy house of force,
118These hands of mine should raze it to the ground,
119Unless that thou wouldst bring me to my love.

ADAM.

120Nay, and you be so impatient, I'll be gone.

ALICE.

121Stay, Adam, stay; thou wert wont to be my friend.
122Ask Mosbie how I have incurr'd his wrath;
123Bear him from me these pair of silver dice,
124With which we play'd for kisses many a time,
125And when I lost, I won, and so did he;¬—-
126Such winning and such losing Jove send me!
127And bid him, if his love do not decline,
128Come this morning but along my door,
129And as a stranger but salute me there:
130This may he do without suspect or fear.

ADAM.

131Exit Adam. I'll tell him what you say, and so farewell.

ALICE.

132Do, and one day I'll make amends for all.—-
133I know he loves me well, but dares not come,
134Because my husband is so jealious,
135And these my narrow-prying neighbours blab,
136Hinder our meetings when we would confer.
137But, if I live, that block shall be remov'd,
138And, Mosbie, thou that comes to me by stealth,
139Shalt neither fear the biting speech of men,
140Nor Arden's looks; as surely shall he die
141As I abhor him and love only thee.

Here enters MICHAEL.

142How now, Michael, whither are you going?

MICHAEL:

143To fetch my master's nag.
144I hope you'll think on me.

ALICE.

145Ay, but, Michael, see you keep your oath,
146And be as secret as you are resolute.

MICHAEL.

147I'll see he shall not live above a week.

ALICE.

148On that condition, Michael, here is my hand:
149None shall have Mosbie's sister but thyself.

MICHAEL.

150I understand the painter here hard by
151Hath made report that he and Sue is sure.

ALICE.

152There's no such matter, Michael; believe it not.

MICHAEL.

153But he hath sent a dagger sticking in a heart,
154With a verse or two stolen from a painted cloth,
155The which I hear the wench keeps in her chest.
156Well, let her keep it! I shall find a fellow
157That can both write and read and make rhyme too.
158And if I do—-well, say no more:
159I'll send from London such a taunting letter
160As she shall eat the heart he sent with salt
161And fling the dagger at the painter's head.

ALICE.

162What needs all this? I say that Susan's thine.

MICHAEL.

163Why, then I say that I will kill my master,
164Or anything that you will have me do.

ALICE.

165But, Michael, see you do it cunningly.

MICHAEL.

166Why, say I should be took, I'll ne'er confess
167That you know anything; and Susan, being a maid,
168May beg me from the gallows of the shrieve.

ALICE.

169Trust not to that, Michael.

MICHAEL.

170You cannot tell me, I have seen it, I.
171But, mistress, tell her, whether I live or die,
172I'll make her more worth than twenty painters can;
173For I will rid mine elder brother away,
174And then the farm of Bolton is mine own.
175Who would not venture upon house and land,
176When he may have it for a right down blow?

Here enters MOSBIE.

ALICE.

177Yonder comes Mosbie. Michael, get thee gone,
178Exit Michael. And let not him nor any know thy drifts.
179Mosbie, my love!

MOSBIE.

180Away, I say, and talk not to me now.

ALICE.

181A word or two, sweet heart, and then I Will.
182'Tis yet but early days, thou needs not fear.

MOSBIE.

183Where is your husband?

ALICE.

184'Tis now high water, and he is at the quay.

MOSBIE.

185There let him be; henceforward know me not.

ALICE.

186Is this the end of all thy solemn oaths?
187Is this the fruit thy reconcilement buds?
188Have I for this given thee so many favours,
189Incurr'd my husband's hate, and, out alas!
190Made shipwreck of mine honour for thy sake?
191And dost thou say “henceforward know me not”?
192Remember, when I lock'd thee in my closet,
193What were thy words and mine; did we not both
194Decree to murder Arden in the night?
195The heavens can witness, and the world can tell,
196Before I saw that falsehood look of thine,
197'Fore I was tangled with thy 'ticing speech,
198Arden to me was dearer than my soul,—-
199And shall be still: base peasant, get thee gone,
200And boast not of thy conquest over me,
201Gotten by witchcraft and mere sorcery!
202For what hast thou to countenance my love,
203Being descended of a noble house,
204And match'd already with a gentleman
205Whose servant thou may'st be!—-and so farewell.

MOSBIE.

206Ungentle and unkind Alice, now I see
207That which I ever fear'd, and find too true:
208A woman's love is as the lightning-flame,
209Which even in bursting forth consumes itself.
210To try thy constancy have I been strange;
211Would I had never tried, but liv'd in hope!

ALICE.

212What need'st thou try me whom thou ne'er found false?

MOSBIE.

213Yet pardon me, for love is jealious.

ALICE.

214So lists the sailor to the mermaid's song,
215So looks the traveller to the basilisk:
216I am content for to be reconcil'd,
217And that, I know, will be mine overthrow.

MOSBIE.

218Thine overthrow? first let the world dissolve.

ALICE.

219Nay, Mosbie, let ine still enjoy thy love,
220And happen what will, I am resolute.
221My saving husband hoards up bags of gold
222To make our children rich, and now is he
223Gone to unload the goods that shall be thine,
224And he and Franklin will to London straight.

MOSBIE.

225To London, Alice? If thou'lt be rul'd by me,
226We'll make him sure enough for coming there.

ALICE.

227Ah, would we could!

MOSBIE.

228I happen'd on a painter yesternight,
229The only cunning man of Christendom;
230For he can temper poison with his oil,
231That whoso looks upon the work he draws
232Shall, with the beams that issue from his sight,
233Suck venom to his breast and slay himself.
234Sweet Alice, he shall draw thy counterfeit,
235That Arden may by gazing on it perish.

ALICE.

236Ay, but Mosbie, that is dangerous,
237For thou, or I, or any other else,
238Coming into the chamber where it hangs, may die.

MOSBIE.

239Ay, but we'll have it cover’d with a cloth
240And hung up in the study for himself.

ALICE.

241It may not be, for when the picture's drawn,
242Arden, I know, will come and show it me.

MOSBIE.

243Fear not; we'll have that shall serve the turn.
244This is the painter's house; I'll call him forth.

ALICE.

245But, Mosbie, I'll have no such picture, I.

MOSBIE.

246I pray thee leave it to my discretion.
247How, Clarke!

Here enters CLARKE.

248Oh, you are an honest man of your word! you serv'd me well.

CLARKE.

249Why, sir, I'll do it for you at any time,
250Provided, as you have given your word,
251I may have Susan Mosbie to my wife.
252For, as sharp-witted poets, whose Sweet verse
253Make heavenly gods break off their nectar draughts
254And lay their ears down to the lowly earth,
255Use humble promise to their sacred Muse,
256So we that are the poets‘ favourites
257Must have a love; ay, Love is the painter's muse,
258That makes him frame a speaking countenance,
259A weeping eye that witnesses heart's grief.
260Then tell me, Master Mosbie, shall I have her?

ALICE.

261'Tis pity but he should; he'll use her well.

MOSBIE.

262Clarke, here's my hand: my sister shall be thine.

CLARKE.

263Then, brother, to requite this courtesy,
264You shall command my life, my skill, and all.

ALICE.

265Ah, that thou couldst be secret.

MOSBIE.

266Fear him not; leave; I have talk'd sufficient.

CLARKE.

267You know not me that ask such questions.
268Let it suffice I know you love him well,
269And fain would have your husband made away:
270Wherein, trust me, you show a noble mind,
271That rather than you'll live with him you hate,
272You'll venture life, and die with him you love.
273The like will I do for my Susan's sake.

ALICE.

274Yet nothing could inforce me to the deed
275But Mosbie's love. Might I without control
276Enjoy thee still, then Arden should not die:
277But seeing I cannot, therefore let him die.

MOSBIE.

278Enough, sweet Alice; thy kind words makes me melt.
279Your trick of poison'd pictures we dislike;
280Some other poison would do better far.

ALICE.

281Ay, such as might be put into his broth,
282And yet in taste not to be found at all.

CLARKE.

283I know your mind, and here I have it for you.
284Put but a dram of this into his drink,
285Or any kind of broth that he shall eat,
286And he shall die within an hour after.

ALICE.

287As I am a gentlewoman, Clarke, next day
288Thou and Susan shall be marrièd.

MOSBIE.

289And I'll make her dowty more than I'll talk of, Clarke.

CLARKE.

290Yonder's your husband. Mosbie, I'll be gone.

Here enters ARDEN and FRANKLIN.

ALICE.

291In good time see where my husband comes.
292Exit Clarke. Master Mosbie, ask him the question yourself.

MOSBIE.

293Master Arden, being at London yesternight,
294The Abbey lands whereof you are now possess'd
295Were offer'd me on some occasion
296By Greene, one of Sir Antony Ager's men:
297I pray you, sir, tell me, are not the lands yours?
298Hath any other interest herein?

ARDEN.

299Mosbie, that question we'll decide anon.
300Alice, make ready my breakfast, I must hence.

Exit Alice.

301As for the lands, Mosbie, they are mine
302By letters patents from his Majesty.
303But I must have a mandate for my wife;
304They say you seek to rob me of her love:
305Villain, what makes thou in her company?
306She's no companion for so base a groom.

MOSBIE.

307Arden, I thought not on her, I carne to thee;
308But rather than I pocket up this wrong—-¬

FRANKLIN.

309What will you do, sir?

MOSBIE.

310Revenge it on the proudest of you both.

Then Arden draws forth Mosbie' s sword.

ARDEN.

311So, sirrah; you may not wear a sword,
312The statute makes against artificers;
313I warrant that I do. Now use your bodkin,
314Your Spanish needle, and your pressing iron,
315For this shall go with me; and mark my words,
316You, goodman botcher, 'tis to you I speak:
317The next time that I take thee near my house,
318Instead of legs I'll make thee crawl on stumps.

MOSBIE.

319Ah, Master Arden, you have injur'd me:
320I do appeal to God and to the world.

FRANKLIN.

321Why, canst thou deny thou wert a botcher once?

MOSBIE.

322Measure me what I am, not what I was.

ARDEN.

323Why, what art thou now but a velvet drudge,
324A cheating steward, and base-minded peasant?

MOSBIE.

325Arden, now thou hast belch'd and vomited
326The rancorous venom of thy mis-swoll'n heart,
327Hear me but speak: as I intend to live
328With God and his elected saints in heaven,
329I never meant more to solicit her;
330And that she knows, and all the world shall see.
331I lov'd her once;—-sweet Arden, pardon me,
332I could not choose, her beauty fir'd my heart!
333But time hath quench'd these over-raging coals;
334And, Arden, though I now frequent thy house,
335'Tis for my sister's sake, her waiting-maid,
336And not for hers. Mayest thou enjoy her long:
337Hell-fire and wrathful vengeance light on me,
338If I dishonour her or injure thee.

ARDEN.

339Mosbie, with these thy protestations
340The deadly hatred of my heart's appeased,
341And thou and I'll be friends, if this prove true.
342As for the base terms I gave thee late,
343Forget them, Mosbie: I had cause to speak,
344When all the knights and gentlemen of Kent
345Make common table-talk of her and thee.

MOSBIE.

346Who lives that is not touch'd with slanderous tongues?

FRANKLIN.

347Then, Mosbie, to eschew the speech of men,
348Upon whose general bruit all honour hangs,
349Forbear his house.

ARDEN.

350Forbear it! nay, rather frequent it more:
351The world shall see that I distrust her not.
352To warn him on the sudden from my house
353Were to confirm the rumour that is grown.

MOSBIE.

354By my faith, sir, you say true,
355And therefore will I sojourn here a while,
356Until our enemies have talk'd their fill;
357And then, I hope, they'll cease, and at last confess
358How causeless they have injur'd her and me.

ARDEN.

359And I will lie at London all this term
360To let them see how light I weigh their words.

Here enters ALICE.

ALICE.

361Husband, sit down; your breakfast will be cold.

ARDEN.

362Come, Master Mosbie, will you sit with us?

MOSBIE.

363I cannot eat, but I'll sit for company.

ARDEN.

364Sirrah Michael, see our horse be ready.

ALICE.

365Husband, why pause ye? why eat you not?

ARDEN.

366I am not well; there's something in this broth
367That is not wholesome: didst thou make it, Alice?

ALICE.

368I did, and that's the cause it likes not you.

Then she throws down the broth on the ground.

369There's nothing that I do can please your taste;
370You were best to say I would have poison'd you.
371I cannot speak or cast aside my eye,
372But he imagines I have stepp'd awry.
373Here's he that you cast in my teeth so oft:
374Now will I be convinc'd or purge myself.
375I charge thee speak to this mistrustful man,
376Thou that wouldst see me hang, thou, Mosbie, thou:
377What favour hast thou had more than a kiss
378At coming or departing from the town?

MOSBIE.

379You wrong yourself and me to cast these doubts:
380Your loving husband is not jealious.

ARDEN.

381Why, gentle Mistress Alice,
382Cannot I be ill but you'll accuse yourself?
383Franklin, thou hast a box of mithridate;
384I'll take a little to prevent the worst.

FRANKLIN.

385Do so, and let us presently take horse;
386My life for yours, ye shall do well enough.

ALICE.

387Give me a spoon, I'll eat of it myself.
388Would it were full of poison to the brim!
389Then should my cares and troubles have an end.
390Was ever silly woman so tormented?

ARDEN.

391Be patient, sweet love; I mistrust not thee.

ALICE.

392God will revenge it, Arden, if thou dost;
393For never woman lov'd her husband better
394Than I do thee.

ARDEN.

395I know it, sweet Alice; cease to complain,
396Lest that in tears I answer thee again.

FRANKLIN.

397Come, leave this dallying, and let us away.

ALICE.

398Forbear to wound me with that bitter word;
399Arden shall go to London in my arms.

ARDEN.

400Loth am I to depart, yet I must go.

ALICE.

401Wilt thou to London, then, and leave me here?
402Ah, if thou love me, gentle Arden, stay.
403Yet, if thy business be of great import,
404Go, if thou wilt, I'll bear it as I may;
405But write from London to me every week,
406Nay, every day, and stay no longer there
407Than thou must needs, lest that I die for sorrow.

ARDEN.

408I'll write unto thee every other tide,
409And so farewell, sweet Alice, till we meet next.

ALICE.

410Farewell, husband, seeing you'll have it so;
411And, Master Franklin, seeing you take him hence,
412In hope you'll hasten him home, I'll give you this.

And then she kisseth him.

FRANKLIN.

413And if he stay, the fault shall not be mine.
414Mosbie, farewell, and see you keep your oath.

MOSBIE.

415I hope he is not jealous of me now.

ARDEN.

416No, Mosbie, no; hereafter think of me
417As of your dearest friend, and so farewell.

Exeunt Arden, Franklin, and Michael.

ALICE.

418I am glad he is gone; he was about to stay,
419But did you mark me then how I brake off?

MOSBIE.

420Ay, Alice, and it was cunningly perform'd.
421But what a villain is this painter Clarke!

ALICE.

422Was it not a goodly poison that he gave?
423Why, he's as well now as he was before.
424It should have been some fine confection
425That might have given the broth some dainty taste:
426This powder was too gross and populous.

MOSBIE.

427But had he eaten but three spoonfuls more,
428Then had he died, and our love continu'd.

ALICE.

429Why, so it shall, Mosbie, albeit he live.

MOSBIE.

430It is unpossible, for I have sworn
431Never hereafter to solicit thee,
432Or, whilst he lives, once more importune thee.

ALICE.

433Thou shalt not need, I will importune thee.
434What, shall an oath make thee forsake my love?
435As if I have not sworn as much myself
436And given my hand unto him in the church!
437Tush, Mosbie, oaths are words, and words is wind,
438And wind is mutable: then, I conclude,
439'Tis childishness to stand upon an oath.

MOSBIE.

440Well provèd, Mistress Alice; yet by your leave
441I'll keep mine unbroken whilst he lives.

ALICE.

442Ay, do, and spare not, his time is but short;
443For if thou beest as resolute as I,
444We'l1 have him murder'd as he walks the streets.
445In London many alehouse ruffians keep,
446Which, as I hear, will murder men for gold.
447They shall be soundly fee'd to pay him home.

Here enters GREENE.

MOSBIE.

448Alice, whaes he that comes yonder? knowest thou him?

ALICE.

449Mosbie, be gone: I hope 'tis one that comes
450Exit Mosbie. To put in practice our intended drifts.

GREENE.

451Mistress Arden, you are well met.
452I am sorry that your husband is from home,
453Whenas my purpos'd journey was to him;
454Yet all my labour is not spent in vain,
455For I suppose that you can full discourse
456And flat resolve me of the thing I seek.

ALICE.

457What is it, Master Greene? If that I may
458Or can with safety, I will answer you.

GREENE.

459I heard your husband hath the grant of late,
460Confirm'd by letters patents from the king,
461Of all the lands of the Abbey of Feversham,
462Generally intitl'd, so that all former grants
463Are cut off; whereof I myself had one;
464But now my interest by that is void.
465This is all, Mistress Arden; is it true or no?

ALICE.

466True, Master Greene; the lands are his in state,
467And whatsoever leases were before
468Are void for term of Master Arden's life;
469He hath the grant under the Chancery seal.

GREENE.

470Pardon me, Mistress Arden, I must speak,
471For I am touch'd. Your husband doth me wrong
472To wring me from the little land I have.
473My living is my life, only that [land]
474Resteth remainder of my portion.
475Desire of wealth is endless in his mind,
476And he is greedy-gaping still for gain;
477Nor cares he though young gentlemen do beg,
478So he may scrape and hoard up in his pouch.
479But, seeing he hath taken my lands, I'll value life
480As careless as he is careful for to get:
481And tell him this from me, I'll be reveng'd,
482And so as he shall wish the Abbey lands
483Had rested still within their former state.

ALICE.

484Alas, poor gentleman, I pity you,
485And woe is me that any man should want!
486God knows 'tis not my fault; but wonder not
487Though he be hard to others, when to me¬
488Ah, Master Greene, God knows how I am us'd.

GREENE.

489Why, Mistress Arden, can the crabbèd churl
490Use you unkindly? respects he not your birth,
491Your honourable friends, nor what you brought?
492Why, all Kent knows your parentage and what you are.

ALICE.

493Ah, Master Greene, be it spoken in secret here,
494I never live good day with him alone:
495When he is at home, then have I froward looks,
496Hard words and blows to mend the match withal;
497And though I might content as good a man,
498Yet doth he keep in every comer trulls;
499And [being] weary with his trugs at home,
500Then rides he straight to London; there, forsooth,
501He revels it among such filthy ones
502As counsels him to make away his wife.
503Thus live I daily in continual fear,
504In sorrow, so despairing of redress
505As every day I wish with hearty prayer
506That he or I were taken forth the world.

GREENE.

507Now trust me, Mistress Alice, it grieveth me
508So fair a creature should be so abus'd.
509Why, who would have thought the civil sir so sullen?
510He looks so smoothly. Now, fie upon him, churl!
