Ben Jonson

Bartholomew Fair

← Act IV Epilogue →

5.1

[Enter] LANTERN [LEATHERHEAD, finely dressed], FILCHER, [and] SHARKWELL [with a banner and a drum. They make ready their puppet theatre.]

LANTERN

1Well, luck and Saint Barthol’mew! Out with the sign of our invention, in the name of wit, and do you beat the drum the while! All the foul i’the Fair, I mean all the dirt in Smithfield — that’s one of Master Littlewit’s carwitchets now — will be thrown at our banner today, if the matter does not please the people. Oh, the motions, that I Lantern Leatherhead have given light to i’my time, since my master Pod died! Jerusalem Pod was a master of motions was a stately thing; and so was Nineveh , and before him. The City of Norwich , and Sodom and Gomorrah, with the rising o’the prentices and pulling down the bawdy-houses there upon Shrove Tuesday. But The Gunpowder Plot : there was a get-penny! I have presented that to an eighteen- or twenty-pence audience nine times in an afternoon. Your home-born projects prove ever the best, they are so easy and familiar. They put too much learning i’their things nowadays, and that, I fear, will be the spoil o’this. Littlewit? I say, Micklewit! If not too mickle! — Look to your gathering there, Goodman Filcher.

FILCHER

2I warrant you, sir.

LANTERN

3An there come any gentlefolks, take twopence apiece, Sharkwell.

SHARKWELL

4I warrant you, sir — threepence, an we can.

[Exeunt into the booth.]

5.2

The JUSTICE comes in like a porter.

JUSTICE

1This later disguise I have borrowed of a porter shall carry me out to all my great and good ends, which, however interrupted, were never destroyed in me. Neither is the hour of my severity yet come to reveal myself, wherein cloudlike I will break out in rain and hail, lightning and thunder, upon the head of enormity. Two main works I have to prosecute first: one is to invent some satisfaction for the poor, kind wretch who is out of his wits for my sake, and yonder I see him coming. I will walk aside, and project for it.

[He steps aside.]

[Enter] WINWIFE [and] GRACE.

WINWIFE

2I wonder where Tom Quarlous is, that he returns not. It may be he is struck in here to seek us.

GRACE

3See, here’s our madman again.

[Enter separately] QUARLOUS [and Mistress] PURECRAFT.

Quarlous in the habit of the madman is mistaken by Mistress Purecraft.

QUARLOUS

4[Aside] I have made myself as like him as his gown and cap will give me leave.

PURECRAFT

5[To Quarlous] Sir, I love you, and would be glad to be mad with you in truth.

WINWIFE

6[Aside] How! My widow in love with a madman?

PURECRAFT

7Verily, I can be as mad in spirit as you.

QUARLOUS

8By whose warrant? Leave your canting. [To Grace] Gentlewoman, have I found you? — save ye, quit ye, and multiply ye. (He desires to see the book of Mistress Grace.) Where’s your book? ’Twas a sufficient name I marked, let me see ’t, be not afraid to show ’t me.

GRACE

9What would you with it, sir?

QUARLOUS

10Mark it again, and again, at your service.

GRACE

11Here it is, sir: this was it you marked.

QUARLOUS

12‘Palamon’! Fare you well, fare you well.

WINWIFE

13How, Palamon!

GRACE

14Yes, faith, he has discovered it to you now, and therefore ’twere vain to disguise it longer. I am yours, sir, by the benefit of your fortune.

WINWIFE

15And you have him, mistress, believe it, that shall never give you cause to repent her benefit, but make you rather to think that in this choice she had both her eyes.

GRACE

16I desire to put it to no danger of protestation.

[Exeunt Grace and Winwife.]

QUARLOUS

17[Aside] ‘Palamon’ the word, and Winwife the man!

PURECRAFT

18[To Quarlous] Good sir, vouchsafe a yoke-fellow in your madness, shun not one of the sanctified sisters that would draw with you in truth.

QUARLOUS

19Away, you are a herd of hypocritical, proud ignorants, rather wild than mad, fitter for woods and the society of beasts than houses and the congregation of men. You are the second part of the society of canters, outlaws to order and discipline and the only privileged church-robbers of Christendom. Let me alone.
[Aside] ‘Palamon’ the word, and Winwife the man!

PURECRAFT

20[Aside] I must uncover myself unto him or I shall never enjoy him, for all the cunning men’s promises. — Good sir, hear me: I am worth six thousand pound; my love to you is become my rack. I’ll tell you all, and the truth, since you hate the hypocrisy of the parti-coloured Brotherhood. These seven years I have been a wilful holy widow only to draw feasts and gifts from my entangled suitors; I am also by office an assisting Sister of the Deacons, and a devourer, instead of a distributer, of the alms. I am a special maker of marriages for our decayed Brethren with our rich widows, for a third part of their wealth when they are married, for the relief of the poor elect: as also our poor handsome young virgins with our wealthy bachelors or widowers, to make them steal from their husbands, when I have confirmed them in the faith and got all put into their custodies. And if I ha’ not my bargain, they may sooner turn a scolding drab into a silent minister than make me leave pronouncing reprobation and damnation unto them. Our elder, Zeal-of-the-land, would have had me, but I know him to be the capital knave of the land, making himself rich by being made feoffee in trust to deceased Brethren, and cozening their heirs by swearing the absolute gift of their inheritance. And thus having eased my conscience and uttered my heart with the tongue of my love, enjoy all my deceits together, I beseech you! I should not have revealed this to you, but that in time I think you are mad, and I hope you’ll think me so, too, sir!

QUARLOUS

21Stand aside, I’ll answer you presently. (He considers with himself of it.) Why should not I marry this six thousand pound, now I think on’t? And a good trade too, that she has beside, ha? The t’other wench, Winwife is sure of; there’s no expectation for me there! Here I may make myself some saver yet, if she continue mad — there’s the question. It is money that I want: why should I not marry the money, when ’tis offered me? I have a licence and all: it is but razing out one name and putting in another. There’s no playing with a man’s fortune! I am resolved! I were truly mad, an I would not!
(He takes her along with him.) Well, come your ways, follow me; an you will be mad, I’ll show you a warrant!

PURECRAFT

22Most zealously, it is that I zealously desire.

The Justice calls him [Aside].

JUSTICE

23Sir, let me speak with you.

QUARLOUS

24By whose warrant?

JUSTICE

25The warrant that you tender and respect so, Justice Overdo’s! I am the man, friend Troubleall, though thus disguised (as the careful magistrate ought) for the good of the republic in the Fair, and the weeding out of enormity. Do you want a house or meat, or drink, or clothes? Speak whatsoever it is, it shall be supplied you. What want you?

QUARLOUS

26Nothing but your warrant.

JUSTICE

27My warrant? For what?

QUARLOUS

28To be gone, sir.

JUSTICE

29Nay, I pray thee, stay. I am serious, and have not many words, nor much time to exchange with thee. Think what may do thee good.

QUARLOUS

30Your hand and seal will do me a great deal of good — nothing else in the whole Fair that I know.

JUSTICE

31If it were to any end, thou shouldst have it willingly.

QUARLOUS

32Why, it will satisfy me — that’s end enough — to look on. An you will not gi’ it me, let me go.

JUSTICE

33Alas! Thou shalt ha’ it presently; I’ll but step into the scrivener’s hereby, and bring it. Do not go away.

The Justice goes out.

QUARLOUS

34[Aside] Why, this madman’s shape will prove a very fortunate one, I think! Can a ragged robe produce these effects? If this be the wise Justice, and he bring me his hand, I shall go near to make some use on’t. He is come already!

And [the JUSTICE] returns.

JUSTICE

35Look thee! Here is my hand and seal, ‘Adam Overdo’. If there be anything to be written above in the paper that thou want’st now or at any time hereafter, think on’t. It is my deed: I deliver it so. Can your friend write?

QUARLOUS

36Her hand for a witness, and all is well.

JUSTICE

37With all my heart.

He urgeth Mistress Purecraft [and she signs the deed].

QUARLOUS

38[Aside] Why should not I ha’ the conscience to make this a bond of a thousand pound now, or what I would else?

JUSTICE

39[Handing over the document] Look you, there it is; and I deliver it as my deed again.

QUARLOUS

40[To Mistress Purecraft] Let us now proceed in madness.

He takes her in with him.

JUSTICE

41Well, my conscience is much eased; I ha’ done my part. Though it doth him no good, yet Adam hath offered satisfaction! The sting is removed from hence. Poor man, he is much altered with his affliction, it has brought himlow! Now for my other work, reducing the young man I have followed so long in love, from the brink of his bane to the centre of safety. Here, or in some such like vain place, I shall be sure to find him. I will wait the good time.

[He steps to one side.]

5.3

[Enter] SHARKWELL, FILCHER [at the entrance to the puppet theatre; then enter] COKES. The BOYS o’the Fair follow him.

COKES

1How now? What’s here to do? Friend, art thou the master of the monuments?

SHARKWELL

2’Tis a motion, an’t please your worship.

JUSTICE

3[Aside] My fantastical brother-in-law, Master Barthol’mew Cokes!

COKES

4A motion, what’s that? (He reads the bill.) ‘The Ancient Modern History of Hero and Leander , otherwise called The Touchstone of True Love , with as true a trial of friendship between Damon and Pythias, two faithful friends o’the Bankside.’ Pretty, i’faith — what’s the meaning on’t? Is’t an interlude? Or what is’t?

FILCHER

5Yes, sir. Please you come near; we’ll take your money within.

COKES

6Back with these children; they do so follow me up and down.

[Enter] JOHN.

JOHN

7[To Filcher] By your leave, friend.

FILCHER

8You must pay, sir, an you go in.

JOHN

9Who, I? I perceive thou know’st not me. Call the master o’the motion.

SHARKWELL

10What, do you not know the author, fellow Filcher? You must take no money of him; he must come in gratis. Master Littlewit is a voluntary: he is the author.

JOHN

11Peace, speak not too loud: I would not have any notice taken that I am the author till we see how it passes.

COKES

12Master Littlewit, how dost thou?

JOHN

13Master Cokes! You are exceeding well met. What, in your doublet and hose, without a cloak or a hat?

COKES

14I would I might never stir, as I am an honest man, and by that fire: I have lost all i’the Fair, and all my acquaintance too. Did’st thou meet anybody that I know, Master Littlewit? My man Numps, or my sister Overdo, or Mistress Grace? Pray thee, Master Littlewit, lend me some money to see the interlude here. I’ll pay thee again, as I am a gentleman. If thou’lt but carry me home, I have money enough there.

JOHN

15Oh, sir, you shall command it. What, will a crown serve you?

COKES

16I think it will. What do we pay for coming in, fellows?

FILCHER

17Twopence, sir.

COKES

18Twopence? There’s twelvepence, friend. Nay, I am a gallant, as simple as I look now, if you see me with my man about me, and my artillery again.

JOHN

19Your man was i’the stocks e’en now, sir.

COKES

20Who, Numps?

JOHN

21Yes, faith.

COKES

22For what, i’faith? I am glad o’that — remember to tell me on’t anon; I have enough now! What manner of matter is this, Master Littlewit? What kind of actors ha’ you? Are they good actors?

JOHN

23Pretty youths, sir: all children, both old and young. Here’s the master of ’em —

[Enter] LANTERN.

LANTERN

24(Leatherhead whispers to Littlewit) Call me not Leatherhead, but Lantern.

JOHN

25— Master Lantern, that gives light to the business.

COKES

26In good time, sir, I would fain see ’em; I would be glad to drink with the young company. Which is the tiring house? LANTERN Troth, sir, our tiring house is somewhat little: we are but beginners yet; pray, pardon us. You cannot go upright in’t.

COKES

27No? Not now my hat is off? What would you have done with me, if you had had me feather and all, as I was once today? Ha’ you none of your pretty, impudent boys now, to bring stools, fill tobacco, fetch ale, and beg money, as they have at other houses? Let me see some o’your actors.

JOHN

28Show him ’em, show him ’em, Master Lantern: this is a gentleman that is a favourer of the quality.

[Lantern goes into the booth.]

JUSTICE

29[Aside] Ay, the favouring of this licentious quality is the consumption of many a young gentleman — a pernicious enormity.

He brings them out in a basket.

COKES

30What, do they live in baskets?

LANTERN

31They do lie in a basket, sir; they are o’the small players.

COKES

32These be ‘players minors’, indeed. Do you call these players?

LANTERN

33They are actors, sir, and as good as any, none dispraised, for dumbshows — indeed, I am the mouth of ’em all!

COKES

34Thy mouth will hold ’em all. I think one tailor would go near to beat all this company with a hand bound behind him.

JOHN

35Ay, and eat ’em all, too, an they were in cake-bread.

COKES

36I thank you for that, Master Littlewit, a good jest! Which is your Burbage now?

LANTERN

37What mean you by that, sir?

COKES

38Your best actor: your Field?

JOHN

39Good, i’faith! you are even with me, sir.

LANTERN

40This is he that acts young Leander, sir. He is extremely beloved of the womenkind: they do so affect his action, the green gamesters that come here. And this is lovely Hero; this with the beard, Damon; and this, pretty Pythias; this is the ghost of King Dionysius in the habit of a scrivener, as you shall see anon, at large.

COKES

41Well, they are a civil company; I like ’em for that. They offer not to fleer nor jeer nor break jests, as the great players do. And then, there goes not so much charge to the feasting of ’em, or making ’em drunk, as to the other, by reason of their littleness. Do they use to play perfect? Are they never flustered?

LANTERN

42No, sir. I thank my industry and policy for it; they are as well governed a company, though I say it — And here is young Leander: is as proper an actor of his inches, and shakes his head like an ostler.

COKES

43But do you play it according to the printed book? I have read that.

LANTERN

44By no means, sir.

COKES

45No? How then?

LANTERN

46A better way, sir — that is too learned and poetical for our audience: what do they know what Hellespont is, ‘guilty of true love’s blood’? Or what Abydos is, or ‘the other, Sestos hight’?

COKES

47Thou’rt i’the right: I do not know myself.

LANTERN

48No, I have entreated Master Littlewit to take a little pains to reduce it to a more familiar strain for our people.

COKES

49How, I pray thee, good Master Littlewit?

JOHN

50It pleases him to make a matter of it, sir. But there is no such matter, I assure you. I have only made it a little easy and modern for the times, sir, that’s all: as, for the Hellespont I imagine our Thames here; and then Leander, I make a dyer’s son about Puddle Wharf and Hero a wench o’the Bankside, who, going over one morning to Old Fish Street, Leander spies her land at Trig Stairs, and falls in love with her. Now do I introduce Cupid, having metamorphosed himself into a drawer, and he strikes Hero in love, with a pint of sherry — and other pretty passages there are o’the friendship that will delight you, sir, and please you of judgement.

COKES

51I’ll be sworn they shall: I am in love with the actors already, and I’ll be allied to them presently — they respect gentlemen, these fellows. Hero shall be my fairing: but which of my fairings? — le’ me see — i’faith, my fiddle! And Leander my fiddlestick. Then Damon my drum, and Pythias my pipe, and the ghost of Dionysius my hobby-horse. All fitted.

5.4

[Enter] to them WINWIFE [and] GRACE.

WINWIFE

1Look, yonder’s your Cokes gotten in among his playfellows; I thought we could not miss him at such a spectacle.

GRACE

2Let him alone. He is so busy, he will never spy us. Cokes is handling the puppets. LANTERN Nay, good sir.

COKES

3I warrant thee, I will not hurt her, fellow. What, dost think me uncivil? I pray thee, be not jealous: I am toward a wife.

JOHN

4Well, good Master Lantern, make ready to begin, that I may fetch my wife; and look you be perfect: you undo me else i’my reputation.

LANTERN

5I warrant you, sir, do not you breed too great an expectation of it among your friends: that’s the only hurter of these things.

JOHN

6No, no, no.

[Exit.]

COKES

7I’ll stay here, and see; pray thee, let me see.

WINWIFE

8How diligent and troublesome he is!

GRACE

9The place becomes him, methinks.

JUSTICE

10[Aside] My ward, Mistress Grace, in the company of a stranger! I doubt I shall be compelled to discover myself before my time!

[Enter] KNOCKEM, WHIT, [and] EDGWORTH [with] Mistress OVERDO [and] WIN. [The women are masked, and showily dressed in green gowns.]

The Doorkeepers speak.

FILCHER

11Twopence apiece, gentlemen: an excellent motion!

KNOCKEM

12Shall we have fine fireworks and good vapours?

SHARKWELL

13Yes, captain, and waterworks, too.

WHIT

14I pree dee, take a care o’ dy shmall lady there, Edgworth; I will look to dish tall lady myself.

LANTERN

15Welcome, gentlemen, welcome, gentlemen!

WHIT

16Predee, mashter o’ de monshtersh, help a very sick lady here to a chair to shit in.

LANTERN

17Presently, sir.

[The Doorkeepers] bring Mistress Overdo a chair. [She quickly falls asleep.]

WHIT

18Good fait now, Urs’la’s ale and aqua-vitae ish to blame for’t; shit down, shweetheart, shit down, and shleep a little.

EDGWORTH

19[To Win] Madam, you are very welcome hither.

KNOCKEM

20Yes, and you shall see very good vapours.

JUSTICE

21[Aside] Here is my care come!
(By Edgworth.) I like to see him in so good company; and yet I wonder that persons of such fashion should resort hither!

The Cutpurse courts Mistress Littlewit.

EDGWORTH

22This is a very private house, madam.

LANTERN

23Will it please your ladyship sit, madam?

WIN

24Yes, goodman.
[Aside] They do so all-to-be-madam me, I think they think me a very lady!

EDGWORTH

25What else, madam?

WIN

26Must I put off my mask to him?

EDGWORTH

27Oh, by no means.

WIN

28How should my husband know me, then?

KNOCKEM

29Husband? An idle vapour: he must not know you, nor you him — there’s the true vapour.

JUSTICE

30[Aside] Yea, I will observe more of this.
[To Whit] Is this a lady, friend?

WHIT

31Ay, and dat is anoder lady, shweetheart; if dou hasht a mind to ’em, give me twelvepence from tee, and dou shalt have eder-oder on ’em!

JUSTICE

32Ay!
[Aside] This will prove my chiefest enormity: I will follow this.

EDGWORTH

33Is not this a finer life, lady, than to be clogged with a husband?

WIN

34Yes, a great deal. When will they begin, trow, in the name o’the motion?

EDGWORTH

35By and by, madam: they stay but for company.

KNOCKEM

36Do you hear, puppet-master, these are tedious vapours! When begin you?

LANTERN

37We stay but for Master Littlewit, the author, who is gone for his wife; and we begin presently.

WIN

38[Aside to Edgworth] That’s I, that’s I.

EDGWORTH

39That was you, lady, but now you are no such poor thing.

KNOCKEM

40Hang the author’s wife, a running vapour! Here be ladies will stay for ne’er a Delia o’ ’em all.

WHIT

41But hear me now, here ish one o’ de ladish ashleep. Stay till she but vake, man.

[Enter] to them WASP.

WASP

42How now, friends? What’s here to do?

The Doorkeepers again.

FILCHER

43Twopence apiece, sir: the best motion in the Fair.

WASP

44I believe you lie. If you do, I’ll have my money again, and beat you.

WINWIFE

45Numps is come!

WASP

46Did you see a master of mine come in here: a tall young squire of Harrow o’the Hill, Master Barthol’mew Cokes?

FILCHER

47I think there be such a one within.

WASP

48Look he be: you were best — but it is very likely. I wonder I found him not at all the rest. I ha’ been at the eagle, and the black wolf, and the bull with the five legs and two pizzles — he was a calf at Uxbridge Fair two years agone — and at the dogs that dance the morris, and the hare o’the taber, and missed him at all these! Sure this must needs be some fine sight that holds him so, if it have him.

COKES

49Come, come, are you ready now?

LANTERN

50Presently, sir.

WASP

51[Aside] Hoyday, he’s at work in his doublet and hose. [To Cokes] Do you hear, sir? Are you employed, that you are bare-headed and so busy?

COKES

52Hold your peace, Numps: you ha’ been i’the stocks, I hear.

WASP

53[To himself] Does he know that? Nay, then the date of my authority is out; I must think no longer to reign; my government is at an end. He that will correct another must want fault in himself.

WINWIFE

54[Overhearing] Sententious Numps! I never heard so much from him before.

LANTERN

55Sure, Master Littlewit will not come. Please you take your place, sir; we’ll begin.

COKES

56I pray thee, do: mine ears long to be at it, and my eyes, too. — Oh, Numps, i’the stocks, Numps? Where’s your sword, Numps?

WASP

57I pray you, intend your game, sir; let me alone.

COKES

58Well then, we are quit for all. Come, sit down, Numps; I’ll interpret to thee. Did you see Mistress Grace? — it’s no matter neither, now I think on’t; tell me anon.

WINWIFE

59[To Grace] A great deal of love and care he expresses.

GRACE

60Alas! Would you have him to express more than he has? That were tyranny.

COKES

61Peace, ho; now, now.

LANTERN

62Gentles, that no longer your expectations may wander,
Behold our chief actor, amorous Leander,
With a great deal of cloth lapped about him like a scarf,
For he yet serves his father, a dyer at Puddle Wharf,
Which place we’ll make bold with to call it our Abydos,
As the Bankside is our Sestos, and let it not be denied us.
Now, as he is beating to make the dye take the fuller,
Who chances to come by but fair Hero in a sculler?
And, seeing Leander’s naked leg and goodly calf,
Cast at him from the boat a sheep’s eye and a half.
Now she is landed and the sculler come back;
By and by you shall see what Leander doth lack.

PUPPET LEANDER

63Cole, Cole, old Cole!

LANTERN

64That is the sculler’s name without control.

PUPPET LEANDER

65Cole, Cole, I say, Cole!

LANTERN

66We do hear you.

PUPPET LEANDER

67Old Cole!

LANTERN

68Old coal? Is the dyer turned collier? How do you sell?

PUPPET LEANDER

69A pox o’your manners, kiss my hole here, and smell.

LANTERN

70‘Kiss your hole, and smell’? There’s manners indeed.

PUPPET LEANDER

71Why, Cole, I say, Cole!

LANTERN

72It’s the sculler you need!

PUPPET LEANDER

73Ay, and be hanged.

LANTERN

74Be hanged? Look you yonder,
Old Cole, you must go hang with Master Leander.

PUPPET COLE

75Where is he?

PUPPET LEANDER

76Here, Cole, what fairest of fairs
Was that fare that thou landedst but now a’ Trig Stairs?

COKES

77What was that, fellow? Pray thee, tell me: I scarce understand ’em.

LANTERN

78Leander does ask, sir, what fairest of fairs, Was the fare that he landed but now at Trig Stairs?

PUPPET COLE

79It is lovely Hero.

PUPPET LEANDER

80Nero?

PUPPET COLE

81No, Hero.

LANTERN

82It is Hero
Of the Bankside, he saith — to tell you truth without erring —
Is come over into Fish Street to eat some fresh herring.
Leander says no more, but as fast as he can,
Gets on all his best clothes, and will after to the Swan.

COKES

83Most admirable good, is’t not?

LANTERN

84Stay, sculler.

PUPPET COLE

85What say you?

LANTERN

86You must stay for Leander,
And carry him to the wench.

PUPPET COLE

87You rogue, I am no pander.

COKES

88He says he is no pander. ’Tis a fine language; I understand it now.

LANTERN

89Are you no pander, Goodman Cole? Here’s no man says you are.
You’ll grow a hot Cole, it seems. Pray you, stay for your fare.

PUPPET COLE

90Will he come away?

LANTERN

91What do you say?

PUPPET COLE

92I’d ha’ him come away.

LANTERN

93Would you ha’ Leander come away? Why, pray, sir, stay.
You are angry, Goodman Cole; I believe the fair maid
Came over w’ you a’ trust — tell us, sculler, are you paid?

PUPPET COLE

94Yes, Goodman Hogrubber o’ Pict-hatch.

LANTERN

95How, Hogrubber o’ Pict-hatch?

PUPPET COLE

96Ay, Hogrubber o’ Pict-hatch.
Take you that.
The puppet strikes him over the pate.

LANTERN

97Oh, my head!

PUPPET COLE

98Harm watch, harm catch.

COKES

99‘Harm watch, harm catch’, he says — very good, i’faith. The sculler had like to ha’ knocked you, sirrah.

LANTERN

100Yes, but that his fare called him away.

PUPPET LEANDER

101Row apace, row apace, row, row, row, row, row.

LANTERN

102You are knavishly loaden, sculler: take heed where you go.

PUPPET COLE

103Knave i’your face, Goodman Rogue.

PUPPET LEANDER

104Row, row, row, row, row, row.

COKES

105He said ‘knave i’your face’, friend.

LANTERN

106Ay, sir, I heard him. But there’s no talking to these watermen: they will ha’ the last word.

COKES

107God’s my life! I am not allied to the sculler yet: he shall be ‘Dauphin my boy’. But my fiddlestick does fiddle in and out too much. I pray thee, speak to him on’t: tell him I would have him tarry in my sight more.

LANTERN

108I pray you, be content; you’ll have enough on him, sir.

Now, gentles, I take it here is none of you so stupid
But that you have heard of a little god of love, called Cupid,
Who out of kindness to Leander, hearing he but saw her
This present day and hour , doth turn himself to a drawer.
And because he would have their first meeting to be merry,
He strikes Hero in love to him with a pint of sherry,
Which he tells her from amorous Leander is sent her,
Who after him into the room of Hero doth venter.

Puppet Leander goes into Mistress Hero’s room.

PUPPET JONAS

109A pint of sack, score a pint of sack i’the Coney.

COKES

110Sack? You said but e’en now it should be sherry.

PUPPET JONAS

111Why so it is: sherry, sherry, sherry.

COKES

112‘Sherry, sherry, sherry’! By my troth he makes me merry. I must have a name for Cupid, too. Let me see — thou mightst help me now, an thou wouldest, Numps, at a dead lift, but thou art dreaming o’the stocks still! Do not think on’t, I have forgot it: ’tis but a nine days’ wonder, man; let it not trouble thee.

WASP

113I would the stocks were about your neck, sir, condition I hung by the heels in them, till the wonder were off from you, with all my heart.

COKES

114Well said, resolute Numps. — But hark you, friend, where is the friendship all this while between my drum, Damon, and my pipe, Pythias?

LANTERN

115You shall see by and by, sir.

COKES

116You think my hobby-horse is forgotten, too; no, I’ll see ’em all enact before I go; I shall not know which to love best, else.

KNOCKEM

117This gallant has interrupting vapours, troublesome vapours. Whit, puff with him.

WHIT

118No, I pre dee, captain, let him alone. He is a child, i’faith, la.

LANTERN

119Now, gentles, to the friends, who in number are two,
And lodged in that alehouse in which fair Hero does do.
Damon (for some kindness done him the last week)
Is come fair Hero in Fish Street this morning to seek:
Pythias does smell the knavery of the meeting,
And now you shall see their true friendly greeting.

PUPPET PYTHIAS

120You whoremasterly slave, you.

COKES

121‘Whoremasterly slave, you’? Very friendly and familiar, that.

PUPPET DAMON

122Whoremaster i’thy face,
Thou hast lain with her thyself, I’ll prove’t i’this place.

COKES

123Damon says Pythias has lain with her himself; he’ll prove’t in this place.

LANTERN

124They are whoremasters both, sir, that’s a plain case.

PUPPET PYTHIAS

125You lie like a rogue.

LANTERN

126Do I lie like a rogue?

PUPPET PYTHIAS

127A pimp and a scab.

LANTERN

128A pimp and a scab?
I say between you, you have both but one drab.

PUPPET DAMON

129You lie again.

LANTERN

130Do I lie again?

PUPPET DAMON

131Like a rogue again.

LANTERN

132Like a rogue again?

PUPPET PYTHIAS

133And you are a pimp again.

COKES

134‘And you are a pimp again’, he says.

PUPPET DAMON

135And a scab again.

COKES

136‘And a scab again’, he says.

LANTERN

137And I say again, you are both whoremasters again,
And you have both but one drab again.

They fight.

BOTH PUPPETS

138Dost thou, dost thou, dost thou?

LANTERN

139What, both at once?

PUPPET PYTHIAS

140Down with him, Damon.

PUPPET DAMON

141Pink his guts, Pythias.

LANTERN

142What, so malicious!
Will ye murder me, masters both, i’mine own house?

COKES

143Ho! well acted my drum, well acted my pipe, well acted still!

WASP

144Well acted, with all my heart.

LANTERN

145Hold, hold your hands.

COKES

146Ay, both your hands, for my sake! For you ha’ both done well.

PUPPET DAMON

147Gramercy, pure Pythias.

PUPPET PYTHIAS

148Gramercy, dear Damon.

COKES

149Gramercy to you both, my pipe and my drum.

BOTH PUPPETS

150Come now, we’ll together to breakfast to Hero.

LANTERN

151’Tis well, you can now go to breakfast to Hero;
You have given me my breakfast, with a ’hone and ’honero.

COKES

152How is’t, friend? Ha’ they hurt thee?

LANTERN

153Oh, no!

Between you and I, sir, we do but make show.
Thus, gentles, you perceive, without any denial,
’Twixt Damon and Pythias here, friendship’s true trial.
Though hourly they quarrel thus, and roar each with other,
They fight you no more than does brother with brother.
But friendly together, at the next man they meet
They let fly their anger, as here you might see’t.

COKES

154Well, we have seen’t, and thou hast felt it, whatsoever thou sayest. What’s next? What’s next?

LANTERN

155This while young Leander with fair Hero is drinking,
And Hero grown drunk, to any man’s thinking!
Yet was it not three pints of sherry could flaw her,
Till Cupid, distinguished like Jonas the drawer,
From under his apron, where his lechery lurks,
Put love in her sack. Now mark how it works.

PUPPET HERO

156O Leander, Leander, my dear, my dear Leander,
I’ll for ever be thy goose, so thou’lt be my gander.

COKES

157Excellently well said, fiddle: she’ll ever be his goose, so he’ll be her gander —was’t not so?

LANTERN

158Yes, sir, but mark his answer now.

PUPPET LEANDER

159And sweetest of geese, before I go to bed
I’ll swim o’er the Thames, my goose thee to tread.

COKES

160Brave! He will swim o’er the Thames and tread his goose tonight, he says.

LANTERN

161Ay, peace, sir: they’ll be angry if they hear you eavesdropping, now they are setting their match.

PUPPET LEANDER

162But lest the Thames should be dark, my goose, my dear friend,
Let thy window be provided of a candle’s end.

PUPPET HERO

163Fear not, my gander: I protest, I should handle
My matters very ill if I had not a whole candle.

PUPPET LEANDER

164Well then, look to’t, and kiss me to boot.

LANTERN

165Now, here come the friends again, Pythias and Damon,
And under their cloaks they have of bacon a gammon.

DAMON and PYTHIAS enter [the alehouse].

PUPPET PYTHIAS

166Drawer, fill some wine here. LANTERN How, some wine there?
There’s company already, sir, pray, forbear!

PUPPET DAMON

167’Tis Hero.

LANTERN

168Yes, but she will not be taken,
After sack and fresh herring, with your Dunmow bacon.

PUPPET PYTHIAS

169You lie, it’s Westfabian.

LANTERN

170‘Westphalian’, you should say.

PUPPET DAMON

171If you hold not your peace, you are a coxcomb, I would say.

Leander and Hero are kissing.

PUPPET PYTHIAS

172What’s here? What’s here? Kiss, kiss, upon kiss.

LANTERN

173Ay, wherefore should they not? What harm is in this?
’Tis Mistress Hero.

PUPPET DAMON

174Mistress Hero’s a whore.

LANTERN

175Is she a whore? Keep you quiet, or sir knave, out of door.

PUPPET DAMON

176Knave, out of door?

PUPPET HERO

177Yes, knave, out of door.

Here the Puppets quarrel and fall together by the ears.

PUPPET DAMON

178Whore, out of door.

PUPPET HERO

179I say, knave, out of door.

PUPPET DAMON

180I say, whore, out of door.

PUPPET PYTHIAS

181Yea, so say I, too.

PUPPET HERO

182Kiss the whore o’the arse.

LANTERN

183Now you ha’ something to do:
You must kiss her o’the arse, she says.

BOTH PUPPETS

184So we will, so we will.

[They kick her.]

PUPPET HERO

185Oh, my haunches, oh, my haunches — hold, hold!

LANTERN

186[To Puppet Leander] Stand’st thou still?
Leander, where art thou? Stand’st thou still like a sot,
And not offer’st to break both their heads with a pot?
See who’s at thine elbow, there! Puppet Jonas and Cupid.

PUPPET JONAS

187Upon ’em Leander, be not so stupid.

They fight.

PUPPET LEANDER

188You goat-bearded slave!

PUPPET DAMON

189You whoremaster knave.

PUPPET LEANDER

190Thou art a whoremaster.

PUPPET JONAS

191Whoremasters all.

LANTERN

192See, Cupid with a word has ta’en up the brawl.

KNOCKEM

193These be fine vapours!

COKES

194By this good day, they fight bravely! Do they not, Numps?

WASP

195Yes, they lacked but you to be their second, all this while.

LANTERN

196This tragical encounter, falling out thus to busy us,
It raises up the ghost of their friend Dionysius:
Not like a monarch, but the master of a school,
In a scrivener’s furred gown, which shows he is no fool,
For therein he hath wit enough to keep himself warm.
O Damon, he cries, and Pythias, what harm
Hath poor Dionysius done you in his grave
That, after his death, you should fall out thus, and rave,
And call amorous Leander whoremaster knave?

PUPPET DAMON

197I cannot, I will not, I promise you, endure it.

5.5

[Enter] to them BUSY.

BUSY

1Down with Dagon, down with Dagon! ’Tis I, will no longer endure your profanations.

LANTERN

2What mean you, sir?

BUSY

3I will remove Dagon there, I say, that idol, that heathenish idol, that remains (as I may say) a beam, a very beam: not a beam of the sun, nor a beam of the moon, nor a beam of a balance, neither a house beam, nor a weaver’s beam, but a beam in the eye, in the eye of the Brethren; a very great beam, an exceeding great beam; such as are your stage-players, rhymers, and morris dancers, who have walked hand in hand in contempt of the Brethren and the Cause; and been borne out by instruments of no mean countenance.

LANTERN

4Sir, I present nothing but what is licensed by authority.

BUSY

5Thou art all licence, even licentiousness itself, Shimei!

LANTERN

6I have the Master of Revels’ hand for’t, sir.

BUSY

7The Master of Rebels’ hand thou hast: Satan’s! Hold thy peace: thy scurrility shut up thy mouth. Thy profession is damnable, and in pleading for it thou dost plead for Baal. I have long opened my mouth wide and gaped, I have gaped as the oyster for the tide after thy destruction, but cannot compass it by suit or dispute, so that I look for a bickering ere long, and then a battle.

KNOCKEM

8Good Banbury-vapours.

COKES

9Friend, you’d have an ill match on’t, if you bicker with him here. Though he be no man o’the fist, he has friends that will go to cuffs for him. Numps, will not you take our side?

EDGWORTH

10Sir, it shall not need. In my mind, he offers him a fairer course: to end it by disputation! Hast thou nothing to say for thyself in defence of thy quality?

LANTERN

11Faith, sir, I am not well studied in these controversies between the hypocrites and us. But here’s one of my motion, Puppet Dionysius, shall undertake him, and I’ll venture the cause on’t.

COKES

12Who? My hobby-horse? Will he dispute with him?

LANTERN

13Yes, sir, and make a hobby-ass of him, I hope.

COKES

14That’s excellent! Indeed he looks like the best scholar of ’em all. — Come, sir, you must be as good as your word now.

BUSY

15I will not fear to make my spirit and gifts known! Assist me, zeal, fill me, fill me, that is, make me full!

WINWIFE

16[To Grace] What a desperate, profane wretch is this! Is there any ignorance or impudence like his, to call his zeal to fill him against a puppet?

GRACE

17I know no fitter match than a puppet to commit with an hypocrite!

BUSY

18First, I say unto thee, idol, thou hast no calling.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

19You lie, I am called Dionysius.

LANTERN

20The Motion says you lie: he is called Dionysius i’the matter, and to that calling he answers.

BUSY

21I mean no vocation, idol, no present lawful calling.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

22Is yours a lawful calling?

LANTERN

23The motion asketh if yours be a lawful calling?

BUSY

24Yes, mine is of the spirit.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

25Then idol is a lawful calling.

LANTERN

26He says, then idol is a lawful calling! For you called him idol, and your calling is of the spirit.

COKES

27Well disputed, hobby-horse!

BUSY

28Take not part with the wicked, young gallant. He neigheth and hinnyeth; all is but hinnying sophistry. I call him idol again. Yet I say, his calling, his profession, is profane: it is profane, idol.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

29It is not profane!

LANTERN

30It is not profane, he says.

BUSY

31It is profane.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

32It is not profane.

BUSY

33It is profane.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

34It is not profane.

LANTERN

35Well said, confute him with ‘not’ still. — You cannot bear him down with your base noise, sir.

BUSY

36Nor he me, with his treble creaking, though he creak like the chariot wheels of Satan. I am zealous for the Cause —

LANTERN

37As a dog for a bone.

BUSY

38And I say, it is profane, as being the page of Pride and the waiting-woman of Vanity.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

39Yea? What say you to your tire-women, then? —

LANTERN

40Good.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

41— or feather-makers i’the Friars, that are o’your faction of faith? Are not they with their perukes and their puffs, their fans and their huffs, as much pages of Pride and waiters upon Vanity? What say you? What say you? What say you?

BUSY

42I will not answer for them.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

43Because you cannot, because you cannot. Is a bugle-maker a lawful calling? Or the confect-makers, such you have there? Or your French fashioner? You’d have all the sin within yourselves, would you not? Would you not?

BUSY

44No, Dagon.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

45What then, Dagonet? Is a puppet worse than these?

BUSY

46Yes, and my main argument against you is that you are an abomination: for the male among you putteth on the apparel of the female, and the female of the male.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

47You lie, you lie, you lie abominably.

COKES

48Good, by my troth, he has given him the lie thrice.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

49It is your old stale argument against the players, but it will not hold against the puppets, for we have neither male nor female amongst us. And that thou mayst see if thou wilt, like a malicious purblind zeal as thou art!

The puppet takes up his garment.

EDGWORTH

50By my faith, there he has answered you, friend — by plain demonstration.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

51Nay, I’ll prove against ere a rabbin of ’em all that my standing is as lawful as his; that I speak by inspiration as well as he; that I have as little to do with learning as he; and do scorn her helps as much as he.

BUSY

52I am confuted; the Cause hath failed me.

PUPPET DIONYSIUS

53Then be converted, be converted.

LANTERN

54Be converted, I pray you, and let the play go on!

BUSY

55Let it go on. For I am changed, and will become a beholder with you!

COKES

56That’s brave, i’faith: thou hast carried it away, hobby-horse! On with the play!

The Justice discovers himself.

JUSTICE

57Stay, now do I forbid, I — Adam Overdo! Sit still, I charge you.

COKES

58What, my brother-i’-law!

GRACE

59My wise guardian!

EDGWORTH

60Justice Overdo!

JUSTICE

61It is time to take enormity by the forehead, and brand it; for I have discovered enough.

5.6

[Enter] to them, QUARLOUS (like the madman) [and Mistress] PURECRAFT.

QUARLOUS

1Nay, come, mistress bride. You must do as I do now. You must be mad with me, in truth. [He indicates the deed.] I have here Justice Overdo for it.

JUSTICE

2Peace, good Troubleall; come hither, and you shall trouble none. I will take the charge of you, and your friend, too. (To the Cutpurse and Mistress Littlewit.) You, also, young man, shall be my care: stand there.

EDGWORTH

3[Aside] Now, mercy upon me.

The rest are stealing away.

KNOCKEM

4[Aside to Whit] Would we were away, Whit: these are dangerous vapours! Best fall off with our birds, for fear o’the cage.

JUSTICE

5Stay, is not my name your terror?

WHIT

6Yesh, faith, man, and it ish for tat we would be gone, man.

[Enter] JOHN.

JOHN

7O gentlemen! Did you not see a wife of mine? I ha’ lost my little wife, as I shall be trusted — my little pretty Win. I left her at the great woman’s house in trust yonder, the pig-woman’s, with Captain Jordan and Captain Whit — very good men — and I cannot hear of her. Poor fool, I fear she’s stepped aside. — Mother, did you not see Win?

JUSTICE

8If this grave matron be your mother, sir, stand by her, et digito compesce labellum; I may perhaps spring a wife for you anon. — Brother Barthol’mew, I am sadly sorry to see you so lightly given and such a disciple of enormity, with your grave governor, Humphrey. But stand you both there in the middle place; I will reprehend you in your course. — Mistress Grace, let me rescue you out of the hands of the stranger.

WINWIFE

9Pardon me, sir, I am a kinsman of hers.

JUSTICE

10Are you so? Of what name, sir?

WINWIFE

11Winwife, sir.

JUSTICE

12Master Winwife? I hope you have won no wife of her, sir. If you have, I will examine the possibility of it at fit leisure. Now, to my enormities: look upon me, O London! and see me, O Smithfield: the Example of Justice and Mirror of Magistrates, the true top of formality and scourge of enormity. Hearken unto my labours, and but observe my discoveries; and compare Hercules with me, if thou dar’st, of old, or Columbus, Magellan, or our countryman Drake, of later times. Stand forth, you weeds of enormity, and spread. (To Busy) First, Rabbi Busy, thou superlunatical hypocrite; (To Lantern) next, thou other extremity, thou profane professor of puppetry, little better than poetry; (To the Horse-corser, and Cutpurse) then thou strong debaucher and seducer of youth: witness this easy and honest young man; (Then Captain Whit, and Mistress Littlewit) now, thou esquire of dames, madams, and twelvepenny ladies; now, my green madam herself, of the price. Let me unmask your ‘Ladyship’.

JOHN

13Oh, my wife, my wife, my wife!

JUSTICE

14Is she your wife? Redde te Harpocratem!

Enter TROUBLEALL [without his gown and hat, and covering himself with a large pan, pursued by] URSULA [and] NIGHTINGALE.

TROUBLEALL

15By your leave, stand by, my masters, be uncovered!

URSULA

16Oh, stay him, stay him! Help to cry, Nightingale: my pan, my pan!

JUSTICE

17What’s the matter?

NIGHTINGALE

18He has stol’n Gammer Urs’la’s pan.

TROUBLEALL

19Yes, and I fear no man but Justice Overdo.

JUSTICE

20Urs’la? Where is she? (To Ursula and Nightingale.) Oh, the sow of enormity, this! Welcome, stand you there; you, songster, there.

URSULA

21An please your worship, I am in no fault: a gentleman stripped him in my booth, and borrowed his gown and his hat, and
[Indicating Troubleall] he ran away with my goods here for it.

JUSTICE

22Then this is the true madman, and (To Quarlous) you are the enormity!

QUARLOUS

23[Removing borrowed clothes and false beard] You are i’the right: I am mad but from the gown outward.

JUSTICE

24Stand you there.

QUARLOUS

25Where you please, sir.

[Waking,] Mistress Overdo is sick, and her husband is silenced.

MRS OVERDO

26Oh, lend me a basin, I am sick, I am sick! Where’s Master Overdo? Bridget, call hither my Adam.

JUSTICE

27How?

WHIT

28Dy very own wife, i’fait, worshipful Adam.

MRS OVERDO

29Will not my Adam come at me? Shall I see him no more then?

QUARLOUS

30Sir, why do you not go on with the enormity? Are you oppressed with it? I’ll help you. Hark you, sir, i’your ear: your ‘innocent young man’ you have ta’en such care of all this day is a cutpurse, that hath got all your brother Cokes his things, and helped you to your beating and the stocks. If you have a mind to hang him now, and show him your magistrate’s wit, you may — but I should think it were better recovering the goods, and to save your estimation in pardoning him. I thank you, sir, for the gift of your ward, Mistress Grace: [He shows the deed.] look you, here is your hand and seal, by the way. Master Winwife, give you joy, you are ‘Palamon’, you are possessed o’the gentlewoman, but she must pay me value: here’s warrant for it. And honest madman, there’s thy gown and cap again; I thank thee for my wife. (To the widow) Nay, I can be mad, sweetheart, when I please, still: never fear me. And careful Numps, where’s he? I thank him for my licence.

WASP

31How!

QUARLOUS

32’Tis true, Numps.

WASP

33I’ll be hanged then.

QUARLOUS

34Look i’your box, Numps. (Wasp misseth the licence) —
[To Justice Overdo] Nay, sir, stand not you fixed here like a stake in Finsbury to be shot at, or the whipping-post i’the Fair, but get your wife out o’the air — it will make her worse else. And remember you are but Adam, flesh and blood! — you have your frailty. Forget your other name of Overdo, and invite us all to supper. There you and I will compare our ‘ discoveries’, and drown the memory of all enormity in your bigg’st bowl at home.

COKES

35How now, Numps, ha’ you lost it? I warrant, ’twas when thou wert i’the stocks. Why dost not speak?

WASP

36I will never speak while I live again, for aught I know.

JUSTICE

37Nay, Humphrey, if I be patient, you must be so, too. This pleasant conceited gentleman hath wrought upon my judgement, and prevailed. I pray you, take care of your sick friend, Mistress Alice. And my good friends all —

QUARLOUS

38And no ‘enormities’.

JUSTICE

39— I invite you home with me to my house to supper. I will have none fear to go along, for my intents are ad correctionem, non ad destructionem; ad aedificandum, non ad diruendum. So, lead on.

COKES

40Yes, and bring the actors along: we’ll ha’ the rest o’the play at home!

[Exeunt.]

THE END
