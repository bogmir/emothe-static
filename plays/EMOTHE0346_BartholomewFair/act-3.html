Ben Jonson

Bartholomew Fair

← Act II Act IV →

3.1

[Enter] WHIT, HAGGIS, [and] BRISTLE. LEATHERHEAD [and] TRASH [return to their wares].

WHIT

1Nay, ’tish all gone now! Dish ’tish, phen tou vilt not be phitin call, Master Offisher. Phat ish a man te better to lishen out noishes for tee, an tou art in an oder ’orld, being very shuffishient noishes, and gallantsh, too? One o’their brabblesh would have fed ush all dish fortnight. But tou art so bushy about beggarsh still, tou hast no leshure to intend shentlemen, an’t be.

HAGGIS

2Why, I told you, Davy Bristle.

BRISTLE

3Come, come, you told me a pudding, Toby Haggis, a matter of nothing — I am sure it came to nothing! You said, let’s go to Urs’la’s, indeed, but then you met the man with the monsters, and I could not get you from him. An old fool, not leave seeing yet? HAGGIS Why, who would ha’ thought anybody would ha’ quarrelled so early, or that the ale o’the Fair would ha’ been up so soon?

WHIT

4Phy? Phat a’clock toest tou tink it ish, man?

HAGGIS

5I cannot tell.

WHIT

6Tou art a vishe vatchman, i’te meanteeme.

HAGGIS

7Why? Should the watch go by the clock, or the clock by the watch, I pray?

BRISTLE

8One should go by another, if they did well.

WHIT

9Tou art right now! Phen did’st tou ever know or hear of a shuffishient vatchman but he did tell the clock, phat bushiness soever he had?

BRISTLE

10Nay, that’s most true, a sufficient watchman knows what a’clock it is.

WHIT

11Shleeping or vaking! Ash well as te clock himshelf, or te jack dat shtrikes him!

BRISTLE

12Let’s inquire of Master Leatherhead or Joan Trash here. — Master Leatherhead, do you hear, Master Leatherhead?

WHIT

13If it be a Ledderhead, ’tish a very tick Ledderhead, tat sho mush noish vill not piersh him.

LEATHERHEAD

14I have a little business now: good friends, do not trouble me.

WHIT

15Phat? Because o’ty wrought neetcap, and ty phelvet sherkin, man? Phy, I have sheen tee in ty ledder sherkin ere now, Mashter o’ de Hobby-horses, as bushy and as stately as tou sheem’st to be.

TRASH

16Why, what an you have, Captain Whit? He has his choice of jerkins, you may see by that, and his caps too, I assure you, when he pleases to be either sick or employed.

LEATHERHEAD

17God-a-mercy Joan, answer for me.

WHIT

18[To the Watchmen] Away, be not sheen i’my company: here be shentlemen and men of vorship.

[Exeunt Haggis and Bristle.]

3.2

[Enter] QUARLOUS [and] WINWIFE.

QUARLOUS

1We had wonderful ill luck to miss this prologue o’the purse, but the best is, we shall have five acts of him ere night. He’ll be spectacle enough! I’ll answer for’t.

WHIT

2O Creesh! Duke Quarlous, how dosht tou? Tou dosht not know me, I fear? I am te vishesht man, but Justish Overdo, in all Barthol’mew Fair now. Gi’ me twelvepence from tee, I vill help tee to a vife vorth forty marks for’t, an’t be.

QUARLOUS

3Away, rogue, pimp, away!

WHIT

4And she shall show tee as fine cut-’ork for’t in her shmock, too, as tou cansht vish, i’faith. Vilt tou have her, vorshipful Vinvife? I vill help tee to her here, be ant be, in te pig-quarter: gi’ me ty twel’pence from tee.

WINWIFE

5Why, there’s twel’pence: pray thee, wilt thou be gone?

WHIT

6Tou art a vorthy man and a vorshipful man still.

QUARLOUS

7Get you gone, rascal.

WHIT

8I do mean it, man. Prinsh Quarlous, if tou hasht need on me, tou shalt find me here, at Urs’la’s. I vill see phat ale and punk ish i’te pigshty for tee, bless ty good vorship.

[He withdraws to Ursula’s booth.]

[Enter] BUSY, JOHN, [Mistress] PURECRAFT, [and] WIN.

QUARLOUS

9Look who comes here! John Littlewit!

WINWIFE

10And his wife, and my widow, her mother: the whole family.

QUARLOUS

11’Slight, you must gi’ ’em all fairings now!

WINWIFE

12Not I, I’ll not see ’em.

[He draws Quarlous aside.]

QUARLOUS

13They are going a-feasting. What schoolmaster’s that, is with ’em?

WINWIFE

14That’s my rival, I believe, the baker!

BUSY

15So, walk on in the middle way, foreright; turn neither to the right hand nor to the left; let not your eyes be drawn aside with vanity nor your ear with noises.

QUARLOUS

16Oh, I know him by that start!

LEATHERHEAD

17What do you lack? What do you buy, pretty mistress? A fine hobby-horse, to make your son a tilter? A drum, to make him a soldier? A fiddle, to make him a reveller? What is’t you lack? Little dogs for your daughters? Or babies, male or female?

BUSY

18Look not toward them, hearken not: the place is Smithfield, or the field of smiths, the grove of hobby-horses and trinkets; the wares are the wares of devils. And the whole Fair is the shop of Satan! They are hooks and baits, very baits, that are hung out on every side to catch you, and to hold you as it were by the gills and by the nostrils, as the fisher doth: therefore, you must not look nor turn toward them — The heathen man could stop his ears with wax against the harlot o’the sea: do you the like with your fingers against the bells of the Beast.

WINWIFE

19[To Quarlous] What flashes comes from him!

QUARLOUS

20Oh, he has those of his oven! A notable hot baker ’twas, when he plied the peel. He is leading his flock into the Fair now.

WINWIFE

21Rather driving ’em to the pens, for he will let ’em look upon nothing.

[Enter] KNOCKEM [and] WHIT [from Ursula’s booth.]

KNOCKEM

22Gentlewomen, the weather’s hot! Whither walk you? Have a care o’your fine velvet caps, the Fair is dusty. Take a sweet delicate booth, with boughs, here i’the way, and cool yourselves i’the shade — you and your friends. (Littlewit is gazing at the sign, which is the pig’s head with a large writing under it.)
The best pig and bottle-ale i’the Fair, sir. Old Urs’la is cook, there you may read: the pig’s head speaks it. Poor soul, she has had a stringhalt, the maryhinchco, but she’s prettily amended.

WHIT

23A delicate show-pig, little mistress, with shweet sauce, and crackling, like de bay-leaf i’ de fire, la! Tou shalt ha’ de clean side o’ de table-clot and di glass vashed with phatersh of Dame Annessh Clear.

JOHN

24This’s fine, verily: ‘Here be the best pigs, and she does roast ’em as well as ever she did’, the pig’s head says.

KNOCKEM

25Excellent, excellent, mistress, with fire o’ juniper and rosemary branches! [To John] The oracle of the pig’s head, that, sir.

PURECRAFT

26Son, were you not warned of the vanity of the eye? Have you forgot the wholesome admonition so soon?

JOHN

27Good mother, how shall we find a pig, if we do not look about for’t? Will it run off o’the spit into our mouths, think you, as in Lubberland, and cry, ‘Wee, wee’?

BUSY

28No, but your mother, religiously wise, conceiveth it may offer itself by other means to the sense, as by way of steam, which I think it doth, here in this place — huh! huh! — yes, it doth.
(Busy scents after it like a hound.) And it were a sin of obstinacy, great obstinacy, high and horrible obstinacy, to decline or resist the good titillation of the famelic sense, which is the smell. Therefore be bold — huh! huh! huh! — follow the scent. Enter the tents of the unclean for once, and satisfy your wife’s frailty. Let your frail wife be satisfied; your zealous mother, and my suffering self, will also be satisfied.

JOHN

29Come, Win, as good winny here as go farther and see nothing.

BUSY

30We scape so much of the other vanities by our early entering.

PURECRAFT

31It is an edifying consideration.

WIN

32[Aside to John] This is scurvy, that we must come into the Fair and not look on’t.

JOHN

33Win, have patience, Win, I’ll tell you more anon.

KNOCKEM

34Mooncalf, entertain within there! The best pig i’the booth, a pork-like pig! These are Banbury-bloods, o’the sincere stud, come a-pig-hunting. Whit, wait, Whit, look to your charge.

BUSY

35A pig prepare presently, let a pig be prepared to us.

[Exit Whit, guiding Busy, Mistress Purecraft, John, and Win into the booth.]

[Enter] MOONCALF [and] URSULA [from the booth].

MOONCALF

36’Slight, who be these?

URSULA

37Is this the good service, Jordan, you’d do me?

KNOCKEM

38Why, Urs? Why, Urs? Thou’lt ha’ vapours i’thy leg again presently; pray thee, go in: ’t may turn to the scratches else.

URSULA

39Hang your vapours, they are stale, and stink like you. Are these theguests o’the game you promised to fill my pit withal today?

KNOCKEM

40Ay, what ail they, Urs?

URSULA

41Ail they? They are all sippers, sippers o’the city: they look as they would not drink off two penn’orth of bottle-ale amongst ’em.

MOONCALF

42A body may read that i’their small printed ruffs.

KNOCKEM

43Away, thou art a fool, Urs, and thy Mooncalf, too, i’your ignorant vapours now! Hence! Good guests, I say, right hypocrites, good gluttons. In, and set a couple o’ pigs o’the board, and half a dozen of the biggest bottles afore ’em, and call Whit. [Exit Mooncalf into the booth.]
I do not love to hear innocents abused: fine ambling hypocrites! And a stone-puritan, with a sorrel head and beard, good-mouthed gluttons — two to a pig. Away!

URSULA

44Are you sure they are such?

KNOCKEM

45O’the right breed, thou shalt try ’em by the teeth, Urs. Where’s this Whit?

[Exit Ursula into her booth.]

[Enter WHIT.]

WHIT

46Behold, man, and see, what a worthy man am ee!
With the fury of my sword, and the shaking of my beard,
I will make ten thousand men afeared.

KNOCKEM

47Well said, brave Whit! In, and fear the ale out o’the bottles into the bellies of the Brethren and the Sisters. Drink to the Cause and pure vapours.

[Exeunt Whit and Knockem into the booth.]

QUARLOUS

48My roarer is turned tapster, methinks. Now were a fine time for thee, Winwife, to lay aboard thy widow — thou’lt never be master of a better season or place. She that will venture herself into the Fair, and a pig-box, will admit any assault, be assured of that.

WINWIFE

49I love not enterprises of that suddenness, though.

QUARLOUS

50I’ll warrant thee then, no wife out o’the widows’ hundred. If I had but as much title to her as to have breathed once on that strait stomacher of hers, I would now assure myself to carry her yet, ere she went out of Smithfield. Or she should carry me, which were the fitter sight, I confess. But you are a modest undertaker, by circumstances and degrees. Come, ’tis disease in thee, not judgement — I should offer at all together. Look, here’s the poor fool again that was stung by the wasp erewhile.

[They stand aside.]

3.3

[Enter] JUSTICE [OVERDO].

JUSTICE

1I will make no more orations, shall draw on these tragical conclusions. And I begin now to think that, by a spice of collateral justice, Adam Overdo deserved this beating; for I, the said Adam, was one cause (a by-cause) why the purse was lost — and my wife’s brother’s purse, too — which they know not of yet. But I shall make very good mirth with it at supper — that will be the sport — and put my little friend Master Humphrey Wasp’s choler quite out of countenance: when, sitting at the upper end o’my table, as I use, and drinking to my brother Cokes and Mistress Alice Overdo, as I will, my wife, for their good affection to old Bradley, I deliver to ’em it was I that was cudgelled, and show ’em the marks. To see what bad events may peep out o’the tail of good purposes! The care I had of that civil young man I took fancy to this morning (and have not left it yet) drew me to that exhortation; which drew the company, indeed; which drew the cutpurse; which drew the money; which drew my brother Cokes his loss; which drew on Wasp’s anger; which drew on my beating — a pretty gradation! And they shall ha’ it i’their dish, i’faith, at night for fruit — I love to be merry at my table. I had thought once, at one special blow he ga’ me, to have revealed myself! But then (I thank thee, fortitude!) I remembered that a wise man ( and who is ever so great a part o’the commonwealth in himself) for no particular disaster ought to abandon a public good design. The husbandman ought not, for one unthankful year, to forsake the plough; the shepherd ought not, for one scabbed sheep, to throw by his tar-box; the pilot ought not, for one leak i’the poop, to quit the helm; nor the alderman ought not, for one custard more at a meal, to give up his cloak; the constable ought not to break his staff and forswear the watch, for one roaring night; nor the piper o’the parish — ut parvis componere magna solebam — to put up his pipes, for one rainy Sunday. These are certain knocking conclusions: out of which I am resolved, come what come can, come beating, come imprisonment, come infamy, come banishment, nay, come the rack, come the hurdle — welcome all! — I will not discover who I am till my due time; and yet still, all shall be, as I said ever, in justice’ name, and the King’s, and for the commonwealth.

[Exit.]

WINWIFE

2What does he talk to himself, and act so seriously? Poor fool!

QUARLOUS

3No matter what. Here’s fresher argument: intend that.

3.4

[Enter] COKES, Mistress OVERDO, [and] GRACE, [followed by] WASP [loaded with purchases]

Leatherhead [and] Trash [display their wares].

COKES

1Come, Mistress Grace, come, sister, here’s more fine sights yet, i’faith. God’s lid, where’s Numps? LEATHERHEAD What do you lack, gentlemen? What is’t you buy? Fine rattles? Drums? Babies? Little dogs? And birds for ladies? What do you lack?

COKES

2Good honest Numps, keep afore: I am so afraid thou’lt lose somewhat — my heart was at my mouth when I missed thee.

WASP

3You were best buy a whip i’your hand to drive me.

COKES

4Nay, do not mistake, Numps, thou art so apt to mistake — I would butwatch the goods. Look you now, the treble fiddle was e’en almost like to be lost!

WASP

5Pray you, take heed you lose not yourself: your best way were e’en get up and ride for more surety. Buy a token’s worth of great pins to fasten yourself to my shoulder. LEATHERHEAD What do you lack, gentlemen? Fine purses, pouches, pin-cases, pipes? What is’t you lack? A pair o’ smiths to wake you i’the morning? Or a fine whistling bird?

COKES

6Numps, here be finer things than any we ha’ bought, by odds! And more delicate horses, a great deal! Good Numps, stay, and come hither.

WASP

7Will you scorse with him? You are in Smithfield, you may fit yourself with a fine easy-going street-nag for your saddle again’ Michaelmas Term, do. Has he ne’er a little odd cart for you to make a caroche on i’the country, with four pied hobby-horses? Why the measles should you stand here with your train, cheaping of dogs, birds, and babies? You ha’ no children to bestow ’em on, ha’ you?

COKES

8No, but again’ I ha’ children, Numps, that’s all one.

WASP

9Do, do, do, do: how many shall you have, think you? An I were as you, I’d buy for all my tenants, too: they are a kind o’ civil savages, that will part with their children for rattles, pipes, and knives. You were best buy a hatchet or two, and truck with ’em.

COKES

10Good Numps, hold that little tongue o’thine, and save it a labour. I am resolute Bat, thou know’st.

WASP

11A resolute fool, you are, I know, and a very sufficient coxcomb, with all my heart! Nay, you have it, sir, an you be angry: turd i’your teeth, twice (if I said it not once afore) and much good do you.

WINWIFE

12[To Quarlous] Was there ever such a self-affliction? And so impertinent?

QUARLOUS

13Alas! His care will go near to crack him; let’s in, and comfort him.

[They join the others.]

WASP

14Would I had been set i’the ground, all but the head on me, and had my brains bowled at or threshed out, when first I underwent this plague of a charge!

QUARLOUS

15How now, Numps! Almost tired i’your protectorship? Overparted? Overparted?

WASP

16Why, I cannot tell, sir, it may be I am. Does’t grieve you?

QUARLOUS

17No, I swear does’t not, Numps: to satisfy you.

WASP

18Numps? ’Sblood, you are fine and familiar! How long ha’ we been acquainted, I pray you?

QUARLOUS

19I think it may be remembered, Numps, that! ’Twas since morning, sure.

WASP

20Why, I hope I know’t well enough, sir. I did not ask to be told.

QUARLOUS

21No? Why then?

WASP

22It’s no matter why. You see with your eyes now what I said to you today? You’ll believe me another time?

QUARLOUS

23Are you removing the Fair, Numps?

WASP

24A pretty question! and a very civil one! Yes, faith, I ha’ my lading, you see, or shall have anon: you may know whose beast I am by my burden. If the pannier-man’s jack were ever better known by his loins of mutton, I’ll be flayed and feed dogs for him, when his time comes.

WINWIFE

25[To Quarlous] How melancholy Mistress Grace is yonder! Pray thee, let’s go enter ourselves in ‘ grace’ with her.

[They draw her aside and talk apart with her.]

COKES

26[To Leatherhead] Those six horses, friend, I’ll have —

WASP

27How!

COKES

28And the three Jews’ trumps; and half-a-dozen o’ birds, and that drum (I have one drum already) and your smiths — I like that device o’your smiths very pretty well. And four halberds — and (le’ me see) that fine painted great lady, and her three women for state, I’ll have.

WASP

29No, the shop; buy the whole shop, it will be best, the shop, the shop! LEATHERHEAD If his worship please.

WASP

30Yes, and keep it during the Fair, bob-chin.

COKES

31Peace, Numps. [To Leatherhead] Friend, do not meddle with him, an you be wise, and would show your head above board; he will sting thorough your wrought nightcap, believe me. A set of these violins I would buy, too, for a delicate young noise I have i’the country that are every one a size less than another, just like your fiddles. I would fain have a fine young masque at my marriage, now I think on’t — but I do want such a number o’things. And Numps will not help me now, and I dare not speak to him.

TRASH

32Will your worship buy any gingerbread, very good bread, comfortable bread?

COKES

33Gingerbread! Yes, let’s see.

He runs to her shop.

WASP

34There’s the t’other springe!

LEATHERHEAD

35Is this well, Goody Joan? To interrupt my market, in the midst, and call away my customers? Can you answer this at the Piepowders?

TRASH

36Why, if his mastership have a mind to buy, I hope my ware lies as open as another’s: I may show my ware, as well as you yours.

COKES

37Hold your peace; I’ll content you both: I’ll buy up his shop, and thy basket.

WASP

38Will you, i’faith?

LEATHERHEAD

39Why should you put him from it, friend?

WASP

40Cry you mercy! You’d be sold too, would you? What’s the price on you, jerkin and all as you stand? Ha’ you any qualities?

TRASH

41Yes, Goodman Angry-man, you shall find he has qualities, if you cheapen him.

WASP

42God’s so, you ha’ the selling of him! What are they? Will they be bought for love or money?

TRASH

43No, indeed, sir.

WASP

44For what then? Victuals?

TRASH

45He scorns victuals, sir, he has bread and butter at home, thanks be to God! And yet he will do more for a good meal, if the toy take him i’the belly — marry then, they must not set him at lower end; if they do, he’ll go away, though he fast. But put him atop o’the table, where his place is, and he’ll do you forty fine things. He has not been sent for and sought out for nothing at your great city-suppers, to put down Coryate and Cokeley, and been laughed at for his labour; he’ll play you all the puppets i’the town over, and the players, every company, and his own company too: he spares nobody!

COKES

46I’faith?

TRASH

47He was the first, sir, that ever baited the fellow i’the bear’s skin, an’t like your worship: no dog ever came near him since. And for fine motions!

COKES

48Is he good at those, too? Can he set out a masque, trow?

TRASH

49O Lord, master! Sought to far and near for his inventions. And he engrosses all, he makes all the puppets i’the Fair.

COKES

50Dost thou, in troth, old velvet jerkin? Give me thy hand.

TRASH

51Nay, sir, you shall see him in his new velvet jerkin, and a scarf, too, at night, when you hear him interpret Master Littlewit’s motion.

COKES

52Speak no more, but shut up shop presently, friend. I’ll buy both it and thee, too, to carry down with me, and her hamper beside. Thy shop shall furnish out the masque and hers the banquet: I cannot go less to set out anything with credit. What’s the price, at a word, o’thy whole shop, case and all, as it stands?

LEATHERHEAD

53Sir, it stands me in six-and-twenty shillings sevenpence halfpenny, besides three shillings for my ground.

COKES

54Well, thirty shillings will do all, then! And what comes yours to?

TRASH

55Four shillings and eleven pence, sir, ground and all, an’t like your worship.

COKES

56Yes, it does like my worship very well, poor woman. That’s five shillings more.
[He counts out the money to them in turn.] What a masque shall I furnish out for forty shillings — twenty pound Scotch — and a banquet of gingerbread! There’s a stately thing! Numps! Sister! And my wedding gloves too — that I never thought on afore! All my wedding gloves, gingerbread! Oh, me! What a device will there be, to make ’em eat their fingers’ ends! And delicate brooches for the bridemen, and all! And then I’ll ha’ this posy put to ’em: ‘For the best grace’, meaning Mistress Grace, my wedding posy.

GRACE

57I am beholden to you, sir, and to your Barthol’mew-wit.

WASP

58You do not mean this, do you? Is this your first purchase?

COKES

59Yes, faith, and I do not think, Numps, but thou’lt say it was the wisest act that ever I did in my wardship.

WASP

60Like enough! I shall say anything, I!

3.5

[Enter] EDGWORTH [and] NIGHTINGALE, [followed by] JUSTICE [OVERDO].

JUSTICE

1[Aside] I cannot beget a project, with all my political brain, yet; my project is how to fetch off this proper young man from his debauched company. I have followed him all the Fair over, and still I find him with this songster. And I begin shrewdly to suspect their familiarity, and the young man of a terrible taint: poetry! — with which idle disease if he be infected, there’s no hope of him, in a state-course. Actum est of him for a commonwealth’s-man, if he go to’t in rhyme, once.

EDGWORTH

2[Aside to Nightingale] Yonder he is buying o’ gingerbread: set in quickly, before he part with too much on his money.

NIGHTINGALE

3[sings] My masters and friends and good people, draw near, etc.

[A crowd begins to gather.]

COKES

4Ballads! Hark, hark! (He runs to the ballad man.) Pray thee, fellow, stay a little. — Good Numps, look to the goods. — What ballads hast thou? Let me see, let me see myself.

WASP

5[To Quarlous and Winwife] Why so! He’s flown to another lime-bush; there he will flutter as long more, till he ha’ ne’er a feather left. Is there a vexation like this, gentlemen? Will you believe me now? Hereafter shall I have credit with you?

QUARLOUS

6Yes, faith, shalt thou, Numps, and thou art worthy on’t, for thou sweatest for’t.
[Aside to Winwife; Grace overhears] I never saw a young pimp-errant and his squire better matched.

WINWIFE

7Faith, the sister comes after ’em well, too.

GRACE

8Nay, if you saw the Justice her husband, my guardian, you were fitted for the mess. He is such a wise one his way —

WINWIFE

9I wonder we see him not here.

GRACE

10Oh! He is too serious for this place, and yet better sport than the other three, I assure you, gentlemen, where’er he is, though ’t be o’the Bench.

COKES

11How dost thou call it? A Caveat against Cutpurses! — a good jest, i’faith. I would fain see that demon, your cutpurse you talk of, that delicate-handed devil. They say he walks hereabout — I would see him walk now. Look you, sister, here, here, let him come, sister, and welcome. (He shows his purse boastingly.) Ballad man, does any cutpurses haunt hereabout? Pray thee, raise me one or two; begin, and show me one.

NIGHTINGALE

12Sir, this is a spell against ’em, spick-and-span new, and ’tis made as ’twere in mine own person, and I sing it in mine own defence. But ’twill cost a penny alone, if you buy it.

COKES

13No matter for the price, thou dost not know me, I see — I am an odd Barthol’mew.

MRS OVERDO

14Has’t a fine picture, brother?

COKES

15Oh, sister, do you remember the ballads o’er the nursery chimney at home o’my own pasting up: there be brave pictures! [To Nightingale] Other manner of pictures than these, friend.

WASP

16Yet these will serve to pick the pictures out o’your pockets, you shall see.

COKES

17So I heard ’em say. Pray thee, mind him not, fellow: he’ll have an oar in everything.

NIGHTINGALE

18It was intended, sir, as if a purse should chance to be cut in my presence now, I may be blameless though — as by the sequel will more plainly appear.

COKES

19We shall find that i’the matter. Pray thee, begin.

NIGHTINGALE

20To the tune of Pagington’s Pound, sir.

COKES

21[Sings] Fa, la la la, la la la, fa la la la. Nay, I’ll put thee in tune and all! Mine own country dance! Pray thee, begin.

NIGHTINGALE

22It is a gentle admonition, you must know, sir, both to the purse-cutter and the purse-bearer.

COKES

23Not a word more out o’the tune, an thou lov’st me: Fa, la la la, la la la, fa la la la. Come, when?

NIGHTINGALE

24[Sings] My masters and friends, and good people draw near,
And look to your purses, for that I do say ;

COKES

25Ha, ha, this chimes! Good counsel at first dash.

NIGHTINGALE

26And though little money in them you do bear,
It cost more to get than to lose in a day

COKES

27Good!

NIGHTINGALE

28You oft have been told,
Both the young and the old,
And bidden beware of the cutpurse so bold:

COKES

29Well said! He were to blame that would not, i’faith.

NIGHTINGALE

30Then if you take heed not, free me from the curse,
Who both give you warning, for and the cutpurse.
Youth, youth, thou hadst better been starved by thy nurse,
Than live to be hangèd for cutting a purse.

COKES

31Good, i’faith, how say you, Numps? Is there any harm i’this?

NIGHTINGALE

32It hath been upbraided to men of my trade
That oftentimes we are the cause of this crime.

COKES

33The more coxcombs they that did it, I wusse.

NIGHTINGALE

34Alack and for pity, why should it be said?
As if they regarded or places or time.
Examples have been
Of some that were seen
In Westminster Hall, yea the pleaders between.
Then why should the judges be free from this curse
More than my poor self, for cutting the purse?

COKES

35God-a-mercy for that! Why should they be more free, indeed?

NIGHTINGALE

36Youth, youth, thou hadst better been starved by thy nurse,
Than live to be hangèd for cutting a purse.

COKES

37That again, good ballad-man, that again. (He sings the burden with him [as Nightingale repeats it.] )
Oh, rare! I would fain rub mine elbow now, but I dare not pull out my hand. On, I pray thee — he that made this ballad shall be poet to my masque.

NIGHTINGALE

38At Worcester ’tis known well, and even i’the jail,
A knight of good worship did there show his face,
Against the foul sinners in zeal for to rail,
And lost (ipso facto) his purse in the place.

COKES

39Is it possible?

NIGHTINGALE

40Nay, once from the seat
Of judgement so great,
A judge there did lose a fair pouch of velvete.

COKES

41I'faith?

NIGHTINGALE

42O Lord for thy mercy, how wicked or worse,
Are those that so venture their necks for a purse!
Youth, youth, thou hadst better been starved by thy nurse,
Than live to be hangèd for cutting a purse.

COKES

43[Singing along again] Youth, youth, etc! Pray thee, stay a little, friend, yet. — O’thy conscience, Numps, speak, is there any harm i’this?

WASP

44To tell you true, ’tis too good for you, ’less you had grace to follow it.

JUSTICE

45[Aside] It doth discover enormity, I’ll mark it more: I ha’not liked a paltry piece of poetry so well a good while.

COKES

46[Singing] Youth, youth, etc! Where’s this youth now? A man must call upon him for his own good, and yet he will not appear. Look here, here’s for him.
(He shows his purse.) Handy-dandy, which hand will he have? On, I pray thee, with the rest. I do hear of him, but I cannot see him, this Master Youth, the cutpurse.

NIGHTINGALE

47At plays and at sermons, and at the sessions,
’Tis daily their practice such booty to make.
Yea, under the gallows at executions,
They stick not the stare-abouts’ purses to take.
Nay one without grace,
At a far better place,
At court and in Christmas, before the King’s face.

COKES

48That was a fine fellow! I would have him now.

NIGHTINGALE

49Alack then for pity, must I bear the curse,
That only belongs to the cunning cutpurse?

COKES

50But where’s their cunning now, when they should use it? They are all chained now, I warrant you. Youth, youth, thou hadst better, etc. The rat-catcher’s charm! — are all fools and asses to this? A pox on ’em that they will not come! That a man should have such a desire to a thing, and want it.

QUARLOUS

51[To Winwife] Fore God, I’d give half the Fair, an ’twere mine, for a cutpurse for him, to save his longing.

COKES

52Look you, sister, here, here, where is’t now? Which pocket is’t in, for a wager?

He shows his purse again.

WASP

53I beseech you leave your wagers, and let him end his matter, an’t may be.

COKES

54Oh, are you edified, Numps?

JUSTICE

55[Aside] Indeed, he does interrupt him too much. There Numps spoke to purpose.

COKES

56Sister, I am an ass! I cannot keep my purse! [He shows it(again.)] On, on, I pray thee, friend.

WINWIFE

57Will you see sport? Look, there’s a fellow gathers up to him, mark!

[As Nightingale sings the next verse,] Edgworth gets up to [Cokes], and tickles him in the ear with a straw twice to draw his hand out of his pocket [first finding a handkerchief and next the purse].

QUARLOUS

58Good, i’faith! Oh, he has lighted on the wrong pocket.

WINWIFE

59He has it. ’Fore God, he is a brave fellow; pity he should be detected.

NIGHTINGALE

60But O you vile nation of cutpurses all ,
Relent and repent, and amend and be sound,
And know that you ought not, by honest men’s fall,
Advance your own fortunes, to die above ground,
And though you go gay,
In silks as you may,
It is not the high way to heaven (as they say).
Repent then, repent you, for better, for worse,
And kiss not the gallows for cutting a purse.
Youth, youth, thou hadst better been starved by thy nurse,
Than live to be hangèd for cutting a purse.

ALL

61An excellent ballad! an excellent ballad!

EDGWORTH

62Friend, let me ha’ the first, let me ha’ the first, I pray you.

[He slips the purse and handkerchief to Nightingale.]

COKES

63Pardon me, sir: first come, first served. And I’ll buy the whole bundle, too.

WINWIFE

64[To Quarlous] That conveyance was better than all, did you see’t? He has given the purse to the ballad singer.

QUARLOUS

65Has he?

EDGWORTH

66Sir, I cry you mercy: I’ll not hinder the poor man’s profit. Pray you, mistake me not.

COKES

67Sir, I take you for an honest gentleman, if that be mistaking. I met youtoday afore — Ha! humph! O God! My purse is gone, my purse, my purse, etc.

WASP

68Come, do not make a stir and cry yourself an ass thorough the Fair afore your time.

COKES

69Why, hast thou it, Numps? Good Numps, how came you by it? I mar’l!

WASP

70I pray you, seek some other gamester to play the fool with; you may lose it time enough, for all your Fair-wit.

COKES

71By this good hand, glove and all, I ha’ lost it already, if thou hast it not — feel else — and Mistress Grace’s handkerchief, too, out o’the t’other pocket.

WASP

72Why, ’tis well, very well, exceeding pretty and well.

EDGWORTH

73Are you sure you ha’ lost it, sir?

COKES

74O God! Yes. As I am an honest man, I had it but e’en now, at ‘Youth, youth’.

NIGHTINGALE

75I hope you suspect not me, sir.

EDGWORTH

76Thee? That were a jest indeed! Dost thou think the gentleman is foolish? Where hadst thou hands, I pray thee? Away, ass, away!

[Exit Nightingale.]

JUSTICE

77[Aside, sneaking away] I shall be beaten again, if I be spied.

EDGWORTH

78Sir, I suspect an odd fellow yonder is stealing away.

MRS OVERDO

79Brother, it is the preaching fellow! You shall suspect him. He was at your t’other purse, you know! [Overdo is caught.] Nay, stay, sir, and view the work you ha’ done. An you be beneficed at the gallows and preach there, thank your own handiwork.

COKES

80Sir, you shall take no pride in your preferment; you shall be silenced quickly.

JUSTICE

81What do you mean, sweet buds of gentility?

COKES

82To ha’ my pennyworths out on you, bud. No less than two purses a day serve you? I thought you a simple fellow when my man Numps beat you i’the morning, and pitied you —

MRS OVERDO

83So did I, I’ll be sworn, brother; but now I see he is a lewd and pernicious enormity (as Master Overdo calls him).

JUSTICE

84[Aside] Mine own words turned upon me, like swords.

COKES

85Cannot a man’s purse be at quiet for you i’the master’s pocket, but you must entice it forth and debauch it?

WASP

86Sir, sir, keep your ‘debauch’ and your fine Barthol’mew-terms to yourself, and make as much on ’em as you please. But gi’ me this from you, i’the meantime: I beseech you, see if I can look to this.

[He tries to take the box from Cokes.]

COKES

87Why, Numps?

WASP

88Why? Because you are an ass, sir — there’s a reason the shortest way, an you will needs ha’ it. Now you ha’ got the trick of losing, you’d lose your breech, an ’twere loose. I know you, sir: come, deliver. (Wasp takes the licence from him.) You’ll go and crack the vermin you breed now, will you? — ’tis very fine. Will you ha’ the truth on’t? They are such reckless flies as you are, that blow cutpurses abroad in every corner; your foolish having of money makes ’em. An there were no wiser than I, sir, the trade should lie open for you, sir, it should, i’faith, sir. I would teach your wit to come to your head, sir, as well as your land to come into your hand, I assure you, sir.

WINWIFE

89Alack, good Numps.

WASP

90Nay, gentlemen, never pity me, I am not worth it. Lord send me at home once to Harrow o’the Hill again; if I travel any more, call me Coryate, with all my heart.

[Exeunt Cokes, Mistress Overdo, and Wasp, with Justice Overdo.]

QUARLOUS

91[To Edgworth] Stay, sir, I must have a word with you in private, do you hear?

EDGWORTH

92With me, sir? What’s your pleasure, good sir?

QUARLOUS

93Do not deny it: you are a cutpurse, sir. This gentleman here and I saw you — nor do we mean to detect you (though we can sufficiently inform ourselves toward the danger of concealing you), but you must do us a piece of service.

EDGWORTH

94Good gentlemen, do not undo me; I am a civil young man, and but a beginner, indeed.

QUARLOUS

95Sir, your beginning shall bring on your ending, for us. We are no catchpoles nor constables. That you are to undertake is this: you saw the old fellow with the black box here?

EDGWORTH

96The little old governor, sir?

QUARLOUS

97That same: I see you have flown him to a mark already. I would ha’ you get away that box from him, and bring it us.

EDGWORTH

98Would you ha’ the box and all, sir? Or only that, that is in’t? I’ll get you that, and leave him the box to play with still — which will be the harder o’the two — because I would gain your worships’ good opinion of me.

WINWIFE

99He says well: ’tis the greater mastery, and ’twill make the more sport when ’tis missed.

EDGWORTH

100Ay, and ’twill be the longer a-missing, to draw on the sport.

QUARLOUS

101But look you do it now, sirrah, and keep your word, or —

EDGWORTH

102Sir, if ever I break my word with a gentleman, may I never read word at my need. Where shall I find you?

QUARLOUS

103Somewhere i’the Fair hereabouts. Dispatch it quickly. [Exit Edgworth.]
I would fain see the careful fool deluded! Of all beasts, I love the serious ass: he that takes pains to be one, and plays the fool with the greatest diligence that can be.

GRACE

104Then you would not choose, sir, but love my guardian, Justice Overdo, who is answerable to that description in every hair of him.

QUARLOUS

105So I have heard. But how came you, Mistress Wellborn, to be his ward, or have relation to him at first?

GRACE

106Faith, through a common calamity: he bought me, sir; and now he will marry me to his wife’s brother, this wise gentleman that you see, or else I must pay value o’my land.

QUARLOUS

107’Slid, is there no device of disparagement, or so? Talk with some crafty fellow, some picklock o’the law! Would I had studied a year longer i’the Inns of Court, an’t had been but i’your case.

WINWIFE

108[Aside] Ay, Master Quarlous, are you proffering?

GRACE

109You’d bring but little aid, sir.

WINWIFE

110[Aside] I’ll look to you, i’faith, gamester. — An unfortunate foolish tribe you are fall’n into, lady. I wonder you can endure ’em.

GRACE

111Sir, they that cannot work their fetters off must wear ’em.

WINWIFE

112You see what care they have on you, to leave you thus.

GRACE

113Faith, the same they have of themselves, sir. I cannot greatly complain, if this were all the plea I had against ’em.

WINWIFE

114’Tis true! But will you please to withdraw with us a little, and make them think they have lost you. I hope our manners ha’ been such hitherto, and our language, as will give you no cause to doubt yourself in our company.

GRACE

115Sir, I will give myself no cause: I am so secure of mine own manners as I suspect not yours.

QUARLOUS

116Look where John Littlewit comes.

WINWIFE

117Away, I’ll not be seen by him.

QUARLOUS

118No, you were not best: he’d tell his mother, the widow.

WINWIFE

119Heart, what do you mean?

QUARLOUS

120Cry you mercy, is the wind there? Must not the widow be named?

[Exeunt Quarlous, Winwife, and Grace.]

3.6

[Enter] JOHN [and] WIN [from Ursula’s booth].

JOHN

1Do you hear, Win, Win?

WIN

2What say you, John?

JOHN

3While they are paying the reckoning, Win, I’ll tell you a thing, Win: we shall never see any sights i’the Fair, Win, except you long still, Win. Good Win, sweet Win, long to see some hobby-horses, and some drums, and rattles, and dogs, and fine devices, Win: the bull with the five legs, Win, and the great hog. Now you ha’ begun with pig, you may long for anything, Win, and so for my motion, Win.

WIN

4But we sha’ not eat o’the bull and the hog, John: how shall I long, then?

JOHN

5Oh, yes, Win! You may long to see as well as to taste, Win: how did the ’pothecary’s wife, Win, that longed to see the anatomy, Win? Or the lady, Win, that desired to spit i’the great lawyer’s mouth after an eloquent pleading? I assure you they longed, Win: good Win, go in, and long.

[John and Win return to the booth.]

TRASH

6I think we are rid of our new customer, brother Leatherhead; we shall hear no more of him.

They plot to be gone.

LEATHERHEAD

7All the better: let’s pack up all, and be gone before he find us.

TRASH

8Stay a little, yonder comes a company: it may be we may take some more money.

[Enter] KNOCKEM [and] BUSY.

KNOCKEM

9Sir, I will take your counsel, and cut my hair, and leave vapours: I see that tobacco, and bottle-ale, and pig, and Whit, and very Urs’la herself, is all vanity.

BUSY

10Only pig was not comprehended in my admonition, the rest were. For long hair, it is an ensign of pride, a banner, and the world is full of those banners, very full of banners. And bottle-ale is a drink of Satan’s, a diet-drink of Satan’s, devised to puff us up, and make us swell in this latter age of vanity, as the smoke of tobacco, to keep us in mist and error. But the fleshly woman ( which you call Urs’la) is above all to be avoided, having the marks upon her of the three enemies of man: the World, as being in the Fair; the Devil, as being in the fire; and the Flesh, as being herself.

[Enter Mistress] PURECRAFT.

PURECRAFT

11Brother Zeal-of-the-land, what shall we do? My daughter Win-the-fight is fall’n into her fit of longing again —

BUSY

12For more pig? There is no more, is there?

PURECRAFT

13— to see some sights i’the Fair.

BUSY

14Sister, let her fly the impurity of the place swiftly, lest she partake of the pitch thereof. Thou art the seat of the Beast, O Smithfield, and I will leave thee. Idolatry peepeth out on every side of thee.

KNOCKEM

15[Aside] An excellent right hypocrite! Now his belly is full, he falls a-railing and kicking, the jade. A very good vapour! I’ll in, and joy Urs’la with telling how her pig works: two and a half he ate to his share. And he has drunk a pailful. He eats with his eyes, as well as his teeth.

[Exit.]

LEATHERHEAD

16What do you lack, gentlemen? What is’t you buy? Rattles, drums, babies —

BUSY

17Peace, with thy apocryphal wares, thou profane publican: thy bells, thy dragons, and thy Toby’s dogs. Thy hobby-horse is an idol, a very idol, a fierce and rank idol, and thou the Nebuchadnezzar, the proud Nebuchadnezzar of the Fair, that sett’st it up for children to fall down to and worship.

LEATHERHEAD

18Cry you mercy, sir, will you buy a fiddle to fill up your noise?

[Enter JOHN and WIN.]

JOHN

19Look, Win, do, look, a God’s name, and save your longing. Here be fine sights.

PURECRAFT

20Ay, child. So you hate ’em, as our Brother Zeal does, you may look on ’em.

LEATHERHEAD

21Or what do you say to a drum, sir?

BUSY

22It is the broken belly of the Beast, and thy bellows there are his lungs, andthese pipes are his throat, those feathers are of his tail, and thy rattles the gnashing of his teeth.

TRASH

23And what’s my gingerbread, I pray you?

BUSY

24The provender that pricks him up. Hence with thy basket of popery, thy nest of images, and whole legend of ginger-work.

LEATHERHEAD

25Sir, if you be not quiet the quicklier, I’ll ha’ you clapped fairlyby the heels for disturbing the Fair.

BUSY

26The sin of the Fair provokes me, I cannot be silent.

PURECRAFT

27Good Brother Zeal!

LEATHERHEAD

28Sir, I’ll make you silent, believe it.

JOHN

29[Aside to Leatherhead] I’d give a shilling you could, i’faith, friend.

LEATHERHEAD

30Sir, give me your shilling — I’ll give you my shop if I do not, and I’ll leave it in pawn with you i’the meantime.

JOHN

31[Giving the money] A match, i’faith, but do it quickly then.

[Exit Leatherhead.]

BUSY

32(He speaks to the widow.) Hinder me not, woman. — I was moved in spirit, to be here this day in this Fair, this wicked and foul Fair — and fitter may it be called a Foul than a Fair — to protest against the abuses of it, the foul abuses of it, in regard of the afflicted Saints, that are troubled, very much troubled, exceedingly troubled, with the opening of the merchandise of Babylon again, and the peeping of popery upon the stalls, here, here, in the high places. See you not Goldilocks, the purple strumpet, there in her yellow gown and green sleeves? The profane pipes, the tinkling timbrels? A shop of relics!

JOHN

33[Protecting Leatherhead’s wares] Pray you, forbear: I am put in trust with ’em.

BUSY

34And this idolatrous grove of images, this flasket of idols! which I will pull down —

Overthrows the gingerbread.

TRASH

35Oh, my ware, my ware, God bless it!

BUSY

36— in my zeal, and glory to be thus exercised.

LEATHERHEAD enters with OFFICERS [led by POACHER].

LEATHERHEAD

37Here he is: pray you, lay hold on his zeal — we cannot sell a whistle, for him, in tune. Stop his noise first!

BUSY

38Thou canst not: ’tis a sanctified noise. I will make a loud and most strong noise, till I have daunted the profane enemy. And for this Cause —

LEATHERHEAD

39Sir, here’s no man afraid of you, or your Cause. You shall swear it i’the stocks, sir.

BUSY

40— I will thrust myself into the stocks, upon the pikes of the land.

LEATHERHEAD

41[To Officers] Carry him away.

PURECRAFT

42What do you mean, wicked men?

BUSY

43Let them alone; I fear them not.

[Exeunt Officers with Busy, followed by Mistress Purecraft.]

JOHN

44[To Win] Was not this shilling well ventured, Win, for our liberty? Now we may go play, and see over the Fair where we list ourselves. My mother is gone after him, and let her e’en go, and loose us.

WIN

45Yes, John, but I know not what to do.

JOHN

46For what, Win?

WIN

47For a thing I am ashamed to tell you, i’faith, and ’tis too far to go home.

JOHN

48I pray thee, be not ashamed, Win. Come, i’faith, thou shall not be ashamed. Is it anything about the hobby-horse-man? An’t be, speak freely.

WIN

49Hang him, base bob-chin, I scorn him; no, I have very great what’sha’- call’um, John.

JOHN

50Oh! Is that all, Win? We’ll go back to Captain Jordan, to the pig-woman’s, Win. He’ll help us, or she with a dripping pan, or an old kettle, or something. The poor greasy soul loves you, Win, and, after, we’ll visit the Fair all over, Win, and see my puppet play, Win — you know it’s a fine matter, Win.

[Exeunt John and Win.]

LEATHERHEAD

51Let’s away: I counselled you to pack up afore, Joan.

TRASH

52A pox of his Bedlam purity. He has spoiled half my ware: but the best is, we lose nothing, if we miss our first merchant.

LEATHERHEAD

53It shall be hard for him to find or know us when we are translated, Joan.

[Exeunt Leatherhead and Trash with their wares.]
