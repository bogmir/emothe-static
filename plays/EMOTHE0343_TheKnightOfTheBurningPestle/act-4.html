Francis Beaumont

The Knight of the Burning Pestle

← [Interlude 3] [Interlude 4] →

4.1

Enter Jasper [with a letter] and [his] Boy.

Jasper

1There, boy, deliver this. But do it well.
2Hast thou provided me four lusty fellows,
3Able to carry me? And art thou perfect
4In all thy business?

[Jasper's] Boy

In all thy business? Sir, you need not fear,
5I have my lesson here, and cannot miss it.
6The men are ready for you, and what else
7Pertains to this employment.

Jasper

Pertains to this employment. There, my boy,
8Take it, but buy no land.

[Jasper's] Boy

Take it, but buy no land. Faith, sir, 'twere rare
9To see so young a purchaser. I fly,
10And on my wings carry your destiny.

Jasper

11Go, and be happy.

Exit [Jasper's Boy].

Go, and be happy. Now, my latest hope,
12Forsake me not, but fling thy anchor out
13And let it hold! Stand fixed, thou rolling stone,
14Till I enjoy my dearest. Hear me, all
15You powers that rule in men celestial!

Exit.

Wife

Go thy ways. Thou art as crooked a sprig as ever grew in London. I warrant him he'll come to some naughty end or other, for his looks say no less. Besides, his father (you know, George) is none of the best. You heard him take me up like a flirt-gill, and sing bawdy songs upon me. But i'faith, if I live, George —

Citizen

Let me alone, sweetheart; I have a trick in my head shall lodge him in the Arches for one year, and make him sing Peccavi ere I leave him, and yet he shall never know who hurt him neither.

Wife

Do, my good George, do.

[Enter a Boy.]

Citizen

What shall we have Rafe do now, boy?

Boy

You shall have what you will, sir.

Citizen

Why so, sir, go and fetch me him, then, and let the Sophy of Persia come and christen him a child.

Boy

Believe me, sir, that will not do so well:, 'tis stale; it has been had before at the Red Bull.

Wife

George, let Rafe travel over great hills, and let him be very weary, and come to the King of Cracovia's house, covered with velvet, and there let the King's daughter stand in her window all in beaten gold, combing her golden locks with a comb of ivory, and let her spy Rafe, and fall in love with him, and come down to him, and carry him into her father's house, and then let Rafe talk with her.

Citizen

Well said, Nell, it shall be so. — Boy, let's ha't done quickly.

Boy

Sir, if you will imagine all this to be done already, you shall hear them talk together; but we cannot present a house covered with black velvet and a lady in beaten gold.

Citizen

Sir boy, let's ha't as you can, then.

Boy

Besides it will show ill-favoredly to have a grocer's prentice to court a king's daughter.

Citizen

Will it so, sir? You are well read in histories: I pray you, what was Sir Dagonet? Was not he prentice to a grocer in London? Read the play of The Four Prentices of London , where they toss their pikes so. I pray you fetch him in, sir, fetch him in.

Boy

It shall be done. — It is not our fault, gentlemen.

Exit.

Wife

Now we shall see fine doings, I warrant thee, George. Oh, here they come. How prettily the King of Cracovia's daughter is dressed.

[4.2]

Enter Rafe and the Lady [Pompiona], [Tim as] Squire and [George as] dwarf.

Citizen

Ay, Nell, it is the fashion of that country, I warrant thee.

Lady

2Welcome, Sir Knight, unto my father's court,
3King of Moldavia, unto me, Pompiona,
4His daughter dear. But sure you do not like
5Your entertainment, that will stay with us
6No longer but a night.

Rafe

No longer but a night. Damsel right fair,
7I am on many sad adventures bound,
8That call me forth into the wilderness.
9Besides, my horse's back is something galled,
10Which will enforce me ride a sober pace.
11But many thanks, fair lady, be to you
12For using errant knight with courtesy.

Lady

13But say, brave knight, what is your name and birth?

Rafe

14My name is Rafe; I am an English man,
15As true as steel, a hearty Englishman,
16And prentice to a grocer in the Strand,
17By deed indent, of which I have one part.
18But Fortune calling me to follow arms,
19On me this holy order I did take
20Of Burning Pestle, which in all men's eyes
21I bear, confounding ladies' enemies.

Lady

22Oft have I heard of your brave countrymen,
23And fertil soile and store of wholesome food.
24My father oft will tell me of a drink
25In England found, and nippitato called,
26Which driveth all the sorrow from your hearts.

Rafe

27Lady, 'tis true; you need not lay your lips
28To better nippitato than there is.

Lady

29And of a wild fowl he will often speak,
30Which powdered-beef-and-mustard callèd is;
31For there have been great wars 'twixt us and you.
32But truly, Rafe, it was not long of me.
33Tell me then, Rafe, could you contented be
34To wear a lady's favor in your shield?

Rafe

35I am a knight of religious order,
36And will not wear a favor of a lady's
37That trusts in Antichrist and false traditions.

Citizen

38Well said, Rafe. Convert her if thou canst.

Rafe

39Besides, I have a lady of my own
40In merry England, for whose virtuous sake
41I took these arms, and Susan is her name,
42A cobblers maid in Milk Street, whom I vow
43ne'er to forsake, whilst life and pestle last.

Lady Pompiona

44Happy that cobbling dame, whoe'er she be,
45That for her own, dear Rafe, hath gotten thee!
46Unhappy I, that ne'er shall see the day
47To see thee more, that bear'st my heart away.

Rafe

48Lady, farewell, I needs must take my leave.

Lady

49Hard-hearted Rafe, that ladies dost deceive!

Citizen

Hark thee, Rafe, there's money for thee. Give something in the King of Cracovia's house; be not beholding to him.

Rafe

51Lady, before I go, I must remember
52Your father's officers, who, truth to tell,
53Have been about me very diligent.
54Hold up thy snowy hand, thou princely maid,
55There's twelvepence for your father's chamberlain,
56And another shilling for his cook
57(For, by my troth, the goose was roasted well),
58And twelvepence for your father's horse-keeper
59For 'nointing my horse' back, and for his butter
60There is another shilling. To the maid
61That washed my boot-hose, there's an English groat,
62And twopence to the boy that wiped my boots.
63And last, fair lady, there is for yourself
64Threepence to buy you pins at Bumbo Fair.

Lady

65Full many thanks, and I will keep them safe
66Till all the heads be off, for thy sake, Rafe.

Rafe

67Advance, my squire and dwarf, I cannot stay.

Lady

68Thou kill'st my heart in parting thus away.

Exeunt.

Wife

I commend Rafe yet that he will not stoop to a Cracovian; there's properer women in London than any are there, iwis. But here comes Master Humphrey and his love again now, George.

Citizen

Ay, coney, peace.

[4.3]

Enter Merchant [Venturewell], Humphrey, Luce, and a Boy [in the service of the Merchant].

Merchant

1Go get you up. I will not be entreated.
2And gossip mine, I'll keep you sure hereafter
3From gadding out again with boys and unthrifts.
4Come, they are womens' tears, I know your fashion. —
5Go, sirrah, lock her in, and keep the key,
6Safe as you love your life.

Exit Luce and Boy.

Safe as you love your life. Now, my son Humphrey,
7You may both rest assurèd of my love
8In this, and reap your own desire.

Humphrey

9I see this love you speak of, through your daughter,
10Although the hole be little; and hereafter
11Will yield the like in all I may, or can,
12Fitting a Christian and a gentleman.

Merchant

13I do believe you, my good son, and thank you;
14For 'twere an impudence to think you flattered.

Humphrey

15It were indeed. But shall I tell you why?
16I have been beaten twice about the lie.

Merchant

17Well, son, no more of compliment: my daughter
18Is yours again. Appoint the time, and take her.
19We'll have no stealing for it. I myself
20And some few of our friends will see you married.

Humphrey

21I would you would, i'faith, for be it known
22I ever was afraid to lie alone.

Merchant

23Some three days hence, then.

Humphrey

24Three days! Let me see:
25'Tis somewhat of the most, yet I agree,
26Because I mean, against the appointed day,
27To visit all my friends in new array.

Enter Servant.

Servant

Sir, there's a gentlewoman without would speak with Your Worship.

Merchant

What is she?

Servant

Sir, I asked her not.

Merchant

Bid her come in.

Enter Mistress Merrythought and Michael.

Mistress Merrythought

Peace be to Your Worship. I come as a poor suitor to you, sir, in the behalf of this child.

Merchant

Are you not wife to Merrythought?

Mistress Merrythought

Yes, truly, would I had ne'er seen his eyes! Ha has undone me and himself and his children, and there he lives at home and sings and hoits and revels among his drunken coompanions. But, I warrant you, where to get a penny to put bread in his mouth, he knows not. And therefore, if it like Your Worship, I would entreat your letter, to the honest host of the Bel in Waltham, that I may place my child under the protection of his tapster, in some settled course of life.

Merchant

35I'm glad the heavens have heard my prayers. — Thy husband,
36When I was ripe in sorrows, laughed at me.
37Thy son, like an unthankful wretch, I having
38Redeemed him from his fall and made him mine,
39To show his love again, first stole my daughter,
40Then wronged this gentleman, and last of all,
41Gave me that grief had almost brought me down
42Unto my grave, had not a stronger hand
43Relieved my sorrows. Go and weep, as I did,
44And be unpitied, for I here profess
45An everlasting hate to all thy name.

Mistress Merrythought

Will you so, sir? How say you by that? — Come, Mick, let him keep his wind to cool his porridge. We'le go to thy nurse's, Mick; she knits silk stockings, boy, and we'll knit too, boy, and be beholding to none of them all.

Exeunt Michael and mother.

Enter [Jasper's] Boy with a letter.

[Jasper's] Boy

47Sir, I take it you are the master of this house.

Merchant

48How then, boy?

[Jasper's] Boy

49Then to yourself, sir, comes this letter.

Merchant

50From whom, my pretty boy?

[Jasper's] Boy

51From him that was your servant, but no more
52Shall that name ever be, for he is dead.
53Grief of your purchased anger broke his heart.
54I saw him die, and from his hand recieved
55This paper, with a charge to bring it hither.
56Read it, and satisfr yourself in all.

Letter.

Merchant

[Reads.] Sir, that I have wronged your love I must confess, in which I have purchased to myself, besides mine own undoing, the ill opinion of my friends. Let not your anger, good sir, outlive me, but suffer me to rest in peace with your forgiveness. Let my body, if a dying man may so much prevail with you, be brought to your daughter, that she may truly know my hot flames are now buried, and withal receive a testimony of the zeal I bore her virtue. Farewell for ever, and be ever happy. Jasper.

2God's hand is great in this! I do forgive him,
3Yet I am glad he's quiet, where I hope
4He will not bite again. — Boy, bring the body
5And let him have his will, if that be all.

[Jasper's] Boy

6'Tis here without, sir.

Merchant

'Tis here without, sir. So, sir, if you please
7You may conduct it in. I do not fear it.

Humphrey

8I'll be your usher boy, for, though I say it,
9He owed me something once, and well did pay it.

Exeunt.

Enter Luce alone.

Luce

10If there be any punishment inflicted
11Upon the miserable more than yet I feel,
12Let it together seiz me, and at once
13Press down my soule. I cannot bear the pain
14Of these delaying tortures. — Thou that art
15The end of all and the sweet rest of all,
16Come, come, O Death! Bring me to thy peace,
17And blot out all the memory I nourish
18Both of my father and my cruel friend. —
19O wretched maid, still living to be wretched,
20To be a say to Fortune in her changes
21And grow to number times and woes together,
22How happy had I been, if being born
23My grave had been my cradle!

Enter Servant.

Servant

My grave had been my cradle! By your leave,
24Young mistress, here's a boy hath brought a coffin.
25What 'a would say, I know not; but your father
26Charged me to give you notice. Here they come.

Enter two[, Jasper's Boy and a carrier,] bearing a coffin, Jasper in it.

Luce

27For me, I hope, 'tis come; and 'tis most welcome.

[Jasper's] Boy

28Fair mistress, let me not add greater grief
29To that great store you have already. Jasper
30(That whilst he lived was yours, now dead,
31And here enclosed) commanded me to bring
32His body hither, and to crave a tear
33From those fair eyes, though he deserve not pity,
34To deck his funeral, for so he bid me
35Tell her for whom he died.

Luce

Tell her for whom he died. He shall have many:
36Good friends, depart a little whilst I take
37My leave of this dead man that once I loved.

(Exeunt coffin-carrier and [Jasper's] Boy [and Servant].)

38Hold, yet a little, life, and then I give thee
39To thy first heavenly being. O my friend!
40Hast thou deceived me thus and got before me?
41I shall not long be after, but believe me,
42Thou wert too cruel, Jasper, 'gainst thyself,
43In punishing the fault I could have pardoned,
44With so untimely death, Thou didst not wrong me,
45But ever wert most kind, most true, most loving;
46And I the most unkind, most false, most cruel.
47Didst thou but ask a tear? I'll give thee all,
48Even all my eyes can pour down, all my sighs
49And all myself. Before thou goest from me
50There are but sparing rites. But if thy soul
51Be yet about this place and can behold
52And see what I prepare to deck thee with,
53It shall go up, born on the wings of peace
54And satisfied: first will I sing thy dirge,
55Then kiss thy pale lips, and then die myself,
56And fill one coffin and one grave together.

Song

57Come you whose loves are dead,
58And, whiles I sing,
59Weep, and wring
60Every hand and every head,
61Bind with cypress and sad yew,
62Ribbons black and candles blue,
63For him that was of men most true.
64Come with heavy moaning,
65And on his grave
66Let him have
67Sacrifice of sighs and groaning.
68Let him have fair flowers enow,
69White and purple, green and yellow,
70For him that was of men most true.
71Thou sable cloth, sad cover of my joys,
72I lift thee up, and thus I meet with death.

[Jasper rises out of the coffin.]

Jasper

73And thus you meet the living.

Luce

74Save me, heaven!

Jasper

75Nay, do not fly me, fair, I am no spirit.
76Look better on me. Do you know me yet?

Luce

77O thou dear shadow of my friend!

Jasper

O thou dear shadow of my friend! Dear substance,
78I swear I am no shadow. Feel my hand:
79It is the same it was. I am your Jasper,
80Your Jasper that's yet living, and yet loving.
81Pardon my rash attempt, my foolish proof
82I put in practice of your constancy;
83For sooner should my sword have drunk my blood
84And set my soul at liberty than drawn
85The least drop from that body. For which boldness
86Doom me to anything: if death, I take it
87And willingly.

Luce

And willingly. This death I'll give you for it.

[Kisses him.]

88So, now I am satisfied: you are no spirit,
89But my own truest, truest, truest friend.
90Why do you come thus to me?

Jasper

Why do you come thus to me? First to see you,
91Then to convey you hence.

Luce

Then to convey you hence. It cannot be,
92For I am locked up here and watched at all hours,
93That 'tis impossible for me to scape.

Jasper

94Nothing more possible. Within this coffin
95Do you convey yourself. Let me alone.
96I have the wits of twenty men about me.
97Only I crave the shelter of your closet
98A little, and then fear me not. Creep in
99That they may presently convey you hence.
100Fear nothing, dearest love. I'll be your second.

[She gets in the coffin.]

101Lie close, so. All goes well yet. — Boy!

[Enter Jasper's Boy and coffin-carrier.]

[Jasper's] Boy

102At hand, sir.

Jasper

103Convey away the coffin, and be wary.

[Jasper's] Boy

104'Tis done already.

Jasper

'Tis done already. Now must I go conjure.

Exit.

[Jasper's Boy and the coffin-carrier start to leave.]

Enter Merchant [Venturewell].

Merchant

Boy! Boy!

[Jasper's] Boy

Your servant, sir.

Merchant

Do me this kindness, boy — hold here's a crown—: before thou bury the body of this fellow, carry it to his old merry father, and salute him from me, and bid him sing; he hath cause.

[Jasper's] Boy

I will, sir.

Merchant

And then bring me word what tune he is in, and have another crown. But do it truly. I have fitted him a bargain, now, will vex him.

[Jasper's] Boy

God bless Your Worship's health, sir.

Merchant

Farewell, boy.

Exeunt [the Boy and the coffin-carriers in one direction, the Merchant in another.]

[4.4]

Enter Master Merrythought.

Wife

Ah, old Merrythought, art thou there again? Let's hear some of thy songs.

Old Merrythought

2[Sings.] Who can sing a merrier note,
3Then he that cannot change a groat?

Not a denier left, and yet my heart leaps. I do wonder yet, as old as I am, that any man will follow a trade, or serve, that may sing and laugh, and walk the streets. My wife and both my sons are I know not where. I have nothing left, nor know I how to come by meat to supper; yet am I merry still, for I know I shall find it upon the table at six o'clock. Therefore, hang thought!

[Sings.]

5I would not be a servigman
6To carry the cloak-bag still,
7Nor would I be a falconer
8The greedy hawks to fill.
9But I would be in a good house,
10And have a good master too.
11But I would eat and drink of the best,
12And no work would I do.

This is it that keeps life and soul together: mirth. This is the philosopher's stone that they write so much on, that keeps a man ever young.

Enter a Boy.

1 Boy

Sir, they say they know all your money is gone, and they will trust you for no more drink.

Old Merrythought

Will they not? Let 'em choose. The best is I have mirth at home, and need not send abroad for that. Let them keep their drink to themselves.

[Sings.]

16For Jillian of Berry she dwells on a hill,
17And she hath good beer and ale to sell.
18And of good fellows she thinks no ill,
19And thither will we go now, now, now, now,
20And when you have made a little stay,
21You need not ask what is to pay,
22But kiss your hostess and go your way,
23And thither, etc.

Enter another Boy.

2 Boy

Sir, I can get no bread for supper.

Old Merrythought

Hang bread and supper! Let's preserve our mirth, and we shall never feel hunger, I'll warrant you. Let's have a catch. Boy, follow me, come.

Merrythought, 1 Boy, 2 Boy

26(Sing this catch.) Ho, ho, nobody at home.
27Meat, nor drink, nor money ha' we none,
28Fill the pot, Eedy,
29Never more need I.

Old Merrythought

So, boys, enough, follow me, let's change our place and we shall laugh afresh.

Exeunt.
