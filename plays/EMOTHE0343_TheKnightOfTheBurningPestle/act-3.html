The Knight of the Burning Pestle

3.1

Enter Jasper and Luce.

Jasper

1Come, my dear. Though we have lost our way,
2We have not lost ourselves. Are you not weary
3With this night's wand'ring, broken from your rest,
4And frighted with the terror that attends
5The darkness of these wild unpeopled place?

Luce

6No, my best friend, I cannot either fear
7Or entertain a weary thought whilst you,
8The end of all my full desires, stand by me.
9Let them that lose their hopes, and live to languish
10Amongst the number of forsaken lovers,
11Tell the long weary steps, and number time,
12Start at a shadow, and shrink up their blood,
13Whilst I, possessed with all content and quiet,
14Thus take my pretty love, and thus embrace him.

Jasper

15You have caught me, Luce, so fast, that whilst I live
16I shall become your faithfull prisoner,
17And wear these chains for ever. Come, sit down,
18And rest your body, too too delicate
19For these disturbances. So, will you sleep?
20Come, do not be more able than you are.
21I know you are not skillful in these watches,
22For women are no soldiers. Be not nice,
23But take it. Sleep, I say.

Luce

But take it. Sleep, I say. I cannot sleep.
24Indeed I cannot, friend.

Jasper

Indeed I cannot, friend. Why, then we'll sing,
25And try how that will work upon our senses.

Luce

26I'll sing, or say, or anything but sleep.

Jasper

27Come, little mermaid, rob me of my heart
28With that enchanting voice.

Luce

With that enchanting voice. You mock me, Jasper.

Jasper

29Tell me, dearest, what is love?

Luce

30'Tis a lightning from above,
31'Tis an arrow, 'tis a fire,
32'Tis a boy they call desire.
33'Tis a smile
34Doth beguile

Jasper

35The poor hearts of men that prove.
36Tell me more, are women true?

Luce

37Some love change, and so do you.

Jasper

38Are they fair, and never kind?

Luce

39Yes, when men turn with the wind.

Jasper

40Are they froward?

Luce

41Ever toward
42Those that love, to love anew.

Jasper

43Dissemble it no more. I see the god
44Of heavy sleep lay on his heavy mace
45Upon your eyelids.

Luce

Upon your eyelids. I am very heavy.

Jasper

46Sleep, sleep, and quiet rest crown thy sweet thoughts.
47Keep from her fair blood distempers, startings,
48Horrors, and fearful shapes. Let all her dreams
49Be joys, and chaste delights, embraces, wishes,
50And such new pleasures, as the ravished soul
51Gives to the senses. So, my charms have took.
52Keep her, you powers divine, whilst I contemplate
53Upon the wealth and beauty of her mind.
54She is only fair and constant, only kind,
55And only to thee, Jasper. Oh, my joys!
56Whither will you transport me? Let not fulness
57Of my poor buried hopes come up together
58And overcharge my spirits. I am weak.
59Some say, however ill, the sea and women
60Are governed by the moon: both ebb and flow,
61Both full of changes. Yet to them that know
62And truly judge, these but opinions are,
63And heresies to bring on pleasing war
64Between our tempers, that without these were
65Both void of after-love and present fear,
66Which are the best of Cupid. O thou child
67Bred from dispair, I dare not entertain thee,
68Having a love without the faults of women,
69And greater in her perfect goods than men!
70Which to make good and please myself the stronger,
71Though certainly I am certain of her love,
72I'll try her, that the world and memory
73May sing to aftertimes her constancy. —

[Draws his sword.]

74Luce, Luce, awake!

Luce

Luce, Luce, awake! Why do you fright me, friend,
75With those distempered looks? What makes your sword
76Drawn in your hand? who hath offended you?
77I prithee, Jasper, sleep. Thou art wild with watching.

Jasper

78Come, make your way to heaven, and bid the world,
79With all the villanies that stick upon it,
80Farewell: you're for another life.

Luce

Farewell: you're for another life. Oh, Jasper!
81How have my tender years committed evil,
82Especially against the man I love,
83Thus to be cropped untimely?

Jasper

Thus to be cropped untimely? Foolish girl,
84Canst thou imagine I could love his daughter
85That flung me from my fortune into nothing,
86Dischargèd me his service, shut the doors
87Upon my poverty, and scorned my prayers,
88Sending me, like a boat without a mast,
89To sink or swim? Come, by this hand you die.
90I must have life and blood to satisfy
91Your father's wrongs.

Wife

Away, George, away, raise the watch at Ludgate and bring a mittimus from the justice for this desperate villain. — Now, I charge you, gentlemen, see the King's peace kept. — Oh, my heart, what a varlet's this, to offer manslaughter upon the harmeless gentlewoman!

Citizen

I warrant thee, sweetheart, we'll have him hampered.

Luce

94Oh, Jasper! Be not cruel;
95If thou wilt kill me, smile and do it quickly,
96And let not many deaths appear before me.
97I am a woman made of fear and love,
98A weak, weak woman. Kill not with thy eyes;
99They shoot me through and through. Strike, I am ready.
100And, dying, still I love thee.

Enter Merchant [Venturewell], Humphrey, and his men.

Merchant

And, dying, still I love thee. Whereabouts?

Jasper

101No more of this; now to myself again.

Humphrey

102There, there he stands with sword, like martial knight,
103Drawn in his hand. Therefore beware the fight,
104You that be wise, for were I good Sir Bevis,
105I would not stay his coming, by your leaves.

Merchant

106Sirrah, restore my daughter.

Jasper

Sirrah, restore my daughter. Sirrah, no.

Merchant

107Upon him then!

Wife

So, down with him, down with him, down with him; cut him i'th'leg, boys, cut him i'th'leg.

Merchant

109Come your ways, minion. I'll provide a cage
110For you; you're grown so tame. — Horse her away.

Humphrey

111Truly I'm glad your forces have the day.

Exeunt. Manet Jasper.

Jasper

112They are gone, and I am hurt. My love is lost,
113Never to get again. O me, unhappy!
114Bleed, bleed, and die. I cannot. Oh, my folly,
115Thou hast betrayed me! Hope, where art thou fled?
116Tell me, if thou be'st anywhere remaining.
117Shall I but see my love again? Oh, no!
118She will not deign to look upon her butcher,
119Nor is it fit she should; yet I must venture.
120O Chance, or Fortune, or whate'er thou art
121That men adore for powerful, hear my cry,
122And let me loving live, or losing die!

Exit.

Wife

Is 'a gone, George?

Citizen

Ay, coney.

Wife

Marry, and let him go, sweetheart. By the faith o'my body a has put me into such a fright that I tremble (as they say) as 'twere an aspen leaf: looke o'my little finger, George, how it shakes. Now, i'truth, every member of my body is the worse for't.

Citizen

Come, hug in mine arms, sweet mouse. He shall not fright thee any more. Alas, mine own dear heart, how it quivers.

[3.2]

Enter Mistress Merrythought, Rafe, Michael, [Tim as] squire, [George as] dwarf, Host, and a Tapster.

Wife

Oh, Rafe, how dost thou Rafe? How hast thou slept tonight? Has the knight used thee well?

Citizen

2Peace, Nell, let Rafe alone.

Tapster

3Master, the reckoning is not paid.

Rafe

4Right courteous knight (who for the order's sake
5Which thou hast ta'en, hang'st out the holy bell,
6As I this flaming pestle bear about),
7We render thanks to your puissant self,
8Your beauteous lady, and your gentle squires,
9For thus refreshing of our wearied limbs,
10Stiffened with hard achievements in wild desert.

Tapster

11Sir, there is twelve shillings to pay.

Rafe

12Thou merry Squire Tapstero, thanks to thee
13For comforting our souls with double jug;
14And if advent'rous Fortune prick thee forth,
15Thou jovial squire, to follow feats of arms,
16Take heed thou tender every lady's cause,
17Every true knight, and every damsel fair;
18But spill the blood of treacherous Saracens,
19And false enchanters that with magick spells
20Have done to death full many a noble knight.

Host

Thou valiant Knight of the Burning Pestle, give ear to me: there is twelve shillings to pay, and as I am a true knight, I will not bate a penny.

Wife

George, I pray thee tell me, must Rafe pay twelve shillings now?

Citizen

No, Nell, no, nothing but the old knight is merry with Rafe.

Wife

Oh, is't nothing else? Rafe will be as merry as he.

Rafe

25Sir Knight, this mirth of yours becomes you well,
26But to requite this liberal courtesy,
27If any of your squires will follow arms,
28He shall receive from my heroic hand
29A knighthood, by the virtue of this pestle.

Host

30Fair knight I thank you for noble offer.
31Therefore, gentle knight,
32Twelve shillings you must pay, or I must cap you.

Wife

Look, George, did not I tell thee as much? The Knight of the Bell is in earnest. Rafe shall not be beholding to him. Give him his money, George, and let him go snick up.

Citizen

Cap Rafe? No. — Hold your hand, Sir Knight of the Bell, there's your money. Have you anything to say to Rafe now? Cap Rafe?

Wife

I would you should know it, Rafe has friends that will not suffer him to be capped for ten times so much, and ten times to the end of that. — Now take thy course, Rafe.

Mistress Merrythought

Come, Michael, thou and I will go home to thy father. He hath enough left to keep us a day or two, and we'll set fellows abroad to cry our purse and our casket. Shall we, Michael?

Michael

Ay, I pray, mother. In truth my feet are full of chilblains with traveling.

Wife

Faith, and those chilblains are a foul trouble, Mistress Merrythought. When your youth comes home, let him rub all the soles of his feet, and the heels, and his ankles, with a mouse skin; or if none of your people can catch a mouse, when he goes to bed, let him roll his feet in the warm embers, and, I warrant you, he shall be well, and you may make him put his fingers between his toes and smell to them; it's very sovereign for his head, if he be costive.

Mistress Merrythought

Master Knight of the Burning Pestle, my son Michael and I bid you farewell. I thank Your Worship heartily for your kindness.

Rafe

40Farewell, fair lady, and your tender squire.
41If, pricking through these deserts, I do hear
42Of any traitorous knight who through his guile
43Hath light upon your casket and your purse,
44I will despoil him of them and restore them.

Mistress Merrythought

45I thank Your Worship.

Exit with Michael.

Rafe

46Dwarf, bear my shield. Squire, elevate my lance.
47And now farewell, you Knight of Holy Bell.

Citizen

48Ay, ay, Rafe, all is paid.

Rafe

49But yet before I go, speak worthy knight,
50If aught you do of sad adventures know,
51Where errant knights may through his prowess win
52Eternal fame, and free some gentle souls
53From endless bonds of steel and ling'ring pain.

Host

Sirrah, go to Nick the Barber, and bid him prepare himself, as I told you before, quickly.

Tapster

I am gone, sir.

Exit Tapster.

Host

56Sir Knight, this wilderness affordeth none
57But the great venture, where full many a knight
58Hath tried his prowess and come off with shame,
59And where I would not have you lose your life
60Against no man but furious fiend of hell.

Rafe

61Speak on, Sir Knight. Tell what he is, and where.
62For here I vow, upon my blazing badge,
63Never to blaze a day in quietness,
64But bread and water will I only eat,
65And the green herb and rock shall be my couch,
66Till I have quelled that man, or beast, or fiend
67That works such damage to all errant knights.

Host

68Not far from hence, near to a craggy cliff,
69At the north end of this distressèd town,
70There doth stand a lowly house
71Ruggedly builded, and in it a cave
72In which an ugly giant now doth won,
73Yclepèd Barbaroso. In his hand
74He shakes a naked lance of purest steel,
75With sleeves turned up, and him before he wears
76A motley garment to preserve his clothes
77From blood of those knights which he massacres,
78And ladies gent. Without his door doth hang
79A copper basin, on a prickant spear;
80At which, no sooner gentle knights can knock,
81But the shrill sound fierce Barbaroso hears,
82And, rushing forth, brings in the errant knight
83And sets him down in an enchanted chair.
84Then with an engine which he hath prepared
85With forty teeth, he claws his courtly crown,
86Next makes him wink, and underneath his chin
87He plants a brazen piece of mighty bord,
88And knocks his bullets round about his cheeks,
89Whilst with his fingers, and an instrument
90With which he snaps his hair off, he doth fill
91The wretch's ears with a most hideous noise.
92Thus every knight adventurer he doth trim,
93And now no creature dares encounter him.

Rafe

94In God's name, I will fight him. Kind sir,
95Go but before me to this dismal cave
96Where this huge giant Barbaroso dwells,
97And by that virtue that brave Rosicleer,
98That damnèd brood of ugly giants slew,
99And Palmerin Frannarco overthrew,
100I doubt not but to curb this traitor foul,
101And to the devil send his guilty soul.

Host

102Brave-sprighted knight, thus far I will perform
103This your request: I'll bring you with in sight
104Of this most loathsome place, inhabited
105By a more loathsome man; but dare not stay,
106For his main force swoops all he sees away.

Rafe

107Saint George set on before! March, squire and page.

Exeunt.

Wife

George, dost think Rafe will confound the giant?

Citizen

I hold my cap to a farthing he does. Why, Nell, I saw him wrestle with the great Dutchman and hurl him.

Wife

Faith, and that Ducthman was a goodly man, if all things were answerable to his bigness. And yet they say there was a Scotchman higher than he, and that they two and a knight met and saw one another for nothing, but of all the sights that ever were in London, since I was married, methinks the little child that was so fair grown about the members was the prettiest — that and the hermophrodite.

Citizen

Nay, by your leave, Nell, Ninivie was better.

Wife

Ninivie? Oh, that was the story of Joan and the wall, was it not, George?

Citizen

Yes, lamb.

[3.3]

Enter Mistress Merrythought.

Wife

Look, George, here comes Mistress Merrythought again. And I would have Rafe come and fight with the giant. I tell you true, I long to see't.

Citizen

Good Mistress Merrythought, be gone, I pray you, for my sake. I pray you, forbear a little. You shall have audience presently. I have a little business.

Wife

Mistress Merrythought, if it please you to refrain your passion a little till Rafe have dispatched the giant out of the way, we shall think ourselves much bound to you. I thank you, good Mistress Merrythought.

Exit Mistress Merrythought.

Enter a Boy.

Citizen

Boy, come hither. Send away Rafe and this whoreson giant quickly.

Boy

In good faith, sir, we cannot. You'll utterly spoil our play, and make it to be hissed, and it cost money. You will not suffer us to go on with our plot. — I pray, gentlemen, rule him.

Citizen

Let him come now and dispatch this, and I'll trouble you no more.

Boy

Will you give me your hand of that?

Wife

Give him thy hand, George, do, and I'll kiss him. I warrant thee, the youth means plainly.

Boy

I'll send him to you presently.

Wife

[She kisses him.] I thank you, little youth. Exit Boy. — Faith, the child hath a sweet breath, George, but I think it be troubled with the worms. Carduus Benedictus and mare's milk were the only thing in the world for't. Oh, Rafe's here, George. — God send thee good luck, Rafe.

[3.4]

Enter Rafe, Host, [Tim as] squire, and [George as] dwarf. [A barber’s basin and spear and a string of teeth are displayed.]

Host

1Puissant knight, yonder his mansion is.
2Lo where the spear and copper basin are.
3Behold that string on which hangs many a tooth,
4Drawn from the gentle jaw of wand'ring knights.
5I dare not stay to sound. He will appear.

Exit Host.

Rafe

6Oh, faint not, heart! Susan, my lady dear,
7The cobblers maid in Milk Street, for whose sake
8I take these arms, oh, let the thought of thee
9Carry thy knight through all adventurous deeds,
10And in the honor of thy beauteous self
11May I destroy this monster Barbaroso. —
12Knock, squire, upon the basin till it break.
13With the shrill strokes, or till the giant speak.

[Tim, as squire, knocks on the barber’s basin.]

Enter Barber.

Wife

14Oh, George, the giant, the giant! — Now, Rafe, for thy life!

Barber

15What fond unknowing wight is this that dares
16So rudely knock at Barbarossa's cell,
17Where no man comes but leaves his fleece behind?

Rafe

18Ay, traitorous caitiff, who am sent by Fate
19To punish all the sad enormities
20Thou hast committed against ladies gent
21And errant knights. Traitor to God and men,
22Prepare thyself. This is the dismal hour
23Appointed for thee to give strict account
24Of all thy beastly treacherous villainies.

Barber

25Foolhardy knight, full soon thou shalt aby
26This fond reproach. Thy body will I bang,

(He takes down his pole.)

27And lo, upon that string thy teeth shall hang.
28Prepare thyself, for dead soon shalt thou be.

Rafe

29Saint George for me!

Barber

Saint George for me! Gargantua for me!

They fight.

Wife

To him, Rafe to him! Hold up the giant! Set out thy leg before, Rafe!

Citizen

Falsify a blow, Rafe, falsif, a blow. The Giant lies open on the left side.

Wife

Bear't off, bear't off still! There, boy! — Oh, Rafe's almost down, Rafe's almost down.

Rafe

Susan inspire me! Now have up again.

Wife

Up, up, up, up, up! So, Rafe, down with him, down with him, Rafe.

Citizen

Fetch him o'er the hip, boy!

[Rafe knocks the barber down.]

Wife

There, boy, kill, kill, kill, kill, kill, Rafe!

Citizen

No, Rafe get all out of him first.

Rafe

38Presumptuous man, see to what desperate end
39Thy treachery hath brought thee. The just gods,
40Who never prosper those that do despise them,
41For all the villainies which thou hast done
42To knights and ladies, now have paid thee home
43By my stiff arm, a knight adventurous.
44But say, vile wretch, before I send thy soul
45To sad Avernus, whither it must go,
46What captives hold'st thou in thy sable cave?

Barber

47Go in and free them all. Thou hast the day.

Rafe

48Go, squire and dwarf, search in this dreadful cave
49And free the wretched prisoners from their bonds.

Exit [Tim as] squire and [George as] dwarf.

Barber

50I crave for mercy, as thou art a knight
51And scorn'st to spill the blood of those that beg.

Rafe

52Thou showed'st no mercy, nor shalt thou have any.
53Prepare thyself, for thou shalt surely die.

Enter [Tim as] squire leading one [the 1 Knight] winking, with a basin under his chin.

[Tim as] Squire

54Behold, brave knight, here is one prisoner,
55Whom this wild man hath usèd as you see.

Wife

56This is the first wise word I heard the squire speak.

Rafe

57Speak what thou art, and how thou hast been used,
58That that I may give condign punishment.

1 Knight

59I am a knight that took my journey post
60Northward from London, and in courteous wise
61This giant trained me to his loathsome den,
62Under pretense of killing of the itch,
63And all my body with a powder strewed
64That smarts and stings, and cut away my beard
65And my curled locks wherein were ribbons tied,
66And with a water washed my tender eyes
67(Whilst up and down about me still he skipped),
68Whose virtue is that, till mine eyes be wiped
69With a dry cloth, for this my foul disgrace
70I shall not dare to look a dog i'th'face.

Wife

Alas, poor knight. — Relieve him, Rafe; relieve poor knights whilst you live.

Rafe

72My trusty squire, convey him to the town,
73Where he may finderelief. Adieu, fair knight.

Exit [the 1] Knight [with Tim, who then reenters].

Enter [Geroge as] dwarf leading one [the 2 Knight] with a patch o'er his nose.

[George as] Dwarf

74Puissant knight, of the Burning Pestle hight,
75See here another wretch, whom this foul beast
76Hath scorched and scored in this inhumane wise.

Rafe

77Speak me thy name, and eke thy place of birth,
78And what hath been thy usage in this cave.

2 Knight

79I am a knight, Sir Pockhole is my name,
80And by my birth I am a Londoner,
81Free by my copy, but my ancestors
82Were Frenchmen all; and riding hard this way,
83Upon a trotting horse, my bones did ache,
84And I, faint knight, to ease my weary limbs,
85Light at this cave, when straight this furious fiend,
86With sharpest instrument of purest steel,
87Did cut the gristle of my nose away,
88And in the place this velvet plaster stands.
89Relieve me, gentle knight, out of his hands.

Wife

Good Rafe, relieve Sir Pockhole and send him away, for in truth his breath stinks.

Rafe

91Convey him straight after the other knight. —
92Sir Pockhole, fare you well.

2 Knight

93Kind sir, good night.

Exit [with George, who then reenters].

Cries within.

3 Knight

[within] Deliver us!

Woman

[within] Deliver us!

Wife

Hark, George, what a woeful cry there is! I think some woman lies in there.

3 Knight

[within] Deliver us!

Woman

[within] Deliver us!

Rafe

99What gastly noise is this? Speak, Barbaroso,
100Or, by this blazing steel, thy head goes off.

Barber

101Prisoners of mine, whom I in diet keep.
102Send lower down into the cave,
103And in a tub that's heated smoking hot
104There may they find them and deliver them.

Rafe

105Run, squire and dwarf, deliver them with speed.

Exeunt [Tim as] squire and [George as] dwarf.

Wife

But will not Rafe kill this giant? Surely I am afeard, if he let him go, he will do as much hurt as ever he did.

Citizen

Not so, mouse, neither, if he could convert him.

Wife

Ay, George, if he could convert him. But a giant is not so soon converted as one of us ordinary people. There's a pretty tale of a witch, that had the devil's mark about her — God bless us —, that had a giant to her son that was called Lob-lie-by-the-fire. Didst never hear it, George?

Enter [Tim as] squire, leading a Man [the 3 Knight] with a glass of lotion in his hand, and [George as] the dwarf, leading a Woman with diet bread and drink.

Citizen

109Peace, Nell, here comes the prisoners.

[George as] Dwarf

110Here be these pinèd wretches, manful knight,
111That for these six weeks have not seen a wight.

Rafe

112Deliver what you are, and how you came
113To this sad cave, and what your usage was.

3 Knight

114I am an errant knight that followed arms,
115With spear and shield, and in my tender years
116I stricken was with Cupid's fiery shaft,
117And fell in love with this my lady dear,
118And stole her from her friends in Turnbull Street,
119And bore her up and down from town to town,
120Where we did eat and drink and musick hear,
121Till at the length at this unhappy town
122We did arrive, and, coming to this cave,
123This beast us caught and put us in a tub,
124Where we this two months sweat, and should have done
125Another month if you had not relieved us.

Woman

126This bread and water hath our diet been,
127Together with a rib cut from a neck
128Of burnèd mutton. Hard hath been our fare.
129Release us from this ugly giant's snare.

3 Knight

130This hath been all the food we have received;
131But only twice a day, for novelty,
132He gave a spoonful of this hearty broth,

(Pulls out a syringe.)

133To each of us, through this same slender quill.

Rafe

134From this infernal monster you shall go,
135That useth knights and gentle ladies so. —
136Convey them hence.

Exeunt Man [the 3 Knight] and Woman [with Tim and George, who then reenter].

Citizen

Coney, I can tell thee the gentlemen like Rafe.

Wife

Ay, George, I see it well enough. — Gentlemen, I thank you all heartily for gracing my man Rafe, and I promise you you shall see him oft'ner.

Barber

139Mercy, great knight, I do recant my ill,
140And henceforth never gentle blood will spill.

Rafe

141I give thee mercy, but yet shalt thou swear
142Upon my Burning Pestle to perform
143Thy promise uttered.

Barber

144I swear and kisse

Rafe

I swear and kisse Depart, then, and amend.
145Come, squire and dwarf, the sun grows towards his set,
146And we have many more adventures yet.

Exeunt.

Citizen

Now Rafe is in this humor, I know he would ha' beaten all the boys in the house if they had been set on him.

Wife

Ay, George, but it is well as it is. I warrant you the gentlemen do consider what it is to overthrow a giant. But look, George, here comes Mistress Merrythought and her son Michael. — Now you are welcome, Mistress Merrythought. Now Rafe has done, you may go on.

[3.5]

Enter Mistress Merrythought and Michael.

Mistress Merrythought

Mick, my boy.

Michael

Ay, forsooth, mother.

Mistress Merrythought

Be merry, Mick. We are at home now, where, I warrant you, you shall find the house flung out at the windowes. [Music and sounds of merriment within] Hark. Hey, dogs, hey! This is the old world, i'faith, with my husband. If I get in among 'em, I'll play em such a lesson that they shall have little list to come scraping hither again. — Why, Master Merrythought! Husband! Charles Merrythought!

Old Merrythought

5([Sings] within.) If you will sing, and dance, and laugh,
6And halloo, and laugh again,
7And then cry "There, boys, there!" why, then,
8One, two, three, and four,
9We shall be merry within this hour.

Mistress Merrythought

Why, Charles, do you not know your own natural wife? I say, open the door, and turn me out those mangy companions. 'Tis more than time that they were fellow and fellowlike with you. You are a gentleman, Charles, and an old man, and father of two children; and I myself (though I say it) by my mother's side, niece to a worshipful gentleman and a conductor: he has been three times in his Majesty's service at Chester, and is now the fourth time, God bless him and his charge, upon his journey.

Old Merrythought

11([Sings at the window.] Go from my window, love, go;
12Go from my window, my dear.
13The wind and the rain
14Will drive you back again;
15You cannot be lodgèd here.

Hark you, Mistress Merrythought, you that walk upon adventures, and forsake your husband, because he sings with never a penny in his purse: what, shall I think myself the worse? Faith, no, I'll be merry. You come not here; here's none but lads of mettle, lives of a hundred yeare and upwards; care never drunk their bloods, nor want made 'em warble

[Sings.]

17Heigh-ho, my heart is heavy.

Mistress Merrythought

Why, Master Merrythought, what am I that you should laugh me to scorn thus abruptly? Am I not your fellow feeler (as we may say) in all our miseries? Your comforter in health and sickness? Have I not brought you children? Are they not like you, Charles? Look upon thine own image, hard-hearted man. And yet for all this —

Old Merrythought

19([Sings] within.) Begone, begone, my Juggy, my puggy,
20Begone, my love, my dear.
21The weather is warm,
22'Twill do thee no harm,
23Thou canst not be lodgèd here.

Be merry, boys. Some light music, and more wine!

Wife

He's not in earnest, I hope, George, is he?

Citizen

What if he be, sweetheart?

Wife

Marry, if he be, George, I'll make bold to tell him he's an ingrant old man to use his bedfellow so scurvily.

Citizen

What! How does he use her, honey?

Wife

Marry, come up, Sir Saucebox! I think you'll take his part, will you not? Lord, how hot you are grown! You are a fine man, an you had a fine dog: it becomes you sweetly.

Citizen

Nay, prithee, Nell chide not; for, as I am an honest man and a true Christian grocer, I do not like his doings.

Wife

I cry you mercy, then, George; you know we are all frail and full of infirmities. — D'ye hear, Master Merrythought? May I crave a word with you?

Old Merrythought

(within) Strike up lively, lads.

Wife

I had not thought in truth, Master Merrythought, that a man of your age and discretion (as I may say), being a gentleman, and therefore known by your gentle conditions, could have used so little respect to the weakness of his wife. For your wife is your own flesh, the staff of your age, your yokefellow, with whose help you draw through the mire of this transitory world. Nay, she's your own rib. And again —

Old Merrythought

34[Sings within.] I come not hither for thee to teach.
35I have no pulpit for thee to preach.
36I would thou hadst kissed me under the breech,
37As thou art a lady gay.

Wife

Marry, with a vengeance! I am heartily sorry for the poor gentlewoman; but if I were thy wife, i'faith, graybeard, i'faith —

Citizen

I prithee, sweet honeysuckle, be content.

Wife

Give me such words that am a gentlewoman born! Hang him, hoary rascal! Get me some drink, George, I am almost molten with fretting. Now, beshrew his knave's heart for it!

[Exit Citizen.]

Old Merrythought

[within] Play me a light lavolto. — Come, be frolick. — Fill the good fellows wine.

Mistress Merrythought

Why, Master Merrythought, are you disposed to make me wait here? You'll open, I hope. I'll fetch them that shall open else.

Old Merrythought

[within] Good woman, if you will sing, I'll give you something; if not —

Song

44You are no love for me, Marg'ret;
45I am no love for you.

— Come aloft, boys, aloft!

Mistress Merrythought

Now a churl's fart in your teeth, sir! — Come Mick, we'll not trouble him; 'a shall not ding us i'th'teeth with his bread and his broth – that he shall not. Come, boy, I'll provide for thee, I warrant thee: we'll go to Master Venturewell's, the merchant. I'll get his letter to mine host of the Bell in Waltham. There I'll place thee with the Tapster. Will not that do well for thee, Mick? And let me alone for that old cuckoldly knave, your father: I'll use him in his kind, I warrant ye.

[Exeunt.]

[Enter Citizen with the beer.]

Wife

Come, George, where's the beer?

Citizen

Here, love.

Wife

This old fornicating fellow will not out of my mind yet.— Gentlemen, I'll begin to you all, and I desire more of your acquaintance, with all my heart. [She drinks.] — Fill the gentlemen some beer, George.

<<Finis actus tertii.>>
