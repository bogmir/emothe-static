2.1

Enter Merchant and Humphrey.

Merchant

And how, faith, how goes it now, son Humphrey?

Humphrey

2Right worshipful and my beloved friend
3And father dear, this matter's at an end.

Merchant

4'Tis well, it should be so. I'm glad the girl
5Is found so tractable.

Humphrey

Is found so tractable. Nay, she must whirl
6From hence, and you must wink (for so, I say,
7The story tells) tomorrow before day.

Wife

George, dost thou think, in thy conscience now, 'twill be a match? Tell me but what thou think'st, sweet rogue. Thou see'st the poor gentleman, dear heart, how it labors and throbs, I warrant you, to be at rest. I'll go move the father for't.

Citizen

No, no, I prithee, sit still, honeysuckle; thou'lt spoil all. If he deny him, I'll bring half a dozen good fellows myself, and in the shutting of an evening knock't up, and there's an end.

Wife

I'll buss thee for that, i'faith, boy. Well, George, well, you have been a wag in your days, I warrant you; but God forgive you, and I do with all my heart.

Merchant

11How was it, son? You told me that tomorrow,
12Before day break, you must convey her hence.

Humphrey

13I must, I must, and thus it is agreed.
14Your daughter rides upon a brown-bay steed,
15I on a sorrel, which I bought of Brian,
16The honest host of the Red Roaring Lion
17In Waltham situate. Then, if you may,
18Consent in seemly sort, lest by delay
19The fatal sisters come and do the office,
20And then you'll sing another song.

Merchant

And then you'll sing another song. Alas,
21Why should you be thus full of grief to me,
22That do as willing as yourself agree
23To anything, so it be good and fair?
24Then steal her when you will, if such a pleasure
25Content you both. I'll sleep and never see it,
26To make your joys more full. But tell me why
27You may not here perform your marriage?

Wife

God's blessing o'thy soul, old man. I'faith, thou art loath to part true hearts. — I see 'a has her, George, and I'm as glad on't. — Well, go thy ways, Humphrey, for a fair-spoken man. I believe thou hast not thy fellow within the walls of London; an I should say the suburbs too, I should not lie. — Why dost not rejoice with me, George?

Citizen

If I could but see Rafe again, I were as merry as mine host, i'faith.

Humphrey

30The cause you seem to ask, I thus declare.
31Help me, O Muses nine! Your daughter sware
32A foolish oath, the more it was the pity;
33Yet none but myself within this city
34Shall dare to say so, but a bold defiance
35Shall meet him, were he of the noble science.
36And yet she sware, and yet why did she sware?
37Truly I cannot tell, unless it were
38For her own ease, for sure sometimes an oath,
39Being sworn, thereafter is like cordial broth.
40And this it was: she swore never to marry
41But such a one whose mighty arm could carry
42(As meaning me, for I am such a one)
43Her bodily away through stick and stone,
44Till both of us arrive, at her request,
45Some ten miles off in the wild Waltham Forest.

Merchant

46If this be all, you shall not need to fear
47Any denial in your love. Proceed;
48I'll neither follow nor repent the deed.

Humphrey

49Goodnight, twenty goodnights, and twenty more.
50And twenty more goodnights, that makes threescore.

Exeunt.

[2.2]

Enter Mistress Merrythought [with a casket] and her son Michael.

Mistress Merrythought

Come, Michael, art thou not weary, boy?

Michael

No, forsooth, mother, not I.

Mistress Merrythought

Where be we now, child?

Michael

Indeed, forsooth, mother, I cannot tell, unless we be at Mile End. Is not all the world Mile End, mother?

Mistress Merrythought

No, Michael, not all the world, boy, but I can assure thee, Michael, Mile End is a goodly matter. There has been a pitch-field, my child, between the naughty Spaniels and the Englishmen, and the Spaniels ran away, Michael, and the Englishmen followed. My neighbor Coxstone was there, boy, and killed them all with a birding piece.

Michael

Mother, forsooth —

Mistress Merrythought

What says my white boy?

Michael

Shall not my father go with us too?

Mistress Merrythought

No, Michael, let thy father go snick up. He shall never come between a pair of sheets with me again, while he lives. Let him stay at home and sing for his supper, boy. Come, child, sit down, and I'll show my boy fine knacks indeed. [They sit down, and she opens the casket.] Look here, Michael: here's a ring, and here's a brooch, and here's a bracelet, and here's two rings more, and here's money and gold by th'eye, my boy.

Michael

Shall I have all this, mother?

Mistress Merrythought

Ay, Michael, thou shalt have all, Michael.

Citizen

How lik'st thou this, wench?

Wife

I cannot tell. I would have Rafe, George; I'll see no more else indeed. la. And I pray you let the youths understand so much by word of mouth, for I tell you truly, I'm afraid o'my boy. Come, come, George, let's be merry and wise. The child's a fatherless child, and say they should put him into a strait pair of gaskins, 'twere worse than knotgrass; he would never grow after it.

Enter Rafe [in ridiculous armor, wearing the pestle as his badge], [Tim as] squire, and [George as] dwarf.

Citizen

Here's Rafe, here's Rafe.

Wife

How do you, Rafe? You are welcome, Rafe, as I may say. It's a good boy. Hold up thy head, and be not afraid; we are thy friends, Rafe. The gentlemen will praise thee, Rafe, if thou play'st thy part with audacity. Begin, Rafe, o'God's name.

Rafe

My trusty squire, unlace my helm, give me my hat. Where are we, or what desert may this be?

[George as] Dwarf

Mirror of knighthood, this is, as I take it, the perilous Waltham down, n whose bottom stands the enchanted valley.

Mistress Merrythought

Oh, Michael, we are betrayed, we are betrayed; here be giants. Fly, boy, fly, boy, fly!

Exeunt mother [Mistress Merrythought] and Michael[, leaving the casket].

Rafe

20Lace on my helm again. What noise is this?
21A gentle lady flying the embrace
22Of some uncourteous knight? I will relieve her.
23Go, squire, and say the knight that wears this pestle
24In honor of all ladies swears revenge
25Upon that recreant coward that pursues her.
26Go, comfort her and that same gentle squire
27That bears her company.

[Tim as] Squire

That bears her company. I go, brave knight.

[Exit.]

Rafe

28My trusty dwarf and friend, reach me my shield,
29And hold it while I swear: first by my knighthood,
30Then by the soul of Amadis de Gaul,
31My famous ancestor, then by my sword,
32The beauteous Brionella girt about me,
33By this bright burning pestle of mine honor,
34The living trophy, and by all respect
35Due to distressèd damsels, here I vow
36Never to end the quest of this fair lady
37And that forsaken squire till by my valor
38I gain their liberty.

[George as] Dwarf

I gain their liberty. Heaven bless the knight
39That thus relieves poor errant gentlewomen.

Exit [with Rafe].

Wife

Ay, marry, Rafe, this has some savor in't. I would see the proudest of them all offer to carry his books after him. But, George, I will not have him go away so soon; I shall be sicke if he go away, that I shall. Call Rafe again, George, call Rafe again, I prithee, sweetheart, let him come fight before me, and let's ha' some drums and some trumpets, and let him kill all that comes near him, an thou lov'st me, George.

Citizen

Peace a little, bird. He shall kill them all, an they were twenty more on 'em than there are.

[2.3]

Enter Jasper.

Jasper

1Now, Fortune, if thou be'st not only ill,
2Show me thy better face, and bring about
3Thy desperate wheel, that I may climb at length
4And stand. This is our place of meeting,
5If love have any constancy. O age,
6Where only wealthy men are counted happy!
7How shall I please thee? How deserve thy smiles,
8When I am only rich in misery?
9My father's blessing and this little coin
10Is my inheritance, a strong revenue.
11From earth thou art, and to the earth I give thee.

[Throws away the money.]

12There grow and multiply, whilst fresher air
13Breeds me a fresher fortune —
Breeds me a fresher fortune — (Spies the casket.) How, illusion!
14What, hath the devil coined himself before me?
15'Tis metal good; it rings well. I am waking,
16And taking too, I hope. Now, God's dear blessing
17Upon his heart that left it here. 'Tis mine.
18These pearls, I take it, were not left for swine.

Exit [with the casket].

Wife

I do not like that this unthrifty youth should embezzle away the money; the poore gentlewoman his mother will have a heavy heart for it, God knows.

Citizen

And reason good, sweetheart.

Wife

But let him go. I'll tell Rafe a tale in's ear shall fetch him again with a wanion, I warrant him, if he be above ground. And besides, George, here are a number of sufficient gentlemen can witness, and myself, and yourself, and the musicians, if we be called in question. But here comes Rafe, George. Thou shalt hear him speak, an he were an emperal.

[2.4]

Enter Rafe and [George as] dwarf.

Rafe

1Comes not Sir Squire again?

[George as] Dwarf

Comes not Sir Squire again? Right courteous knight,
2Your squire doth come and with him comes the lady,

Enter Mistress Merrythought and Michael, and [Tim as] squire.

3For and the Squire of Damsels, as I take it.

Rafe

4Madam, if any service or devoir
5Of a poor errant knight may right your wrongs,
6Command it. I am prest to give you succor,
7For to that holy end I bear my armor,

Mistress Merrythought

Alas, sir, I am a poor gentlewoman, and I have lost my mony in this forest.

Rafe

9"Desert," you would say, lady, and not lost
10Whilst I have sword and lance. Dry up your tears,
11Which ill befits the beauty of that face,
12And tell the story, if I may request it,
13Of your disastrous fortune.

Mistress Merrythought

Out, alas, I left a thousand pound, a thousand pound, e'en all the mony I had laid up for this youth, upon the sight of Your Mastership — you looked so grim, and, as I may say it, saving your presence, more like a giant than a mortal man.

Rafe

15I am as you are, lady; so are they
16All mortal. But why weeps this gentle squire?

Mistress Merrythought

Has he not cause to weep, do you think, when he hath lost his inheritance?

Rafe

18Young hope of valor, weep not; I am here
19That will confound thy foe and pay it dear
20Upon his coward head that dares deny
21Distressèd squires and ladies equity.
22I have but one horse, on which shall ride
23This lady fair behind me, and, before,
24This courteous squire. Fortune will give us more
25Upon our next adventure. Fairely speed
26Beside us, squire and dwarf, to do us need.

Exeunt.

Citizen

Did not I tell you, Nell, what your man would do? By the faith of my body, wench, for clean action and good delivery they may all cast their caps at him.

Wife

And so they may, i'faith, for, I dare speake it boldly, the twelve companies of London cannot match him, timber for timber. Well, George, an he be not inveigled by some of these paltry players, I ha' much marvel. But George, we ha' done our parts if the boy have any grace to be thankful.

Citizen

Yes, I warrant thee, duckling.

[2.5]

Enter Humphrey and Luce.

Humphrey

1Good Mistress Luce, however I in fault am
2For your lame horse, you're welcome unto Waltham.
3But which way now to go or what to say
4I know not truly till it be broad day.

Luce

5Oh, fear not, Master Humphrey, I am guide
6For this place good enough.

Humphrey

For this place good enough. Then up and ride,
7Or, if it please you, walk for your repose,
8Or sit, or, if you will, go pluck a rose:
9Either of which shall be indifferent
10To your good friend and Humphrey, whose consent
11Is so entangled ever to your will
12As the poor harmeless horse is to the mill.

Luce

13Faith, an you say the word, we'll e'en sit down
14And take a nap.

Humphrey

And take a nap. 'Tis better in the town,
15Where we may nap together, for, believe me.
16To sleep without a snatch would mickle grieve me.

Luce

17You're merry, Master Humphrey.

Humphrey

You're merry, Master Humphrey. So I am,
18And have been ever merry from my dam.

Luce

19Your nurse had the less labor.

Humphrey

Your nurse had the less labor. Faith, it may be,
20Unless it were by chance I did beray me.

Enter Jasper.

Jasper

21Luce, dear friend Luce!

Luce

Luce, dear friend Luce! Here, Jasper.

Jasper

Luce, dear friend Luce! Here, Jasper. You are mine.

Humphrey

22If it be so, my friend, you use me fine.
23What do you think I am?

Jasper

What do you think I am? An arrant noddy.

Humphrey

24A word of obloquy! Now, by God's body,
25I'll tell thy master, for I know thee well.

Jasper

26Nay, an you be so forward for to tell,
27Take that, and that,
Take that, and that, [He beats him.] and tell him, sir, I gave it,
28And say I paid you well.

Humphrey

And say I paid you well. Oh, sir, I have it,
29And do confess the payment. Pray be quiet.

Jasper

30Go, get to your nightcap and the diet
31To cure your beaten bones.

Luce

To cure your beaten bones. Alas, poor Humphrey,
32Get thee some wholesome broth with sage and comfry,
33A little oil of roses and a feather,
34To 'noint thy back withal.

Humphrey

To 'noint thy back withal. When I came hither,
35Would I had gone to Paris with John Dorry!

Luce

36Farewell, my pretty Nump. I am very sorry
37I cannot bear thee company.

Humphrey

I cannot bear thee company. Farewell.
38The devil's dam was ne'er so banged in hell.

Exeunt [Luce and Jasper].

Manet Humphrey.

Wife

This young Jasper will prove me another thing, o'my conscience, an he may be suffered. George, dost not see, George, how 'a swaggers and flies at the very heads o' folks as he were a dragon? Well, if I do not do his lesson for wronging the poor gentleman, I am no true woman. His friends that brought him up might have been better occupied, iwis, than ha' taught him these fegaries. He's e'en in the highway to the gallows, God bless him.

Citizen

You're too bitter, coney. The young man may do well enough for all this.

Wife

Come hither, Master Humphrey. Has he hurt you? Now, beshrew his fingers for't. Here, sweetheart, here's some green ginger for thee. Now, beshrew my heart, but 'a has peppernel in's head as big as a pullet's egg. Alas, sweet lamb, how thy temples beat! Take the peace on him, sweetheart, take the peace on him.

Enter a Boy.

Citizen

No, no, you talk like a foolish woman. I'll ha' Rafe fight with him, and swinge him up well-favoredly. — Sirrah boy, come hither: let Rafe come in and fight with Jasper.

Wife

Ay, and beat him well. He's an unhappy boy.

Boy

Sir, you must pardon us, the plot of our play lies contrary, and 'twill hazard the spoiling of our play.

Citizen

Plot me no plots. I'll ha' Rafe come out, I'll make your house too hot for you else.

Boy

Why, sir, he shall, but if anything fall out of order, the gentlemen must pardon us.

Citizen

Go your ways, goodman boy. [Exit Boy.] I'll hold him a penny he shall have his bellyful of fighting now. Ho, here comes Rafe. No more.

Enter Rafe, Mistress Merrythought, Michael, [Tim as] squire, and [George as] dwarf.

Rafe

49What knight is that, squire? Aske him if he keep
50The passage, bound by love of lady fair,
51Or else but prickant.

Humphrey

Or else but prickant. Sir, I am no knight,
52But a poor gentleman, that this same night
53Had stol'n from me on yonder green
54My lovely wife, and suffered (to be seen
55Yet extant on my shoulders) such a greeting
56That, whilst I live, I shall think of that meeting.

Wife

Ay, Rafe, he beat him unmercifully, Rafe. An thou spar'st him, Rafe, I would thou wert hanged.

Citizen

58No more, wife, no more.

Rafe

59Where is the caitiff wretch hath done this deed? —
60Lady, your pardon, that I may proceed
61Upon the quest of this injurious knight. —
62And thou, fair squire, repute me not the worse
63In leaving the great venture of the purse
64And the rich casket till some better leisure.

Enter Jasper and Luce.

Humphrey

65Here comes the broker hath purloined my treasure.

Rafe

66Go, squire, and tell him I am here,
67An errant knight-at-arms, to crave delivery
68Of that fair lady to her own knight's arms.
69If he deny, bid him take choice of ground,
70And so defy him.

[Tim as] Squire

71From the knight that bears
72The Golden Pestle, I defy thee knight.
73Unless thou make fair restitution
74Of that bright lady.

Jasper

Of that bright lady. Tell the knight that sent thee
75Hee is an ass, and I will keep the wench
76And knock his headpiece.

Rafe

And knock his headpiece. Knight, thou art but dead,
77If thou recall not thy uncourteous terms.

Wife

78Break 's pate, Rafe, break 's pate, Rafe, soundly!

Jasper

79Come, knight, I am ready for you. Now, your pestle

(Snatches away his pestle.)

80Shall try what temper, sir, your mortar's of.
81With that he stood upright in his stirrups,
82And gave the Knight of the Calfskin such a knock

[He knocks Rafe down.]

83That he forsook his horse and down he fell.
84And then he leaped upon him, and, plucking off his helmet —

Humphrey

85Nay, an my noble knight be down so soone,
86Though I can scarely go, I needs must run.

Exit Humphrey and Rafe.

Wife

Run, Rafe, run, Rafe, run for thy life, boy! Jasper comes, Jasper comes.

Jasper

88Come, Luce, we must have other arms for you.
89Humphrey and Golden Pestle, both adieu.

Exeunt.

Wife

Sure the devil, God bless us, is in this springald. Why, George, didst ever see such a firedrake? I am afraid my boy's miscarried. If he be, though he were Master Merrythought's son a thousand times, if there be any law in England, I'll make some of them smart for't.

Citizen

No, no, I have found out the matter, sweetheart: Jasper is inchanted; as sure as we are here, he is enchanted. He could no more have stood in Rafe's hands than I can stand in my Lord Mayor's. I'll have a ring to discover all enchantments, and Rafe shall beat him yet. Be no more vexed, for it shall be so.

[2.6]

Enter Rafe, [Tim as] squire, [George as] dwarf, Mistress Meryythought and Michael.

Wife

Oh, husband, here's Rafe again. — Stay, Rafe, let me speak with thee. How dost thou, Rafe? Art thou not shrewdly hurt? The foul great Lunge is laid unmercifully on thee. There's some sugar candy for thee. Proceed, thou shalt have another bout with him.

Citizen

If Rafe had him at the fencing school, if he did not make a puppy of him and drive him up and down the school, he should ne'er come in my shop more.

Mistress Merrythought

Truly, Master Knight of the Burning Pestle, I am weary.

Michael

Indeed, la, mother, and I am very hungry.

Rafe

5Take comfort, gentle dame, and you, fair squire,
6For in this desert there must needs be placed
7Many strong castles, held by courteous knights,
8And till I bring you safe to one of those
9I swear by this my order ne'er to leave you.

Wife

Well said, Rafe. — George, Rafe was ever comfortable, was he not?

Citizen

Yes, duck.

Wife

I shall ne'er forget him. When we had lost our child, you know, it was strayed almost, alone, to Puddle Wharf, and the criers were abroad for it, and there it had drowned itself but for a sculler — Rafe was the most comfortablest to me. "Peace, Mistress," says he, "let it go. I'll get you another as good." Did he not, George? Did he not say so?

Citizen

Yes, indeed, did he, mouse.

[George as] Dwarf

I would we had a mess of pottage and a pot of drink, squire, and were going to bed.

[Tim as] Squire

Why, we are at Waltham town's end, and that's the Bell Inn.

[George as] Dwarf

16Take courage, valiant knight, damsel, and squire.
17I have discovered, not a stone's cast off,
18An ancient castle held by the old knight
19Of the most holy order of the Bell,
20Who gives to all knights errant entertain.
21There plenty is of food, and all prepared
22By the white hands of his own lady dear.
23He hath three squires that welcome all his guests.
24The first, hight Chamberlaino, who will see
25Our beds prepared and bring us snowy sheets,
26Where never footman stretched his buttered hams.
27The second, hight Tapstero, who will see
28Our pots full fillèd, and no froth therein.
29The third, a gentle squire, Ostlero hight,
30Who will our palfreys slick with wisps of straw,
31And in the manger put them oats enough,
32And never grease their teeth with candle-snuff.

Wife

33That same dwarf's a pretty boy, but the squire's a grout-noll.

Rafe

34Knock at the gates, my squire, with stately lance.

Enter Tapster.

Tapster

35Who's there? You're welcome, gentlemen. Will you see a room?

[George as] Dwarf

36Right courteous and valiant Knight of the Burning Pestle,
37This is the Squire Tapstero .

Rafe

38Fair squire Tapstero , I, a wand'ring knight,
39Hight of the Burning Pestle, in the quest
40Of this fair lady's Casket and wrought purse,
41Losing myself in this vast wilderness,
42Am to this castle well by fortune brought,
43Where, hearing of the goodly entertain
44Your knight of holy Order of the Bell
45Gives to all damsels and all errant knights,
46I thought to knock, and now am bold to enter.

Tapster

An't please you see a chamber, you are very welcome.

Exeunt.

Wife

George, I would have something done, and I cannot tell what it is.

Citizen

What is it, Nell?

Wife

Why, George, shall Rafe beat nobody again? Prithee, sweetheart, let him.

Citizen

So he shall, Nell. And if I join with him, we'll knock them all.

[2.7]

Enter Humphrey and Merchant [Venturewell].

Wife

Oh, George, here's Master Humphrey again now, that lost Mistress Luce, and Mistress Lucy's father. Master Humphrey will do somebody's errand, I warrant him.

Humphrey

2Father, it's true, in arms I ne'er shall clasp her,
3For she is stol'n away by your man Jasper.

Wife

4I thought he would tell him.

Merchant

5Unhappy that I am, to lose my child!
6Now I begin to think on Jasper's words,
7Who oft hath urged to me thy foolishness.
8Why didst thou let her go? Thou lov'st her not,
9That wouldst bring home thy life, and not bring her.

Humphrey

10Father, forgive me. Shall I tell you true?
11Look on my shoulders: they are black and blue.
12Whilst to and fro fair Luce and I were winding,
13He came and basted me with a hedge binding.

Merchant

14Get men and horses straight. We will be there
15Within this hour. You know the place again?

Humphrey

16I know the place where he my loins did swaddle.
17I'll get six horses, and to each a saddle.

Merchant

18Meantime I'll go talk with Jasper's father.

Exeunt.

Wife

George, what wilt thou lay with me now that Master Humphrey has not Mistress Luce yet? Speake, George, what wilt thou lay with me?

Citizen

No, Nell, I warrant thee Jasper is at Puckeridge with her by this.

Wife

Nay, George, you must consider Mistress Lucy's feet are tender, and, besides, 'tis dark; and I promise you truly, I do not see how he should get out of Waltham forest with her yet.

Citizen

Nay, coney, what wilt thou lay with me that Rafe has her not yet?

Wife

I will not lay against Rafe, hony, because I have not spoken with him. But look, George, peace, here comes the merry old gentleman again.

[2.8]

Enter old Merrythought.

Old Merrythought

1[Sings.] When it was grown to dark midnight,
2And all were fast asleep,
3In came Margaret's grimly ghost
4And stood at William's feet.

I have money, and meat, and drink beforehand, till tomorrow at noon. Why should I be sad? Methinks I have half a dozen jovial spirits within me.

[Sings.]

6I am three merry men, and three merry men —

To what end should any man be sad in this world? Give me a man that, when he goes to hanging, cries "Trowl the black bowl to me!", and a woman that will sing a catch in her travail. I have seen a man come by my door, with a serious face, in a black cloak, without a hatband, carrying his head as if he looked for pins in the street; I have looked out of my window half a yeare after, and have spied that man's head upon London bridge. 'Tis vile. Never trust a tailor that does not sing at his work: his mind is of nothing but filching.

Wife

Mark this, George, 'tis worth noting: Godfrey, my taylor, you know, never sings, and he had foureteen yards to make this gown; and, I'll be sworn, Mistress Pennistone, the draper's wife had one made with twelve.

Old Merrythought

9[Sings.] 'Tis mirth that fills the veins with blood,
10More than wine, or sleep, or food.
11Let each man keep his heart at ease.
12No man dies of that disease.
13He that would his body keep
14From diseases must not weep,
15But whoever laughs and sings
16Never he his body brings
17Into fevers, gouts, or rheums,
18Or ling'ringly his lungs consumes,
19Or meets with achès in the bone,
20Or catharhs, or griping stone,
21But contented lives for aye.
22The more he laughs, the more he may.

Wife

Look, George, how say'st thou by this, George? Is't not a fine old man? — Now God's blessing o'thy sweet lips. — When wilt thou be so merry, George? Faith, thou art the frowning'st little thing, when thou art angry, in a country.

Enter Merchant [Venturewell].

Citizen

Peace, coney, thou shalt see him taken down too, I warrant thee. Here's Lucy's father come now.

Old Merrythought

25[Sings.] As you came from Walsingham,
26From that holy land,
27There met you not with my true love
28By the way as you came?

Merchant

29Oh, Master Merrythought! My daughter's gone.
30This mirth becomes you not. My daughter's gone.

Old Merrythought

31[Sings.] Why, an if she be, what care I?
32Or let her come, or go, or tarry.

Merchant

33Mock not my misery. It is your son,
34Whom I have made my own when all forsook him,
35Has stol'n my only joy, my child, away.

Old Merrythought

36[Sings.] He set her on a milk-white steed,
37And himself upon a gray.
38He never turned his face again,
39But he bore her quite away.

Merchant

40Unworthy of the kindness I have shown
41To thee, and thine! Too late I well perceive
42Thou art consenting to my daughter's loss.

Old Merrythought

Your daughter? What a stir's here wi' yer daughter? Let her go; think no more on her, but sing loud. If both my sons were on the gallows, I would sing:

[Sings.]

44Down, down, down: they fall
45Down, and arise they never shall.

Merchant

46Oh, might I behold her once again,
47And she once more embrace her agèd sire.

Old Merrythought

Fie, how scurvily this goes! "And she once more embrace her agèd sire"? You'll make a dog on her, will ye? She cares much for her agèd sire, I warrant you.

[Sings.]

49She cares cares not for her daddy,
50nor she cares not for her mammy,
51For she is, she is, she is, she is
52My Lord of Lowgave's lassie.

Merchant

53For this thy scorn I will pursue
54That son of thine to death.

Old Merrythought

55Do, and when you ha' killed him,

[Sings.]

56Give him flowers enow, palmer, give him flowers enow,
57Give him red, and white, and blue, green, and yellow.

Merchant

58I'll fetch my daughter.

Old Merrythought

I'll heare no more o' your daughter; it spoils my mirth.

Merchant

60I say I'll fetch my daughter.

Old Merrythought

61[Sings.] Was never man for lady's sake,
62–Down, down —
63Tormented as I, poor Sir Guy,
64– De derry down —
65For Lucy's sake, that lady bright,
66–Down, down —
67As ever men beheld with eye?
68– De derry down —

Merchant

69I'll be revenged, by heaven.

Exeunt.

Music.
