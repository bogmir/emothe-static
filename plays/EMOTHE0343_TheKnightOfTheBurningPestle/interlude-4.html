[Interlude 4]

Wife

Let him go, George; 'a shall not have any countenance from us, nor a good word from any i'th'company, if I may strike stroke in't.

Citizen

No more 'a sha' not, love. But, Nell, I will have Rafe do a very notable matter now, to the eternal honor and glory of all grocers. — Sirrah, you there, boy. Can none of you hear?

[Enter Boy.]

Boy

Sir, your pleasure.

Citizen

Let Rafe come out on May Day in the morning and speak upon a conduit with all his scarfs about him, and his fethers and his rings and his knacks.

Boy

Why, sir, you do not think of our plot. What will become of that, then?

Citizen

Why, sir, I care not what become on't; I'll have him come out, or I'll fetch him out myself. I'll have something done in honor of the city. Besides, he hath been long enough upon adventures. Bring him out quickly, or if I come in amongst you —

Boy

Well, sir, he shall come out, but if our play miscarry, sir, you are like to pay for't.

Citizen

Bring him away, then.

Exit Boy.

Wife

This will be brave, i'faith. George, shall not he dance the morris too, for the credit of the Strand?

Citizen

No, sweetheart, it will be too much for the boy. Enter Rafe. Oh, there he is, Nell, he's reasonable well in reparel, but he has not rings enough.

Rafe

12London, to thee I do present the merry month of May.
13Let each true subject be content to hear me what I say;
14For from the top of conduit head, as plainly may appear,
15I will both tell my name to you and wherefore I came here.
16My name is Rafe, by due descent, though not ignoble I,
17Yet far inferior to the flock of gracious grocery.
18And by the common counsel of my fellows in the Strand,
19With gilded staff and crossèd scarf, the May Lord here I stand.
20Rejoice, O English hearts, rejoice! Rejoice, O Lovers dear!
21Rejoice, O city, town, and country! Rejoice eke every shire!
22For now the fragrant flowers do spring and sprout in seemely sort;
23The little birds do sit and sing; the lambs do make fine sport.
24And now the birchen tree doth bud, that makes the schoolboy cry;
25The morris rings, while hobbyhorse doth foot it featously.
26The lords and ladies now abroad for their disport and play,
27Do kiss sometimes upon the grass, and sometimes in the hay.
28Now butter with a leaf of sage is good to purge the blood;
29Fly Venus and phlebotomy, for they are neither good.
30Now little fish on tender stone begin to cast their bellies,
31And sluggish snails, that erst were mute, do creep out of their shellies.
32The rumbling rivers now do warm for little boys to paddle;
33The sturdy steed now goes to grass, and up they hang his saddle.
34The heavy hart, the bellowing buck, the rascal and the pricket
35Are now among the yeoman's pease, and leave the fearful thicket.
36And be like them, O you, I say, of this same noble town,
37And lift aloft your velvet heads, and, slipping of your gown,
38With bells on legs and napkins clean unto your shoulders tied,
39With scarves and garters as you please, and "Hey for our town!" cried,
40March out and show your willing minds by twenty and by twenty,
41To Hoxton or to Newington, where ale and cakes are plenty.
42And let it ne'er be said, for shame, that we, the youths of London,
43Lay thrumming of our caps at home, and left our custom undone.
44Up, then, I say, both young and old, both man and maid a-Maying,
45With drums and guns that bounce aloud, and mery tabor playing!
46Which to prolong, God save our King, and send his country peace,
47And root out treason from the land! And so, my friends, I cease.

[Exit.]
