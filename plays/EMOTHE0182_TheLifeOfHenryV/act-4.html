Act IV

[4.0]

[Enter] Chorus.

CHORUS

1095Now entertain conjecture of a time
1096When creeping murmur and the poring dark
1097Fills the wide vessel of the universe.
1098From camp to camp, through the foul womb of night,
1099The hum of either army stilly sounds,
1100That the fixed sentinels almost receive
1101The secret whispers of each other's watch.
1102Fire answers fire, and through their paly flames
1103Each battle sees the other's umbered face.
1104Steed threatens steed, in high and boastful neighs
1105Piercing the night's dull ear, and from the tents,
1106The armorers accomplishing the knights,
1107With busy hammers closing rivets up,
1108Give dreadful note of preparation.
1109The country cocks do crow, the clocks do toll,
1110And the third hour of drowsy morning named.
1111Proud of their numbers and secure in soul,
1112The confident and over-lusty French
1113Do the low-rated English play at dice,
1114And chide the cripple tardy-gaited night,
1115Who like a foul and ugly witch doth limp
1116So tediously away. The poor condemnèd English,
1117Like sacrifices, by their watchful fires
1118Sit patiently and inly ruminate
1119The morning's danger; and their gesture sad,
1120Investing lank-lean cheeks and war-worn coats,
1121Presented them unto the gazing moon
1122So many horrid ghosts. Oh, now, who will behold
1123The royal captain of this ruined band
1124Walking from watch to watch, from tent to tent,
1125Let him cry "Praise and glory on his head!"
1126For forth he goes and visits all his host,
1127Bids them good morrow with a modest smile,
1128And calls them brothers, friends, and countrymen.
1129Upon his royal face there is no note
1130How dread an army hath enrounded him;
1131Nor doth he dedicate one jot of color
1132Unto the weary and all-watchèd night,
1133But freshly looks and overbears attaint
1134With cheerful semblance and sweet majesty,
1135That every wretch, pining and pale before,
1136Beholding him, plucks comfort from his looks.
1137A largess universal like the sun
1138His liberal eye doth give to everyone,
1139Thawing cold fear, that mean and gentle all
1140Behold, as may unworthiness define,
1141A little touch of Harry in the night.
1142And so our scene must to the battle fly,
1143Where — oh, for pity! — we shall much disgrace
1144With four or five most vile and ragged foils
1145Right ill-disposed in brawl ridiculous,
1146The name of Agincourt. Yet sit and see,
1147Minding true things by what their mock'ries be.

Exit.

[4.1]

Enter the King [and Gloucester, meeting Bedford].

KING HENRY

1148Gloucester, 'tis true that we are in great danger;
1149The greater therefore should our courage be.—
1150Good morrow, brother Bedford. God almighty,
1151There is some soul of goodness in things evil,
1152Would men observingly distill it out.
1153For our bad neighbor makes us early stirrers,
1154Which is both healthful and good husbandry.
1155Besides, they are our outward consciences
1156And preachers to us all, admonishing
1157That we should dress us fairly for our end.
1158Thus may we gather honey from the weed,
1159And make a moral of the devil himself.

Enter Erpingham.

1160Good morrow, old Sir Thomas Erpingham.
1161A good soft pillow for that good white head
1162Were better than a churlish turf of France.

ERPINGHAM

1163Not so, my liege. This lodging likes me better,
1164Since I may say "Now lie I like a king."

KING HENRY

1165'Tis good for men to love their present pains.
1166Upon example so, the spirit is eased,
1167And when the mind is quickened, out of doubt
1168The organs, though defunct and dead before,
1169Break up their drowsy grave and newly move
1170With casted slough, and fresh legerity.
1171Lend me thy cloak, Sir Thomas. — Brothers both,
1172Commend me to the princes in our camp.
1173Do my good morrow to them, and anon
1174Desire them all to my pavilion.

GLOUCESTER

1175We shall, my liege.

ERPINGHAM

1176Shall I attend your grace?

KING HENRY

Shall I attend your grace? No, my good knight,
1177Go with my brothers to my lords of England.
1178I and my bosom must debate awhile,
1179And then I would no other company.

ERPINGHAM

1180The lord in heaven bless thee, noble Harry.

Exeunt [all but King Henry, who disguises himself in Erpingham's cloak].

KING HENRY

1181God-a-mercy, old heart; thou speak'st cheerfully.

Enter Pistol.

PISTOL

Che vous la?

KING HENRY

A friend.

PISTOL

Discuss unto me: art thou officer, or art thou base, common, and popular?

KING HENRY

I am a gentleman of a company.

PISTOL

Trail'st thou the puissant pike?

KING HENRY

Even so. What are you?

PISTOL

As good a gentleman as the emperor.

KING HENRY

Then you are a better than the king.

PISTOL

The king's a bawcock and a heart of gold, a lad of life, an imp of fame, of parents good, of fist most valiant. I kiss his dirty shoe, and from heartstring I love the lovely bully. What is thy name?

KING HENRY

Harry le Roy.

PISTOL

Leroy? A Cornish name; art thou of Cornish crew?

KING HENRY

No, I am a Welshman.

PISTOL

Know'st thou Fluellen?

KING HENRY

Yes.

PISTOL

Tell him I'll knock his leek about his pate upon Saint Davy's day.

KING HENRY

Do not you wear your dagger in your cap that day, lest he knock that about yours.

PISTOL

Art thou his friend?

KING HENRY

And his kinsman too.

PISTOL

The fico for thee then.

KING HENRY

I thank you. God be with you.

PISTOL

My name is Pistol called.

Exit [Pistol].

KING HENRY

It sorts well with your fierceness.

Enter Fluellen and Gower [separately].

GOWER

Captain Fluellen.

FLUELLEN

'So! In the name of Jesu Christ speak fewer! It is the greatest admiration in the universal world when the true and aunchient prerogatiffs and laws of the wars is not kept. If you would take the pains but to examine the wars of Pompey the Great, you shall find, I warrant you, that there is no tiddle-taddle nor pibble-babble in Pompey's camp. I warrant you, you shall find the ceremonies of the wars, and the cares of it, and the forms of it, and the sobriety of it, and the modesty of it, to be otherwise.

GOWER

Why, the enemy is loud; you hear him all night.

FLUELLEN

If the enemy is an ass and a fool and a prating coxcomb, is it meet, think you, that we should also, look you, be an ass and a fool and a prating coxcomb, in your own conscience now?

GOWER

I will speak lower.

FLUELLEN

I pray you and beseech you that you will.

[Exeunt Gower and Fluellen.]

KING HENRY

1182Though it appear a little out of fashion,
1183There is much care and valor in this Welshman.

Enter three soldiers: John Bates, Alexander Court, and Michael Williams.

COURT

Brother John Bates, is not that the morning which breaks yonder?

BATES

I think it be, but we have no great cause to desire the approach of day.

WILLIAMS

We see yonder the beginning of the day, but I think we shall never see the end of it. — Who goes there?

KING HENRY

A friend.

WILLIAMS

Under what captain serve you?

KING HENRY

Under Sir Thomas Erpingham.

WILLIAMS

A good old commander and a most kind gentleman. I pray you, what thinks he of our estate?

KING HENRY

Even as men wrecked upon a sand, that look to be washed off the next tide.

BATES

He hath not told his thought to the king?

KING HENRY

No, nor it is not meet he should. For though I speak it to you, I think the king is but a man, as I am. The violet smells to him as it doth to me; the element shows to him as it doth to me; all his senses have but human conditions. His ceremonies laid by, in his nakedness he appears but a man, and though his affections are higher mounted than ours, yet when they stoop, they stoop with the like wing. Therefore when he sees reason of fears, as we do, his fears out of doubt be of the same relish as ours are. Yet in reason, no man should possess him with any appearance of fear, lest he by showing it should dishearten his army.

BATES

He may show what outward courage he will, but I believe, as cold a night as 'tis, he could wish himself in Thames up to the neck. And so I would he were, and I by him, at all adventures, so we were quit here.

KING HENRY

By my troth, I will speak my conscience of the king: I think he would not wish himself anywhere but where he is.

BATES

Then I would he were here alone. So should he be sure to be ransomed, and a many poor men's lives saved.

KING HENRY

I dare say you love him not so ill to wish him here alone, howsoever you speak this to feel other men's minds. Methinks I could not die anywhere so contented as in the king's company, his cause being just and his quarrel honorable.

WILLIAMS

That's more than we know.

BATES

Ay, or more than we should seek after. For we know enough if we know we are the king's subjects. If his cause be wrong, our obedience to the king wipes the crime of it out of us.

WILLIAMS

But if the cause be not good, the king himself hath a heavy reckoning to make, when all those legs and arms and heads chopped off in a battle shall join together at the latter day, and cry all, "We died at such a place," some swearing, some crying for a surgeon, some upon their wives left poor behind them, some upon the debts they owe, some upon their children rawly left. I am afeared there are few die well that die in a battle, for how can they charitably dispose of anything when blood is their argument? Now if these men do not die well, it will be a black matter for the king that led them to it, who to disobey were against all proportion of subjection.

KING HENRY

So if a son that is by his father sent about merchandise do sinfully miscarry upon the sea, the imputation of his wickedness, by your rule, should be imposed upon his father that sent him. Or if a servant, under his master's command transporting a sum of money, be assailed by robbers and die in many irreconciled iniquities, you may call the business of the master the author of the servant's damnation. But this is not so. The king is not bound to answer the particular endings of his soldiers, the father of his son, nor the master of his servant, for they purpose not their death when they purpose their services. Besides, there is no king, be his cause never so spotless, if it come to the arbitrament of swords, can try it out with all unspotted soldiers. Some, peradventure, have on them the guilt of premeditated and contrived murder; some of beguiling virgins with the broken seals of perjury; some, making the wars their bulwark, that have before gored the gentle bosom of peace with pillage and robbery. Now if these men have defeated the law and outrun native punishment, though they can outstrip men, they have no wings to fly from God. War is his beadle; war is his vengeance. So that here men are punished for before-breach of the king's laws in now the king's quarrel. Where they feared the death, they have borne life away; and where they would be safe, they perish. Then if they die unprovided, no more is the king guilty of their damnation than he was before guilty of those impieties for the which they are now visited. Every subject's duty is the king's, but every subject's soul is his own. Therefore should every soldier in the wars do as every sick man in his bed, wash every mote out of his conscience. And dying so, death is to him advantage, or not dying, the time was blessedly lost wherein such preparation was gained. And in him that escapes, it were not sin to think that, making God so free an offer, he let him outlive that day to see his greatness and to teach others how they should prepare.

WILLIAMS

'Tis certain, every man that dies ill, the ill upon his own head; the king is not to answer it.

BATES

I do not desire he should answer for me, and yet I determine to fight lustily for him.

KING HENRY

I myself heard the king say he would not be ransomed.

WILLIAMS

Ay, he said so to make us fight cheerfully, but when our throats are cut he may be ransomed and we ne'er the wiser.

KING HENRY

If I live to see it, I will never trust his word after.

WILLIAMS

You pay him, then! That's a perilous shot out of an elder-gun that a poor and a private displeasure can do against a monarch. You may as well go about to turn the sun to ice with fanning in his face with a peacock's feather. You'll never trust his word after! Come, 'tis a foolish saying.

KING HENRY

Your reproof is something too round. I should be angry with you if the time were convenient.

WILLIAMS

Let it be a quarrel between us if you live.

KING HENRY

I embrace it.

WILLIAMS

How shall I know thee again?

KING HENRY

Give me any gage of thine and I will wear it in my bonnet. Then if ever thou darest acknowledge it, I will make it my quarrel.

WILLIAMS

Here's my glove. Give me another of thine.

KING HENRY

There.

[They exchange gloves.]

WILLIAMS

This will I also wear in my cap. If ever thou come to me and say, after tomorrow, "This is my glove," by this hand I will take thee a box on the ear.

KING HENRY

If ever I live to see it, I will challenge it.

WILLIAMS

Thou darest as well be hanged.

KING HENRY

Well, I will do it, though I take thee in the king's company.

WILLIAMS

Keep thy word. Fare thee well.

BATES

Be friends, you English fools, be friends! We have French quarrels enough, if you could tell how to reckon.

[Exeunt] Soldiers.

KING HENRY

Indeed, the French may lay twenty French crowns to one they will beat us, for they bear them on their shoulders. But it is no English treason to cut French crowns, and tomorrow the king himself will be a clipper. —

1184Upon the king! "Let us our lives, our souls,
1185Our debts, our careful wives, our children, and
1186Our sins lay on the king." We must bear all.
1187O hard condition, twin-born with greatness,
1188Subject to the breath of every fool whose sense
1189No more can feel but his own wringing.
1190What infinite heart's-ease must kings neglect,
1191That private men enjoy?
1192And what have kings that privates have not too,
1193Save ceremony, save general ceremony?
1194And what art thou, thou idol ceremony?
1195What kind of god art thou, that suffer'st more
1196Of mortal griefs than do thy worshippers?
1197What are thy rents? What are thy comings in?
1198O ceremony, show me but thy worth.
1199What? Is thy soul of adoration?
1200Art thou aught else but place, degree, and form,
1201Creating awe and fear in other men,
1202Wherein thou art less happy being feared
1203Than they in fearing?
1204What drink'st thou oft, instead of homage sweet,
1205But poisoned flattery? O, be sick, great greatness,
1206And bid thy ceremony give thee cure.
1207Think'st thou the fiery fever will go out
1208With titles blown from adulation?
1209Will it give place to flexure and low bending?
1210Canst thou, when thou command'st the beggar's knee,
1211Command the health of it? No, thou proud dream
1212That play'st so subtly with a king's repose.
1213I am a king that find thee, and I know
1214'Tis not the balm, the scepter, and the ball,
1215The sword, the mace, the crown imperial,
1216The intertissued robe of gold and pearl,
1217The farcèd title running 'fore the king,
1218The throne he sits on, nor the tide of pomp
1219That beats upon the high shore of this world —
1220No, not all these, thrice-gorgeous ceremony,
1221Not all these, laid in bed majestical,
1222Can sleep so soundly as the wretched slave
1223Who, with a body filled and vacant mind,
1224Gets him to rest, crammed with distressful bread;
1225Never sees horrid night, the child of hell,
1226But like a lackey, from the rise to set,
1227Sweats in the eye of Phoebus, and all night
1228Sleeps in Elysium; next day after dawn
1229Doth rise and help Hyperion to his horse,
1230And follows so the ever-running year
1231With profitable labor to his grave.
1232And but for ceremony, such a wretch,
1233Winding up days with toil and nights with sleep,
1234Had the forehand and vantage of a king.
1235The slave, a member of the country's peace,
1236Enjoys it, but in gross brain little wots
1237What watch the king keeps to maintain the peace,
1238Whose hours the peasant best advantages.

Enter Erpingham.

ERPINGHAM

1239My lord, your nobles, jealous of your absence,
1240Seek through your camp to find you.

KING HENRY

Seek through your camp to find you. Good old knight,
1241Collect them all together at my tent:
1242I'll be before thee.

ERPINGHAM

I'll be before thee. I shall do't, my lord.

Exit.

KING HENRY

1243[Kneeling] O God of battles, steel my soldiers' hearts.
1244Possess them not with fear. Take from them now
1245The sense of reck'ning, ere th'opposèd numbers
1246Pluck their hearts from them. Not today, O Lord,
1247Oh, not today — think not upon the fault
1248My father made in compassing the crown.
1249I Richard's body have interrèd new,
1250And on it have bestowed more contrite tears
1251Than from it issued forcèd drops of blood.
1252Five hundred poor I have in yearly pay
1253Who twice a day their withered hands hold up
1254Toward heaven to pardon blood, and I have built
1255Two chantries where the sad and solemn priests
1256Sing still for Richard's soul. More will I do,
1257Though all that I can do is nothing worth,
1258Since that my penitence comes after all,
1259Imploring pardon.

Enter Gloucester.

GLOUCESTER

1260My liege.

KING HENRY

My liege. My brother Gloucester's voice? Ay,
1261I know thy errand. I will go with thee.
1262The day, my friend, and all things stay for me.

Exeunt.

[4.2]

Enter the Dauphin, Orléans, Rambures, and Beaumont.

ORLÉANS

1263The sun doth gild our armor. Up, my lords!

DAUPHIN

1264Montez à cheval : my horse, varlet lackey, ha!

ORLÉANS

1265Oh, brave spirit!

DAUPHIN

1266Via les eaux et terres.

ORLÉANS

1267Rien plus? L'air et feu ?

DAUPHIN

1268Cieux , Cousin Orléans.

Enter Constable.

Cieux , Cousin Orléans. Now my lord constable?

CONSTABLE

1269Hark how our steeds for present service neigh.

DAUPHIN

1270Mount them, and make incision in their hides,
1271That their hot blood may spin in English eyes
1272And d'out them with superfluous courage. Ha!

RAMBURES

1273What, will you have them weep our horses' blood?
1274How shall we then behold their natural tears?

Enter Messenger.

MESSENGER

1275The English are embattled, you French peers.

CONSTABLE

1276To horse, you gallant princes, straight to horse!
1277Do but behold yon poor and starvèd band
1278And your fair show shall suck away their souls,
1279Leaving them but the shales and husks of men.
1280There is not work enough for all our hands,
1281Scarce blood enough in all their sickly veins
1282To give each naked curtle-ax a stain
1283That our French gallants shall today draw out
1284And sheathe for lack of sport. Let us but blow on them,
1285The vapor of our valor will o'erturn them.
1286'Tis positive against all exceptions, lords,
1287That our superfluous lackeys and our peasants,
1288Who in unnecessary action swarm
1289About our squares of battle, were enough
1290To purge this field of such a hilding foe
1291Though we upon this mountain's basis by
1292Took stand for idle speculation,
1293But that our honors must not. What's to say?
1294A very little little let us do
1295And all is done. Then let the trumpets sound
1296The tucket sonance and the note to mount,
1297For our approach shall so much dare the field
1298That England shall couch down in fear and yield.

Enter Grandpré.

GRANDPRÉ

1299Why do you stay so long, my lords of France?
1300Yon island carrions, desperate of their bones,
1301Ill-favoredly become the morning field.
1302Their ragged curtains poorly are let loose
1303And our air shakes them passing scornfully.
1304Big Mars seems bankrupt in their beggared host
1305And faintly through a rusty beaver peeps.
1306The horsemen sit like fixèd candlesticks
1307With torch-staves in their hand, and their poor jades
1308Lob down their heads, dropping the hides and hips,
1309The gum down-roping from their pale dead eyes,
1310And in their pale dull mouths the gemelled bit
1311Lies foul with chawed grass, still and motionless;
1312And their executors, the knavish crows,
1313Fly o'er them all, impatient for their hour.
1314Description cannot suit itself in words
1315To demonstrate the life of such a battle
1316In life so lifeless as it shows itself.

CONSTABLE

1317They have said their prayers, and they stay for death.

DAUPHIN

1318Shall we go send them dinners and fresh suits
1319And give their fasting horses provender,
1320And after fight with them?

CONSTABLE

1321I stay but for my guard. On to the field;
1322I will the banner from a trumpet take
1323And use it for my haste. Come, come away.
1324The sun is high and we outwear the day.

Exeunt.

[4.3]

Enter Gloucester, Bedford, Exeter, Erpingham with all his host, Salisbury, and Westmorland.

GLOUCESTER

1325Where is the king?

BEDFORD

1326The king himself is rode to view their battle.

WESTMORLAND

1327Of fighting men they have full threescore thousand.

EXETER

1328There's five to one, besides they all are fresh.

SALISBURY

1329God's arm strike with us! 'Tis a fearful odds.
1330God b'wi'you, princes all. I'll to my charge.
1331If we no more meet till we meet in heaven,
1332Then joyfully, my noble lord of Bedford,
1333My dear lord Gloucester, and my good lord Exeter,
1334And my kind kinsman, warriors all, adieu.

BEDFORD

1335Farewell, good Salisbury, and good luck go with thee.

EXETER

1336[To Salisbury] Farewell, kind lord. Fight valiantly today.
1337And yet I do thee wrong to mind thee of it,
1338For thou art framed of the firm truth of valor.

[Exit Salisbury.]

BEDFORD

1339He is as full of valor as of kindness,
1340Princely in both.

Enter the King.

WESTMORLAND

Princely in both. Oh, that we now had here
1341But one ten thousand of those men in England
1342That do no work today.

KING HENRY

That do no work today. What's he that wishes so?
1343My cousin Westmorland? No, my fair cousin,
1344If we are marked to die, we are enough
1345To do our country loss; and if to live,
1346The fewer men, the greater share of honor.
1347God's will, I pray thee wish not one man more.
1348By Jove, I am not covetous for gold,
1349Nor care I who doth feed upon my cost.
1350It earns me not if men my garments wear;
1351Such outward things dwell not in my desires.
1352But if it be a sin to covet honor,
1353I am the most offending soul alive.
1354No, faith, my coz, wish not a man from England.
1355God's peace, I would not lose so great an honor
1356As one man more methinks would share from me
1357For the best hope I have. Oh, do not wish one more.
1358Rather proclaim it, Westmorland, through my host
1359That he which hath no stomach to this fight,
1360Let him depart. His passport shall be made
1361And crowns for convoy put into his purse.
1362We would not die in that man's company
1363That fears his fellowship to die with us.
1364This day is called the feast of Crispian.
1365He that outlives this day and comes safe home
1366Will stand a tiptoe when this day is named
1367And rouse him at the name of Crispian.
1368He that shall see this day and live old age
1369Will yearly on the vigil feast his neighbors
1370And say, "Tomorrow is Saint Crispian."
1371Then will he strip his sleeve and show his scars.
1372Old men forget; yet all shall be forgot,
1373But he'll remember, with advantages,
1374What feats he did that day. Then shall our names,
1375Familiar in his mouth as household words —
1376Harry the king, Bedford and Exeter,
1377Warwick and Talbot, Salisbury and Gloucester —
1378Be in their flowing cups freshly remembered.
1379This story shall the good man teach his son,
1380And Crispin Crispian shall ne'er go by
1381From this day to the ending of the world,
1382But we in it shall be rememberèd,
1383We few, we happy few, we band of brothers.
1384For he today that sheds his blood with me
1385Shall be my brother; be he ne'er so vile,
1386This day shall gentle his condition.
1387And gentlemen in England now abed,
1388Shall think themselves accursed they were not here,
1389And hold their manhoods cheap whiles any speaks
1390That fought with us upon Saint Crispin's day.

Enter Salisbury.

SALISBURY

1391My sovereign lord, bestow yourself with speed.
1392The French are bravely in their battles set
1393And will with all expedience charge on us.

KING HENRY

1394All things are ready if our minds be so.

WESTMORLAND

1395Perish the man whose mind is backward now.

KING HENRY

1396Thou dost not wish more help from England, coz?

WESTMORLAND

1397God's will, my liege, would you and I alone
1398Without more help could fight this royal battle!

KING HENRY

1399Why, now thou hast unwished five thousand men,
1400Which likes me better than to wish us one. —
1401You know your places. God be with you all.

Tucket. Enter Montjoy.

MONTJOY

1402Once more I come to know of thee, King Harry,
1403If for thy ransom thou wilt now compound
1404Before thy most assurèd overthrow,
1405For certainly thou art so near the gulf
1406Thou needs must be englutted. Besides, in mercy
1407The constable desires thee thou wilt mind
1408Thy followers of repentance, that their souls
1409May make a peaceful and a sweet retire
1410From off these fields where, wretches, their poor bodies
1411Must lie and fester.

KING HENRY

Must lie and fester. Who hath sent thee now?

MONTJOY

1412The Constable of France.

KING HENRY

1413I pray thee bear my former answer back:
1414Bid them achieve me and then sell my bones.
1415Good God, why should they mock poor fellows thus?
1416The man that once did sell the lion's skin
1417While the beast lived, was killed with hunting him.
1418A many of our bodies shall no doubt
1419Find native graves, upon the which I trust
1420Shall witness live in brass of this day's work.
1421And those that leave their valiant bones in France,
1422Dying like men, though buried in your dunghills,
1423They shall be famed, for there the sun shall greet them
1424And draw their honors reeking up to heaven,
