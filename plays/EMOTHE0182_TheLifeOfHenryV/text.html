William Shakespeare

The Life of Henry V

Act I

[Prologue]

Enter [Chorus as] Prologue.

CHORUS

35Oh, for a muse of fire, that would ascend
36The brightest heaven of invention,
37A kingdom for a stage, princes to act,
38And monarchs to behold the swelling scene.
39Then should the warlike Harry, like himself,
40Assume the port of Mars, and at his heels,
41Leashed in like hounds, should famine, sword, and fire
42Crouch for employment. But pardon, gentles all,
43The flat unraisèd spirits that hath dared,
44On this unworthy scaffold, to bring forth
45So great an object. Can this cockpit hold
46The vasty fields of France? Or may we cram
47Within this wooden O the very casques
48That did affright the air at Agincourt?
49Oh, pardon, since a crooked figure may
50Attest in little place a million,
51And let us, ciphers to this great account,
52On your imaginary forces work.
53Suppose within the girdle of these walls
54Are now confined two mighty monarchies,
55Whose high, uprearèd, and abutting fronts
56The perilous narrow ocean parts asunder.
57Piece out our imperfections with your thoughts:
58Into a thousand parts divide one man,
59And make imaginary puissance.
60Think, when we talk of horses, that you see them
61Printing their proud hooves i'th'receiving earth.
62For 'tis your thoughts that now must deck our kings,
63Carry them here and there, jumping o'er times,
64Turning th'accomplishment of many years
65Into an hourglass: for the which supply,
66Admit me Chorus to this history,
67Who, prologue-like, your humble patience pray
68Gently to hear, kindly to judge our play.

Exit.

1.1

Enter [the Archbishop of] Canterbury and [the Bishop of] Ely.

CANTERBURY

69My lord, I'll tell you, that self bill is urged
70Which in th'eleventh year of the last king's reign
71Was like, and had indeed against us passed,
72But that the scambling and unquiet time
73Did push it out of further question.

ELY

74But how, my lord, shall we resist it now?

CANTERBURY

75It must be thought on. If it pass against us,
76We lose the better half of our possession,
77For all the temporal lands which men devout
78By testament have given to the Church
79Would they strip from us, being valued thus:
80As much as would maintain, to the king's honor,
81Full fifteen earls and fifteen hundred knights,
82Six thousand and two hundred good esquires,
83And to relief of lazars and weak age
84Of indigent faint souls past corporal toil,
85A hundred almshouses, right well supplied;
86And to the coffers of the king beside,
87A thousand pounds by th'year. Thus runs the bill.

ELY

88This would drink deep.

CANTERBURY

This would drink deep. 'Twould drink the cup and all.

ELY

89But what prevention?

CANTERBURY

90The king is full of grace and fair regard.

ELY

91And a true lover of the holy Church.

CANTERBURY

92The courses of his youth promised it not.
93The breath no sooner left his father's body,
94But that his wildness, mortified in him,
95Seemed to die too. Yea, at that very moment,
96Consideration like an angel came,
97And whipped th'offending Adam out of him,
98Leaving his body as a paradise,
99T'envelop and contain celestial spirits.
100Never was such a sudden scholar made,
101Never came reformation in a flood
102With such a heady currence scouring faults,
103Nor never hydra-headed willfulness
104So soon did lose his seat, and all at once,
105As in this king.

ELY

As in this king. We are blessèd in the change.

CANTERBURY

106Hear him but reason in divinity,
107And, all-admiring, with an inward wish
108You would desire the king were made a prelate.
109Hear him debate of commonwealth affairs,
110You would say it hath been all in all his study.
111List his discourse of war, and you shall hear
112A fearful battle rendered you in music.
113Turn him to any cause of policy,
114The Gordian knot of it he will unloose,
115Familiar as his garter; that when he speaks,
116The air, a chartered libertine, is still,
117And the mute wonder lurketh in men's ears
118To steal his sweet and honeyed sentences,
119So that the art and practic part of life
120Must be the mistress to this theoric.
121Which is a wonder how his grace should glean it,
122Since his addiction was to courses vain,
123His companies unlettered, rude, and shallow,
124His hours filled up with riots, banquets, sports,
125And never noted in him any study,
126Any retirement, any sequestration
127From open haunts and popularity.

ELY

128The strawberry grows underneath the nettle,
129And wholesome berries thrive and ripen best
130Neighbored by fruit of baser quality;
131And so the prince obscured his contemplation
132Under the veil of wildness, which no doubt
133Grew like the summer grass, fastest by night,
134Unseen, yet crescive in his faculty.

CANTERBURY

135It must be so, for miracles are ceased,
136And therefore we must needs admit the means
137How things are perfected.

ELY

How things are perfected. But my good lord,
138How now for mitigation of this bill
139Urged by the commons? Doth his majesty
140Incline to it or no?

CANTERBURY

Incline to it or no? He seems indifferent,
141Or rather swaying more upon our part,
142Than cherishing th'exhibitors against us;
143For I have made an offer to his majesty,
144Upon our spiritual convocation,
145And in regard of causes now in hand,
146Which I have opened to his grace at large,
147As touching France, to give a greater sum
148Than ever at one time the clergy yet
149Did to his predecessors part withal.

ELY

150How did this offer seem received, my lord?

CANTERBURY

151With good acceptance of his majesty,
152Save that there was not time enough to hear,
153As I perceived his grace would fain have done,
154The severals and unhidden passages
155Of his true titles to some certain dukedoms,
156And generally to the crown and seat of France
157Derived from Edward, his great-grandfather.

ELY

158What was th'impediment that broke this off?

CANTERBURY

159The French ambassador upon that instant
160Craved audience; and the hour, I think, is come
161To give him hearing. Is it four o'clock?

ELY

162It is.

CANTERBURY

163Then go we in to know his embassy,
164Which I could with a ready guess declare
165Before the Frenchman speak a word of it.

ELY

166I'll wait upon you, and I long to hear it.

Exeunt.

[1.2]

Enter the King, Humphrey [Duke of Gloucester], Bedford, Clarence, Warwick, Westmorland, and Exeter[, with attendants].

KING HENRY

167Where is my gracious lord of Canterbury?

EXETER

168Not here in presence.

KING HENRY

Not here in presence. Send for him, good uncle.

[Exit attendant.]

WESTMORLAND

169Shall we call in th'ambassador, my liege?

KING HENRY

170Not yet, my cousin. We would be resolved,
171Before we hear him, of some things of weight
172That task our thoughts concerning us and France.

Enter [the] two Bishops[, Canterbury and Ely].

CANTERBURY

173God and his angels guard your sacred throne
174And make you long become it.

KING HENRY

And make you long become it. Sure we thank you.
175My learnèd lord, we pray you to proceed,
176And justly and religiously unfold
177Why the law Salic, that they have in France,
178Or should or should not bar us in our claim.
179And God forbid, my dear and faithful lord,
180That you should fashion, wrest, or bow your reading,
181Or nicely charge your understanding soul
182With opening titles miscreate, whose right
183Suits not in native colors with the truth.
184For God doth know how many now in health
185Shall drop their blood in approbation
186Of what your reverence shall incite us to.
187Therefore take heed how you impawn our person,
188How you awake our sleeping sword of war;
189We charge you in the name of God, take heed.
190For never two such kingdoms did contend
191Without much fall of blood, whose guiltless drops
192Are every one a woe, a sore complaint
193'Gainst him whose wrongs gives edge unto the swords
194That makes such waste in brief mortality.
195Under this conjuration speak, my lord,
196For we will hear, note, and believe in heart
197That what you speak is in your conscience washed
198As pure as sin with baptism.

CANTERBURY

199Then hear me, gracious sovereign, and you peers
200That owe your selves, your lives and services
201To this imperial throne. There is no bar
202To make against your highness' claim to France
203But this, which they produce from Pharamond:
204"In terram Salicam mulieres ne succedant" —
205"No woman shall succeed in Salic land" —
206Which Salic land the French unjustly gloss
207To be the realm of France, and Pharamond
208The founder of this law and female bar.
209Yet their own authors faithfully affirm
210That the land Salic is in Germany,
211Between the floods of Saale and of Elbe,
212Where Charles the Great, having subdued the Saxons,
213There left behind and settled certain French
214Who, holding in disdain the German women
215For some dishonest manners of their life,
216Established then this law: to wit, no female
217Should be inheritrix in Salic land,
218Which Salic, as I said, 'twixt Elbe and Saale,
219Is at this day in Germany, called Meissen.
220Then doth it well appear the Salic law
221Was not devisèd for the realm of France,
222Nor did the French possess the Salic land
223Until four hundred one-and-twenty years
224After defunction of King Pharamond,
225Idly supposed the founder of this law,
226Who died within the year of our redemption
227Four hundred twenty-six; and Charles the Great
228Subdued the Saxons and did seat the French
229Beyond the river Saale in the year
230Eight hundred five. Besides, their writers say
231King Pepin, which deposèd Childeric,
232Did as heir general, being descended
233Of Blithild, which was daughter to King Chlothar,
234Make claim and title to the crown of France.
235Hugh Capet also, who usurped the crown
236Of Charles the Duke of Lorraine, sole heir male
237Of the true line and stock of Charles the Great,
238To find his title with some shows of truth —
239Though in pure truth it was corrupt and naught —
240Conveyed himself as th'heir to th'lady Lingare,
241Daughter to Charlemagne, who was the son
242To Louis the emperor, and Louis the son
243Of Charles the Great. Also King Louis the Tenth,
244Who was sole heir to the usurper Capet,
245Could not keep quiet in his conscience
246Wearing the crown of France till satisfied
247That fair Queen Isabelle, his grandmother,
248Was lineal of the lady Ermengarde,
249Daughter to Charles the foresaid Duke of Lorraine,
250By the which marriage the line of Charles the Great
251Was reunited to the crown of France.
252So that, as clear as is the summer's sun,
253King Pepin's title and Hugh Capet's claim,
254King Louis his satisfaction, all appear
255To hold in right and title of the female;
256So do the kings of France unto this day,
257Howbeit they would hold up this Salic law
258To bar your highness claiming from the female,
259And rather choose to hide them in a net
260Than amply to embar their crooked titles
261Usurped from you and your progenitors.

KING HENRY

262May I with right and conscience make this claim?

CANTERBURY

263The sin upon my head, dread sovereign.
264For in the book of Numbers is it writ:
265"When the man dies, let the inheritance
266Descend unto the daughter." Gracious lord,
267Stand for your own. Unwind your bloody flag,
268Look back into your mighty ancestors.
269Go, my dread lord, to your great-grandsire's tomb,
270From whom you claim; invoke his warlike spirit,
271And your great-uncle's, Edward the Black Prince,
272Who on the French ground played a tragedy,
273Making defeat on the full power of France
274Whiles his most mighty father on a hill
275Stood smiling to behold his lion's whelp
276Forage in blood of French nobility.
277Oh, noble English, that could entertain
278With half their forces the full pride of France
279And let another half stand laughing by,
280All out of work and cold for action!

ELY

281Awake remembrance of these valiant dead,
282And with your puissant arm renew their feats.
283You are their heir, you sit upon their throne,
284The blood and courage that renownèd them
285Runs in your veins, and my thrice-puissant liege
286Is in the very May-morn of his youth,
287Ripe for exploits and mighty enterprises.

EXETER

288Your brother kings and monarchs of the earth
289Do all expect that you should rouse yourself
290As did the former lions of your blood.

WESTMORLAND

291They know your grace hath cause, and means, and might;
292So hath your highness. Never king of England
293Had nobles richer and more loyal subjects,
294Whose hearts have left their bodies here in England
295And lie pavilioned in the fields of France.

CANTERBURY

296Oh, let their bodies follow, my dear liege,
297With bloods and sword and fire to win your right.
298In aid whereof, we of the spiritualty
299Will raise your highness such a mighty sum
300As never did the clergy at one time
301Bring in to any of your ancestors.

KING HENRY

302We must not only arm t'invade the French,
303But lay down our proportions to defend
304Against the Scot, who will make road upon us
305With all advantages.

CANTERBURY

306They of those marches, gracious sovereign,
307Shall be a wall sufficient to defend
308Our inland from the pilfering borderers.

KING HENRY

309We do not mean the coursing snatchers only,
310But fear the main intendment of the Scot,
311Who hath been still a giddy neighbor to us.
312For you shall read that my great-grandfather
313Never went with his forces into France
314But that the Scot on his unfurnished kingdom
315Came pouring like the tide into a breach
316With ample and brim fullness of his force,
317Galling the gleanèd land with hot assays,
318Girding with grievous siege castles and towns,
319That England, being empty of defense,
320Hath shook and trembled at th'ill neighborhood.

CANTERBURY

321She hath been then more feared than harmed, my liege.
322For hear her but exampled by herself:
323When all her chivalry hath been in France
324And she a mourning widow of her nobles,
325She hath herself not only well defended,
326But taken and impounded as a stray
327The king of Scots, whom she did send to France
328To fill King Edward's fame with prisoner kings
329And make their chronicle as rich with praise
330As is the ooze and bottom of the sea
331With sunken wreck and sumless treasuries.

ELY

332But there's a saying very old and true:
333"If that you will France win,
334Then with Scotland first begin."
335For once the eagle England being in prey,
336To her unguarded nest the weasel Scot
337Comes sneaking, and so sucks her princely eggs,
338Playing the mouse in absence of the cat,
339To 'tame and havoc more than she can eat.

EXETER

340It follows then the cat must stay at home,
341Yet that is but a crushed necessity,
342Since we have locks to safeguard necessaries
343And pretty traps to catch the petty thieves.
344While that the armèd hand doth fight abroad,
345Th'advisèd head defends itself at home.
346For government, though high and low and lower
347Put into parts, doth keep in one consent,
348Congreeing in a full and natural close
349Like music.

CANTERBURY

Like music. Therefore doth heaven divide
350The state of man in divers functions,
351Setting endeavor in continual motion,
352To which is fixèd, as an aim or butt,
353Obedience. For so work the honeybees,
354Creatures that by a rule in nature teach
355The act of order to a peopled kingdom.
356They have a king, and officers of sorts,
357Where some like magistrates correct at home;
358Others like merchants venture trade abroad;
359Others, like soldiers armèd in their stings,
360Make boot upon the summer's velvet buds,
361Which pillage they with merry march bring home
362To the tent-royal of their emperor,
363Who, busied in his majesties, surveys
364The singing masons building roofs of gold,
365The civil citizens kneading up the honey,
366The poor mechanic porters crowding in
367Their heavy burdens at his narrow gate,
368The sad-eyed justice with his surly hum
369Delivering o'er to executors pale
370The lazy yawning drone. I this infer:
371That many things, having full reference
372To one consent, may work contrariously.
373As many arrows loosèd several ways
374Come to one mark, as many ways meet in one town,
375As many fresh streams meet in one salt sea,
376As many lines close in the dial's center,
377So may a thousand actions once afoot
378End in one purpose, and be all well borne
379Without defeat. Therefore to France, my liege.
380Divide your happy England into four,
381Whereof take you one quarter into France,
382And you withal shall make all Gallia shake.
383If we, with thrice such powers left at home,
384Cannot defend our own doors from the dog,
385Let us be worried, and our nation lose
386The name of hardiness and policy.

KING HENRY

387Call in the messengers sent from the dauphin.

[Exit attendant.]

388Now are we well resolved, and by God's help
389And yours, the noble sinews of our power,
390France being ours, we'll bend it to our awe,
391Or break it all to pieces. Or there we'll sit,
392Ruling in large and ample empery
393O'er France and all her almost kingly dukedoms,
394Or lay these bones in an unworthy urn,
395Tombless, with no remembrance over them.
396Either our history shall with full mouth
397Speak freely of our acts, or else our grave,
398Like Turkish mute, shall have a tongueless mouth,
399Not worshipped with a waxen epitaph. —

Enter Ambassadors of France.

400Now are we well prepared to know the pleasure
401Of our fair cousin dauphin, for we hear
402Your greeting is from him, not from the king.

AMBASSADOR

403May't please your majesty to give us leave
404Freely to render what we have in charge,
405Or shall we sparingly show you far off
406The dauphin's meaning and our embassy?

KING HENRY

407We are no tyrant, but a Christian king,
408Unto whose grace our passion is as subject
409As is our wretches fettered in our prisons.
410Therefore with frank and with uncurbèd plainness
411Tell us the dauphin's mind.

AMBASSADOR

Tell us the dauphin's mind. Thus, then, in few:
412Your highness, lately sending into France,
413Did claim some certain dukedoms in the right
414Of your great predecessor, King Edward the Third.
415In answer of which claim, the prince our master
416Says that you savor too much of your youth,
417And bids you be advised, there's naught in France
418That can be with a nimble galliard won;
419You cannot revel into dukedoms there.
420He therefore sends you, meeter for your spirit,
421This tun of treasure, and in lieu of this,
422Desires you let the dukedoms that you claim
423Hear no more of you. This the dauphin speaks.

KING HENRY

424What treasure, uncle?

EXETER

What treasure, uncle? Tennis balls, my liege.

KING HENRY

425We are glad the dauphin is so pleasant with us.
426His present and your pains we thank you for.
427When we have matched our rackets to these balls,
428We will in France, by God's grace, play a set
429Shall strike his father's crown into the hazard.
430Tell him he hath made a match with such a wrangler
431That all the courts of France will be disturbed
432With chases. And we understand him well,
433How he comes o'er us with our wilder days,
434Not measuring what use we made of them.
435We never valued this poor seat of England,
436And therefore living hence, did give ourself
437To barbarous license, as 'tis ever common
438That men are merriest when they are from home.
439But tell the dauphin I will keep my state,
440Be like a king, and show my sail of greatness
441When I do rouse me in my throne of France.
442For that I have laid by my majesty
443And plodded like a man for working days,
444But I will rise there with so full a glory
445That I will dazzle all the eyes of France,
446Yea, strike the dauphin blind to look on us.
447And tell the pleasant prince this mock of his
448Hath turned his balls to gunstones, and his soul
449Shall stand sore chargèd for the wasteful vengeance
450That shall fly with them: for many a thousand widows
451Shall this, his mock, mock out of their dear husbands,
452Mock mothers from their sons, mock castles down,
453And some are yet ungotten and unborn
454That shall have cause to curse the dauphin's scorn.
455But this lies all within the will of God,
456To whom I do appeal, and in whose name
457Tell you the dauphin I am coming on
458To venge me as I may, and to put forth
459My rightful hand in a well-hallowed cause.
460So get you hence in peace, and tell the dauphin
461His jest will savor but of shallow wit
462When thousands weep more than did laugh at it. —
463Convey them with safe conduct. — Fare you well.

Exeunt Ambassadors.

EXETER

464This was a merry message.

KING HENRY

465We hope to make the sender blush at it.
466Therefore, my lords, omit no happy hour
467That may give furtherance to our expedition,
468For we have now no thought in us but France,
469Save those to God, that run before our business.
470Therefore let our proportions for these wars
471Be soon collected, and all things thought upon
472That may with reasonable swiftness add
473More feathers to our wings, for, God before,
474We'll chide this dauphin at his father's door.
475Therefore let every man now task his thought
476That this fair action may on foot be brought.

Exeunt.

Act II

[2.0]

Flourish. Enter Chorus.

CHORUS

477Now all the youth of England are on fire
478And silken dalliance in the wardrobe lies.
479Now thrive the armorers, and honor's thought
480Reigns solely in the breast of every man.
481They sell the pasture now to buy the horse,
482Following the mirror of all Christian kings
483With wingèd heels, as English Mercuries.
484For now sits expectation in the air
485And hides a sword from hilts unto the point
486With crowns imperial, crowns and coronets
487Promised to Harry and his followers.
488The French, advised by good intelligence
489Of this most dreadful preparation,
490Shake in their fear, and with pale policy
491Seek to divert the English purposes.
492O England, model to thy inward greatness,
493Like little body with a mighty heart,
494What mightst thou do, that honor would thee do,
495Were all thy children kind and natural!
496But see, thy fault France hath in thee found out:
497A nest of hollow bosoms, which he fills
498With treacherous crowns; and three corrupted men,
499One, Richard, Earl of Cambridge, and the second
500Henry, Lord Scrope of Masham, and the third
501Sir Thomas Grey, knight of Northumberland,
502Have for the gilt of France — oh, guilt indeed! —
503Confirmed conspiracy with fearful France.
504And by their hands this grace of kings must die,
505If hell and treason hold their promises,
506Ere he take ship for France, and in Southampton.
507Linger your patience on, and we'll digest
508Th'abuse of distance, force a play.
509The sum is paid, the traitors are agreed,
510The king is set from London, and the scene
511Is now transported, gentles, to Southampton.
512There is the playhouse now, there must you sit,
513And thence to France shall we convey you safe
514And bring you back, charming the narrow seas
515To give you gentle pass; for if we may,
516We'll not offend one stomach with our play.
517But when the king come forth, and not till then,
518Unto Southampton do we shift our scene.

Exit

[2.1]

Enter Corporal Nym and Lieutenant Bardolph.

BARDOLPH

Well met, Corporal Nym.

NYM

Good morrow, Lieutenant Bardolph.

BARDOLPH

What, are Ancient Pistol and you friends yet?

NYM

For my part, I care not. I say little, but when time shall serve, there shall be smiles. But that shall be as it may. I dare not fight, but I will wink and hold out mine iron. It is a simple one, but what though? It will toast cheese, and it will endure cold as another man's sword will, and there's an end.

BARDOLPH

I will bestow a breakfast to make you friends, and we'll be all three sworn brothers to France. Let’t be so, good Corporal Nym.

NYM

Faith, I will live so long as I may, that's the certain of it. And when I cannot live any longer, I will do as I may. That is my rest; that is the rendezvous of it.

BARDOLPH

It is certain, corporal, that he is married to Nell Quickly, and certainly she did you wrong, for you were troth-plight to her.

NYM

I cannot tell; things must be as they may. Men may sleep, and they may have their throats about them at that time, and some say knives have edges. It must be as it may. Though patience be a tired mare, yet she will plod. There must be conclusions. Well, I cannot tell.

Enter Pistol and [Hostess, formerly Mistress] Quickly.

BARDOLPH

Here comes Ancient Pistol and his wife. Good corporal, be patient here. — How now, mine host Pistol?

PISTOL

Base tyke, call'st thou me host? Now by this hand I swear I scorn the term! Nor shall my Nell keep lodgers.

HOSTESS

No, by my troth, not long, for we cannot lodge and board a dozen or fourteen gentlewomen that live honestly by the prick of their needles, but it will be thought we keep a bawdy house straight. [Nym draws his sword.] Oh, welladay, lady, if he be not hewn now, we shall see willful adultery and murder committed!

BARDOLPH

Good lieutenant, good corporal, offer nothing here.

NYM

Pish.

PISTOL

Pish for thee, Iceland dog, thou prick-eared cur of Iceland.

HOSTESS

Good Corporal Nym, show thy valor and put up your sword.

NYM

Will you shog off? [To Pistol] I would have you solus.

PISTOL

" Solus, " egregious dog? O viper vile! The " solus " in thy most marvelous face. The " solus " in thy teeth, and in thy throat, and in thy hateful lungs, yea in thy maw, perdy. And which is worse, within thy nasty mouth. I do retort the " solus " in thy bowels, for I can take, and Pistol's cock is up, and flashing fire will follow.

NYM

I am not Barbason; you cannot conjure me. I have an humor to knock you indifferently well. If you grow foul with me, Pistol, I will scour you with my rapier, as I may, in fair terms. If you would walk off, I would prick your guts a little in good terms, as I may, and that's the humor of it.

PISTOL

519O braggart vile, and damnèd furious wight,
520The grave doth gape, and doting death is near,
521Therefore exhale.

[Pistol draws his sword.]

BARDOLPH

Hear me, hear me what I say. [Draws his sword (?)] He that strikes the first stroke, I'll run him up to the hilts, as I am a soldier.

PISTOL

An oath of mickle might, and fury shall abate. [They sheathe their swords.] Give me thy fist. Thy forefoot to me give. Thy spirits are most tall.

NYM

I will cut thy throat one time or other in fair terms. That is the humor of it.

PISTOL

Couple a gorge, that is the word. I defy thee again! O hound of Crete, think'st thou my spouse to get? No, to the Spital go, and from the powd'ring tub of infamy fetch forth the lazar kite of Cressid's kind, Doll Tearsheet, she by name, and her espouse. I have, and I will hold the quondam Quickly for the only she, and pauca, there's enough. Go to.

Enter the Boy.

BOY

Mine host Pistol, you must come to my master, and your hostess. He is very sick and would to bed. — Good Bardolph, put thy face between his sheets and do the office of a warming-pan. Faith, he's very ill.

BARDOLPH

Away, you rogue.
