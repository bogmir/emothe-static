Act II

[2.0]

Flourish. Enter Chorus.

CHORUS

477Now all the youth of England are on fire
478And silken dalliance in the wardrobe lies.
479Now thrive the armorers, and honor's thought
480Reigns solely in the breast of every man.
481They sell the pasture now to buy the horse,
482Following the mirror of all Christian kings
483With wingèd heels, as English Mercuries.
484For now sits expectation in the air
485And hides a sword from hilts unto the point
486With crowns imperial, crowns and coronets
487Promised to Harry and his followers.
488The French, advised by good intelligence
489Of this most dreadful preparation,
490Shake in their fear, and with pale policy
491Seek to divert the English purposes.
492O England, model to thy inward greatness,
493Like little body with a mighty heart,
494What mightst thou do, that honor would thee do,
495Were all thy children kind and natural!
496But see, thy fault France hath in thee found out:
497A nest of hollow bosoms, which he fills
498With treacherous crowns; and three corrupted men,
499One, Richard, Earl of Cambridge, and the second
500Henry, Lord Scrope of Masham, and the third
501Sir Thomas Grey, knight of Northumberland,
502Have for the gilt of France — oh, guilt indeed! —
503Confirmed conspiracy with fearful France.
504And by their hands this grace of kings must die,
505If hell and treason hold their promises,
506Ere he take ship for France, and in Southampton.
507Linger your patience on, and we'll digest
508Th'abuse of distance, force a play.
509The sum is paid, the traitors are agreed,
510The king is set from London, and the scene
511Is now transported, gentles, to Southampton.
512There is the playhouse now, there must you sit,
513And thence to France shall we convey you safe
514And bring you back, charming the narrow seas
515To give you gentle pass; for if we may,
516We'll not offend one stomach with our play.
517But when the king come forth, and not till then,
518Unto Southampton do we shift our scene.

Exit

[2.1]

Enter Corporal Nym and Lieutenant Bardolph.

BARDOLPH

Well met, Corporal Nym.

NYM

Good morrow, Lieutenant Bardolph.

BARDOLPH

What, are Ancient Pistol and you friends yet?

NYM

For my part, I care not. I say little, but when time shall serve, there shall be smiles. But that shall be as it may. I dare not fight, but I will wink and hold out mine iron. It is a simple one, but what though? It will toast cheese, and it will endure cold as another man's sword will, and there's an end.

BARDOLPH

I will bestow a breakfast to make you friends, and we'll be all three sworn brothers to France. Let’t be so, good Corporal Nym.

NYM

Faith, I will live so long as I may, that's the certain of it. And when I cannot live any longer, I will do as I may. That is my rest; that is the rendezvous of it.

BARDOLPH

It is certain, corporal, that he is married to Nell Quickly, and certainly she did you wrong, for you were troth-plight to her.

NYM

I cannot tell; things must be as they may. Men may sleep, and they may have their throats about them at that time, and some say knives have edges. It must be as it may. Though patience be a tired mare, yet she will plod. There must be conclusions. Well, I cannot tell.

Enter Pistol and [Hostess, formerly Mistress] Quickly.

BARDOLPH

Here comes Ancient Pistol and his wife. Good corporal, be patient here. — How now, mine host Pistol?

PISTOL

Base tyke, call'st thou me host? Now by this hand I swear I scorn the term! Nor shall my Nell keep lodgers.

HOSTESS

No, by my troth, not long, for we cannot lodge and board a dozen or fourteen gentlewomen that live honestly by the prick of their needles, but it will be thought we keep a bawdy house straight. [Nym draws his sword.] Oh, welladay, lady, if he be not hewn now, we shall see willful adultery and murder committed!

BARDOLPH

Good lieutenant, good corporal, offer nothing here.

NYM

Pish.

PISTOL

Pish for thee, Iceland dog, thou prick-eared cur of Iceland.

HOSTESS

Good Corporal Nym, show thy valor and put up your sword.

NYM

Will you shog off? [To Pistol] I would have you solus.

PISTOL

" Solus, " egregious dog? O viper vile! The " solus " in thy most marvelous face. The " solus " in thy teeth, and in thy throat, and in thy hateful lungs, yea in thy maw, perdy. And which is worse, within thy nasty mouth. I do retort the " solus " in thy bowels, for I can take, and Pistol's cock is up, and flashing fire will follow.

NYM

I am not Barbason; you cannot conjure me. I have an humor to knock you indifferently well. If you grow foul with me, Pistol, I will scour you with my rapier, as I may, in fair terms. If you would walk off, I would prick your guts a little in good terms, as I may, and that's the humor of it.

PISTOL

519O braggart vile, and damnèd furious wight,
520The grave doth gape, and doting death is near,
521Therefore exhale.

[Pistol draws his sword.]

BARDOLPH

Hear me, hear me what I say. [Draws his sword (?)] He that strikes the first stroke, I'll run him up to the hilts, as I am a soldier.

PISTOL

An oath of mickle might, and fury shall abate. [They sheathe their swords.] Give me thy fist. Thy forefoot to me give. Thy spirits are most tall.

NYM

I will cut thy throat one time or other in fair terms. That is the humor of it.

PISTOL

Couple a gorge, that is the word. I defy thee again! O hound of Crete, think'st thou my spouse to get? No, to the Spital go, and from the powd'ring tub of infamy fetch forth the lazar kite of Cressid's kind, Doll Tearsheet, she by name, and her espouse. I have, and I will hold the quondam Quickly for the only she, and pauca, there's enough. Go to.

Enter the Boy.

BOY

Mine host Pistol, you must come to my master, and your hostess. He is very sick and would to bed. — Good Bardolph, put thy face between his sheets and do the office of a warming-pan. Faith, he's very ill.

BARDOLPH

Away, you rogue.

HOSTESS

By my troth, he'll yield the crow a pudding one of these days. The king has killed his heart. Good husband, come home presently.

Exeunt [Boy and Hostess.]

BARDOLPH

Come, shall I make you two friends? We must to France together. Why the devil should we keep knives to cut one another's throats?

PISTOL

Let floods o'erswell, and fiends for food howl on.

NYM

[To Pistol] You'll pay me the eight shillings I won of you at betting?

PISTOL

Base is the slave that pays.

NYM

That now I will have. That's the humor of it.

PISTOL

As manhood shall compound. Push home.

[They] draw [their swords.]

BARDOLPH

[Drawing his sword (?)] By this sword, he that makes the first thrust, I'll kill him. By this sword, I will.

PISTOL

Sword is an oath, and oaths must have their course.

[Sheathes his sword]

BARDOLPH

Corporal Nym, an thou wilt be friends, be friends. An thou wilt not, why then be enemies with me, too. Prithee, put up.

[Nym and Bardolph sheathe their swords. (?)]

PISTOL

A noble shalt thou have, and present pay. And liquor likewise will I give to thee, and friendship shall combine, and brotherhood. I'll live by Nym and Nym shall live by me. Is not this just? For I shall sutler be unto the camp, and profits will accrue. Give me thy hand.

NYM

I shall have my noble?

PISTOL

In cash most justly paid.

NYM

Well, then that's the humor of't.

[Pistol and Nym shake hands. (?)]

Enter Hostess.

HOSTESS

As ever you come of women, come in quickly to Sir John. A poor heart, he is so shaked of a burning quotidian tertian that it is most lamentable to behold. Sweet men, come to him.

[Exit.]

NYM

The king hath run bad humors on the knight. That's the even of it.

PISTOL

Nym, thou hast spoke the right. His heart is fracted and corroborate.

NYM

The king is a good king, but it must be as it may. He passes some humors and careers.

PISTOL

Let us condole the knight, for lambkins, we will live.

[Exeunt.]

[2.2]

Enter Exeter, Bedford, and Westmorland.

BEDFORD

522'Fore God, his grace is bold to trust these traitors.

EXETER

523They shall be apprehended by and by.

WESTMORLAND

524How smooth and even they do bear themselves,
525As if allegiance in their bosoms sat
526Crownèd with faith and constant loyalty.

BEDFORD

527The king hath note of all that they intend
528By interception, which they dream not of.

EXETER

529Nay, but the man that was his bedfellow,
530Whom he hath dulled and cloyed with gracious favors,
531That he should for a foreign purse so sell
532His sovereign's life to death and treachery!

Sound trumpets. Enter the King, Scrope, Cambridge, and Grey[, and attendants.]

KING HENRY

533Now sits the wind fair, and we will aboard. —
534My lord of Cambridge, and my kind lord of Masham,
535And you, my gentle knight, give me your thoughts:
536Think you not that the powers we bear with us
537Will cut their passage through the force of France,
538Doing the execution and the act
539For which we have in head assembled them?

SCROPE

540No doubt, my liege, if each man do his best.

KING HENRY

541I doubt not that, since we are well persuaded
542We carry not a heart with us from hence
543That grows not in a fair consent with ours,
544Nor leave not one behind that doth not wish
545Success and conquest to attend on us.

CAMBRIDGE

546Never was monarch better feared and loved
547Than is your majesty. There's not, I think, a subject
548That sits in heart-grief and uneasiness
549Under the sweet shade of your government.

GREY

550True. Those that were your father's enemies
551Have steeped their galls in honey and do serve you
552With hearts create of duty and of zeal.

KING HENRY

553We therefore have great cause of thankfulness,
554And shall forget the office of our hand
555Sooner than quittance of desert and merit,
556According to the weight and worthiness.

SCROPE

557So service shall with steelèd sinews toil,
558And labor shall refresh itself with hope
559To do your grace incessant services.

KING HENRY

560We judge no less. Uncle of Exeter,
561Enlarge the man committed yesterday
562That railed against our person. We consider
563It was excess of wine that set him on,
564And on his more advice we pardon him.

SCROPE

565That's mercy, but too much security.
566Let him be punished, sovereign, lest example
567Breed, by his sufferance, more of such a kind.

KING HENRY

568Oh, let us yet be merciful.

CAMBRIDGE

569So may your highness, and yet punish too.

GREY

570Sir, you show great mercy if you give him life
571After the taste of much correction.

KING HENRY

572Alas, your too much love and care of me
573Are heavy orisons 'gainst this poor wretch.
574If little faults proceeding on distemper
575Shall not be winked at, how shall we stretch our eye
576When capital crimes, chewed, swallowed, and digested,
577Appear before us? We'll yet enlarge that man,
578Though Cambridge, Scrope, and Grey, in their dear care
579And tender preservation of our person
580Would have him punished. And now to our French causes. —
581Who are the late commissioners?

CAMBRIDGE

Who are the late commissioners? I one, my lord.
582Your highness bade me ask for it today.

SCROPE

583So did you me, my liege.

GREY

584And I, my royal sovereign.

KING HENRY

585[Giving them papers] Then Richard Earl of Cambridge, there is yours.
586There yours, Lord Scrope of Masham; and sir knight,
587Grey of Northumberland, this same is yours.
588Read them and know I know your worthiness. —
589My lord of Westmorland, and uncle Exeter,
590We will aboard to night. — Why, how now, gentlemen?
591What see you in those papers that you lose
592So much complexion? Look ye how they change:
593Their cheeks are paper! Why, what read you there
594That have so cowarded and chased your blood
595Out of appearance?

CAMBRIDGE

Out of appearance? I do confess my fault,
596And do submit me to your highness' mercy.

GREY, SCROPE

597To which we all appeal.

KING HENRY

598The mercy that was quick in us but late
599By your own counsel is suppressed and killed.
600You must not dare for shame to talk of mercy,
601For your own reasons turn into your bosoms
602As dogs upon their masters, worrying you. —
603See you, my princes and my noble peers,
604These English monsters: my lord of Cambridge here,
605You know how apt our love was to accord
606To furnish him with all appurtenants
607Belonging to his honor. And this man
608Hath for a few light crowns lightly conspired
609And sworn unto the practices of France
610To kill us here in Hampton. To the which
611This knight, no less for bounty bound to us
612Than Cambridge is, hath likewise sworn.— But oh,
613What shall I say to thee, Lord Scrope, thou cruel,
614Ingrateful, savage and inhuman creature?
615Thou that didst bear the key of all my counsels,
616That knew'st the very bottom of my soul,
617That almost mightst have coined me into gold,
618Wouldst thou have practiced on me for thy use?
619May it be possible that foreign hire
620Could out of thee extract one spark of evil
621That might annoy my finger? 'Tis so strange
622That though the truth of it stands off as gross
623As black and white, my eye will scarcely see it.
624Treason and murder ever kept together
625As two yoke-devils sworn to either's purpose,
626Working so grossly in unnatural cause
627That admiration did not whoop at them.
628But thou, 'gainst all proportion, didst bring in
629Wonder to wait on treason and on murder,
630And whatsoever cunning fiend it was
631That wrought upon thee so preposterously
632Hath got the voice in hell for excellence;
633And other devils, that suggest by treasons,
634Do botch and bungle up damnation
635With patches, colors, and with forms being fetched
636From glist'ring semblances of piety.
637But he that tempered thee, bade thee stand up,
638Gave thee no instance why thou shouldst do treason
639Unless to dub thee with the name of traitor.
640If that same demon that hath gulled thee thus
641Should with his lion gait walk the whole world,
642He might return to vasty Tartar back
643And tell the legions, "I can never win
644A soul so easy as that Englishman's."
645Oh, how hast thou with jealousy infected
646The sweetness of affiance! Show men dutiful?
647Why so didst thou. Seem they grave and learnèd?
648Why so didst thou. Come they of noble family?
649Why so didst thou. Seem they religious?
650Why so didst thou. Or are they spare in diet,
651Free from gross passion or of mirth or anger,
652Constant in spirit, not swerving with the blood,
653Garnished and decked in modest complement,
654Not working with the eye without the ear,
655And but in purgèd judgment trusting neither?
656Such and so finely bolted didst thou seem.
657And thus thy fall hath left a kind of blot
658To make the full-fraught man, and best, indued
659With some suspicion. I will weep for thee,
660For this revolt of thine, methinks, is like
661Another fall of man.— Their faults are open.
662Arrest them to the answer of the law,
663And God acquit them of their practices.

EXETER

I arrest thee of high treason, by the name of Richard Earl of Cambridge. I arrest thee of high treason, by the name of Henry Lord Scrope of Masham. I arrest thee of high treason, by the name of Thomas Grey, knight of Northumberland.

SCROPE

664Our purposes God justly hath discovered,
665And I repent my fault more than my death,
666Which I beseech your highness to forgive,
667Although my body pay the price of it.

CAMBRIDGE

668For me, the gold of France did not seduce,
669Although I did admit it as a motive
670The sooner to effect what I intended.
671But God be thankèd for prevention,
672Which I in sufferance heartily will rejoice,
673Beseeching God and you to pardon me.

GREY

674Never did faithful subject more rejoice
675At the discovery of most dangerous treason
676Than I do at this hour joy o'er myself,
677Prevented from a damnèd enterprise.
678My fault, but not my body, pardon, sovereign.

KING HENRY

679God quit you in his mercy. Hear your sentence:
680You have conspired against our royal person,
681Joined with an enemy proclaimed and from his coffers
682Received the golden earnest of our death,
683Wherein you would have sold your king to slaughter,
684His princes and his peers to servitude,
685His subjects to oppression and contempt,
686And his whole kingdom into desolation.
687Touching our person seek we no revenge,
688But we our kingdom's safety must so tender,
689Whose ruin you sought, that to her laws
690We do deliver you. Get you therefore hence,
691Poor miserable wretches, to your death,
692The taste whereof God of his mercy give
693You patience to endure, and true repentance
694Of all your dear offences. — Bear them hence.

[Exeunt traitors, guarded.]

695Now, lords, for France, the enterprise whereof
696Shall be to you as us like glorious.
697We doubt not of a fair and lucky war,
698Since God so graciously hath brought to light
699This dangerous treason lurking in our way
700To hinder our beginnings. We doubt not now
701But every rub is smoothèd on our way.
702Then forth, dear countrymen. Let us deliver
703Our puissance into the hand of God,
704Putting it straight in expedition.
705Cheerly to sea; the signs of war advance.
706No king of England if not king of France.

Flourish. [Exeunt.]

[2.3]

Enter Pistol, Nym, Bardolph, Boy, and Hostess.

HOSTESS

Prithee, honey-sweet husband, let me bring thee to Staines.

PISTOL

No, for my manly heart doth earn. Bardolph, be blithe. Nym, rouse thy vaunting veins. Boy, bristle thy courage up. For Falstaff he is dead, and we must earn therefore.

BARDOLPH

Would I were with him wheresome'er he is, either in heaven or in hell.

HOSTESS

Nay, sure he's not in hell. He's in Arthur's bosom, if ever man went to Arthur's bosom. A made a finer end, and went away an it had been any christom child. A parted ev'n just between twelve and one, ev'n at the turning o'th'tide. For after I saw him fumble with the sheets, and play with flowers, and smile upon his finger's end, I knew there was but one way. For his nose was as sharp as a pen, and a babbled of green fields. "How now, Sir John?" quoth I. "What, man, be o' good cheer!" So a cried out, "God, God, God," three or four times. Now I, to comfort him, bid him a should not think of God; I hoped there was no need to trouble himself with any such thoughts yet. So a bade me lay more clothes on his feet. I put my hand into the bed and felt them, and they were as cold as any stone. Then I felt to his knees, and so up-peered, and upward and all was as cold as any stone.

NYM

They say he cried out of sack.

HOSTESS

Ay, that a did.

BARDOLPH

And of women.

HOSTESS

Nay, that a did not.

BOY

Yes, that a did, and said they were devils incarnate.

HOSTESS

A could never abide carnation; 'twas a color he never liked.

BOY

A said once the devil would have him about women.

HOSTESS

A did in some sort, indeed, handle women, but then he was rheumatic, and talked of the whore of Babylon.

BOY

Do you not remember a saw a flea stick upon Bardolph's nose, and a said it was a black soul burning in hell?

BARDOLPH

Well, the fuel is gone that maintained that fire. That's all the riches I got in his service.

NYM

Shall we shog? The king will be gone from Southampton.

PISTOL

Come, let's away. My love, give me thy lips. [Kisses her] Look to my chattels and my moveables. Let senses rule. The world is pitch-and-pay. Trust none, for oaths are straws, men's faiths are wafer-cakes, and Holdfast is the only dog, my duck, therefore caveto be thy counselor. Go, clear thy crystals. Yoke-fellows in arms, let us to France like horse-leeches, my boys: to suck, to suck, the very blood to suck!

BOY

And that's but unwholesome food, they say.

PISTOL

Touch her soft mouth and march.

BARDOLPH

[Kisses her] Farewell, hostess.

NYM

I cannot kiss, that is the humor of it, but adieu.

PISTOL

Let housewifery appear. Keep close, I thee command.

HOSTESS

Farewell. Adieu.

Exeunt.

[2.4]

Flourish. Enter the French King, the Dauphin, the Dukes of Berry and Brittany[, and the Constable of France].

FRENCH KING

707Thus comes the English with full power upon us,
708And more than carefully it us concerns
709To answer royally in our defenses.
710Therefore the Dukes of Berry and of Brittany,
711Of Brabant and of Orléans shall make forth,
712And you, Prince Dauphin, with all swift dispatch
713To line and new repair our towns of war
714With men of courage and with means defendant,
715For England his approaches makes as fierce
716As waters to the sucking of a gulf.
717It fits us then to be as provident
718As fear may teach us, out of late examples
719Left by the fatal and neglected English
720Upon our fields.

DAUPHIN

Upon our fields. My most redoubted father,
721It is most meet we arm us 'gainst the foe,
722For peace itself should not so dull a kingdom,
723Though war nor no known quarrel were in question,
724But that defenses, musters, preparations
725Should be maintained, assembled, and collected
726As were a war in expectation.
727Therefore I say 'tis meet we all go forth
728To view the sick and feeble parts of France.
729And let us do it with no show of fear,
730No, with no more than if we heard that England
731Were busied with a Whitsun morris dance.
732For, my good liege, she is so idly kinged,
733Her scepter so fantastically borne
734By a vain, giddy, shallow, humorous youth,
735That fear attends her not.

CONSTABLE

That fear attends her not. Oh, peace, Prince Dauphin.
736You are too much mistaken in this king.
737Question your grace the late ambassadors —
738With what great state he heard their embassy,
739How well supplied with noble counselors,
740How modest in exception, and withal
741How terrible in constant resolution —
742And you shall find his vanities forespent
743Were but the outside of the Roman Brutus,
744Covering discretion with a coat of folly,
745As gardeners do with ordure hide those roots
746That shall first spring and be most delicate.

DAUPHIN

747Well, 'tis not so, my lord high constable.
748But though we think it so, it is no matter.
749In cases of defense, 'tis best to weigh
750The enemy more mighty than he seems.
751So the proportions of defense are filled,
752Which of a weak and niggardly projection
753Doth like a miser spoil his coat with scanting
754A little cloth.

FRENCH KING

A little cloth. Think we King Harry strong,
755And princes, look you strongly arm to meet him.
756The kindred of him hath been fleshed upon us,
757And he is bred out of that bloody strain
758That haunted us in our familiar paths:
759Witness our too much memorable shame
760When Crécy battle fatally was struck,
761And all our princes captived by the hand
762Of that black name, Edward, Black Prince of Wales,
763Whiles that his mountain sire on mountain standing
764Up in the air, crowned with the golden sun,
765Saw his heroical seed and smiled to see him
766Mangle the work of nature, and deface
767The patterns that by God and by French fathers
768Had twenty years been made. This is a stem
769Of that victorious stock, and let us fear
770The native mightiness and fate of him.

Enter a Messenger.

MESSENGER

771Ambassadors from Harry, King of England,
772Do crave admittance to your majesty.

FRENCH KING

773We'll give them present audience; go and bring them.

[Exit Messenger.]

774You see this chase is hotly followed, friends.

DAUPHIN

775Turn head and stop pursuit, for coward dogs
776Most spend their mouths when what they seem to threaten
777Runs far before them. Good my sovereign,
778Take up the English short, and let them know
779Of what a monarchy you are the head.
780Self-love, my liege, is not so vile a sin
781As self-neglecting.

Enter Exeter.

FRENCH KING

As self-neglecting. From our brother of England?

EXETER

782From him, and thus he greets your majesty:
783He wills you in the name of God almighty
784That you divest yourself, and lay apart
785The borrowed glories that by gift of heaven,
786By law of nature and of nations longs
787To him and to his heirs, namely the crown
788And all wide-stretchèd honors that pertain
789By custom and the ordinance of times
790Unto the crown of France. That you may know
791'Tis no sinister nor no awkward claim
792Picked from the wormholes of long-vanished days,
793Nor from the dust of old oblivion raked,
794He sends you this most memorable line,

[Gives the French King a paper]

795In every branch truly demonstrative,
796Willing you overlook this pedigree.
797And when you find him evenly derived
798From his most famed of famous ancestors,
799Edward the Third, he bids you then resign
800Your crown and kingdom, indirectly held
801From him, the native and true challenger.

FRENCH KING

802Or else what follows?

EXETER

803Bloody constraint: for if you hide the crown
804Even in your hearts, there will he rake for it.
805Therefore in fierce tempest is he coming,
806In thunder and in earthquake, like a Jove,
807That if requiring fail, he will compel.
808And bids you in the bowels of the Lord
809Deliver up the crown, and to take mercy
810On the poor souls for whom this hungry war
811Opens his vasty jaws, and on your head
812Turning the widows' tears, the orphans' cries,
813The dead men's blood, the privy maidens' groans
814For husbands, fathers, and betrothèd lovers
815That shall be swallowed in this controversy.
816This is his claim, his threat'ning, and my message,
817Unless the dauphin be in presence here,
818To whom expressly I bring greeting too.

FRENCH KING

819For us, we will consider of this further.
820Tomorrow shall you bear our full intent
821Back to our brother of England.

DAUPHIN

Back to our brother of England. For the dauphin,
822I stand here for him. What to him from England?

EXETER

823Scorn and defiance, slight regard, contempt,
824And anything that may not misbecome
825The mighty sender doth he prize you at.
826Thus says my king: an if your father's highness
827Do not, in grant of all demands at large,
828Sweeten the bitter mock you sent his majesty,
829He'll call you to so hot an answer of it
830That caves and womby vaultages of France
831Shall chide your trespass and return your mock
832In second accent of his ordinance.

DAUPHIN

833Say if my father render fair return
834It is against my will, for I desire
835Nothing but odds with England. To that end,
836As matching to his youth and vanity,
837I did present him with the Paris balls.

EXETER

838He'll make your Paris Louvre shake for it,
839Were it the mistress court of mighty Europe.
840And be assured, you'll find a difference,
841As we his subjects have in wonder found,
842Between the promise of his greener days
843And these he masters now. Now he weighs time
844Even to the utmost grain. That you shall read
845In your own losses, if he stay in France.

FRENCH KING

846Tomorrow shall you know our mind at full.

Flourish.

EXETER

847Dispatch us with all speed, lest that our king
848Come here himself to question our delay,
849For he is footed in this land already.

FRENCH KING

850You shall be soon dispatched with fair conditions.
851A night is but small breath and little pause
852To answer matters of this consequence.

Exeunt.
