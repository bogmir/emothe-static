Act III

[III.i]

Enter [D’Amville with] the funeral of Montferrers.

D'Amville.

1Set down the body. Pay earth what she lent,
2But she shall bear a living monument
3To let succeeding ages truly know
4That she is satisfy’d what he did owe,
5Both principal and use, because his worth
6Was better at his death than at his birth.

A dead march. Enter the funeral of Charlemont as a soldier.

7And with his body place that memory
8Of noble Charlemont, his worthy son,
9And give their graves the rites that do belong
10To soldiers. They were soldiers both. The father
11Held open war with sin, the son with blood,
12This in a war more gallant, that more good.

The first volley.

13There place their arms, and here their epitaphs,
14And may these lines survive the last of graves:

The Epitaph of Montferrers.

15Here lie the ashes of that earth and fire
16Whose heat and fruit did feed and warm the poor,
17And they, as if they would in sighs expire
18And into tears dissolve, his death deplore.
19He did that good freely, for goodness' sake,
20Unforc’d, for gen’rousness he held so dear
21That he fear’d none but him that did him make,
22And yet he serv’d him more for love than fear.
23So 's life provided that though he did die
24A sudden death, yet died not suddenly.

The Epitaph of Charlemont.

25His body lies interr’d within this would,
26Who died a young man, yet departed old,
27And in all strength of youth that man can have
28Was ready still to drop into his grave.
29For ag’d in virtue, with a youthful eye
30He welcom’d it, being still prepar’d to die,
31And living so, though young depriv’d of breath,
32He did not suffer an untimely death,
33But we may say of his brave bless’d decease:
34He died in war, and yet he died in peace.

The second volley.

35O might that fire revive the ashes of
36This phoenix! Yet the wonder would not be
37So great as he was good and wond’red at
38For that. His life's example was so true
39A practique of religion's theory
40That her divinity seem’d rather the
41Description than th' instruction of his life.
42And of his goodness was his virtuous son
43A worthy imitator. So that on
44These two herculean pillars where their arms
45Are plac’d there may be writ Non ultra . For
46Beyond their lives, as well for youth as age,
47Nor young nor old, in merit or in name,
48Shall e'er exceed their virtues or their fame.

The third volley.

49'Tis done. Thus fair accompliments make foul
50Deeds gracious. Charlemont, come now when t' wout,
51I've bury’d under these two marble stones
52Thy living hopes And thy dead father's bones.

Exeunt.

Enter Castabella mourning, to the monument of Charlemont.

Castabella.

53O thou that know’st me justly Charlemont’s,
54Though in the forc’d possession of another,
55Since from thine own free spirit we receive it
56That our affections cannot be compell’d
57Though our actions may, be not displeas’d, if on
58The altar of his tomb I sacrifice
59My tears. They are the jewels of my love
60Dissolved into grief, and fall upon
61His blasted spring as April dew upon
62A sweet young blossom shak’d before the time.

Enter Charlemont with a Servant.

Charlemont.

63Go see my trunks dispos’d of. I'll but walk
64A turn or two i' th' church and follow you.

Exit Servant.

65O, here's the fatal monument of my
66Dead father first presented to mine eye.
67What's here? In memory of Charlemont?
68Some false relation has abus’d belief.
69I am deluded. But I thank thee, Heaven.
70For ever let me be deluded thus.
71My Castabella mourning o 'er my hearse?
72Sweet Castabella, rise; I am not dead.

Castabella.

73O Heaven defend me!

Falls in a swoon.

Charlemont.

O Heaven defend me! I beshrew my rash
74And inconsid’rate passion. —Castabella!—
75That could not think —my Castabella!— that
76My sudden presence might affright her sense.
77I prithee, my affection, pardon me.

She rises.

78Reduce thy understanding to thine eye.
79Within this habit which thy misinform’d
80Conceit takes only for a shape live both
81The soul and body of thy Charlemont.

Castabella.

82I feel a substance warm and soft and moist,
83Subject to the capacity of sense.

Charlemont.

84Which spirits are not, for their essence is
85Above the nature and the order of
86Those elements whereof our senses are
87Created. Touch my lip. Why turn’st thou from me?

Castabella.

88Grief above griefs! That which should woe relieve,
89Wish’d and obtain’d, gives greater cause to grieve.

Charlemont.

90Can Castabella think it cause of grief
91That the relation of my death proves false?

Castabella.

92The presence of the person we affect,
93Being hopeless to enjoy him, makes our grief
94More passionate than if we saw him not.

Charlemont.

95Why not enjoy? Has absence chang’d thee?

Castabella.

Why not enjoy? Has absence chang’d thee? Yes,
96From maid to wife.

Charlemont.

From maid to wife. Art marry’d?

Castabella.

From maid to wife. Art marry’d? O, I am.

Charlemont.

97Marry’d! Had not my mother been a woman,
98I should protest against the chastity
99Of all thy sex. How can the merchant or
100The Mariner, absent whole years, from wives
101Experienc’d in the satisfaction of
102Desire, promise themselves to find their sheets
103Unspotted with adultery at their
104Return, when you that never had the sense
105Of actual temptation could not stay
106A few short months?

Castabella.

A few short months? O, do but hear me speak.

Charlemont.

107But thou wert wise and didst consider that
108A soldier might be maim’d and so perhaps
109Lose his hability to please thee.

Castabella.

Lose his hability to please thee. No.
110That weakness pleases me in him I have.

Charlemont.

111What, marry’d to a man unable too?
112O strange incontinence! Why, was thy blood
113Increas’d to such a pleurisy of lust,
114That of necessity there must a vein
115Be open’d, though by one that had no skill
116To do't?

Castabella.

To do't? Sir, I beseech you hear me.

Charlemont.

To do't? Sir, I beseech you hear me. Speak.

Castabella.

117Heav’n knows I am unguilty of this act.

Charlemont.

118Why, wert thou forc’d to do 't?

Castabella.

Why, wert thou forc’d to do 't? Heav’n knows I was.

Charlemont.

119What villain did it?

Castabella.

What villain did it? Your uncle D'Amville.
120And he that dispossess’d my love of you
121Hath disinherited you of possession.

Charlemont.

122Disinherited? Wherein have I deserv’d
123To be depriv’d of my dear father's love?

Castabella.

124Both of his love and him. His soul's at rest,
125But here your injur’d patience may behold
126The signs of his lamented memory.

Charlemont finds his father's Monument.

127H’ has found it. When I took him for a ghost
128I could endure the torment of my fear
129More eas’ly than I can his sorrows hear.

Exit.

Charlemont.

130Of all men's griefs must mine be singular?
131Without example? Here I met my grave,
132And all men's woes are bury’d i' their graves
133But mine. In mine my miseries are born.
134I prithee, sorrow, leave a little room
135In my confounded and tormented mind
136For understanding to deliberate
137The cause or author of this accident—
138A close advantage of my absence made
139To dispossess me both of land and wife,
140And all the profit does arise to him
141By whom my absence was first mov’d and urg’d.
142These circumstances, uncle, tell me you
143Are the suspected author of those wrongs,
144Whereof the lightest is more heavy than
145The strongest patience can endure to bear.

Exit.

[III.ii]

Enter D'Amville, Sebastian, and Languebeau [Snuffe].

D'Amville.

1Now sir, your business?

Sebastian.

Now sir, your business? My annuity.

D'Amville.

2Not a denier.

Sebastian.

Not a denier. How would you ha' me live?

D'Amville.

3Why, turn crier. Cannot you turn crier?

Sebastian.

4Yes.

D'Amville.

Yes. Then do so; y' have a good voice for 't.
5Y' are excellent at crying of a rape.

Sebastian.

Sir, I confess in particular respect to yourself I was some-what forgetful. Gen’ral honesty possessed me.

D'Amville.

7Go, th’ art the base corruption of my blood,
8And like a tetter grow’st unto my flesh.

Sebastian.

Inflict any punishment upon me. The severity shall not discourage me if it be not shameful, so you'll but put money i' my purse. The want of money makes a free spirit more mad than the possession does an usurer.

D'Amville.

Not a farthing.

Sebastian.

Would you ha' me turn purse-taker? 'Tis the next way to do 't. For want is like the rack; it draws a man to endanger himself to the gallows rather than endure it.

Enter Charlemont; D'amville counterfeits to take him for a ghost.

D'Amville.

16What art thou? Stay! Assist my troubled sense.
17My apprehension will distract me. Stay!

Languebeau Snuffe avoids him fearfully.

Sebastian.

18What art thou? Speak!

Charlemont.

What art thou? Speak! The spirit of Charlemont.

D'Amville.

19O stay. Compose me. I dissolve.

Languebeau.

No, 'tis profane. Spirits are invisible. 'Tis the fiend i' the likeness of Charlemont. I will have no conversation with Sathan.

Exit.

Sebastian.

21The spirit of Charlemont? I'll try that.

Strike[s], and the blow [is] returned.

22‘Fore God, thou sayest true; th' art all spirit.

D'Amville.

23Go call the officers.

Exit.

Charlemont.

24Th’ art a villain and the son of a villain.

Sebastian.

25You lie.

[They] fight. Sebastian is down.

Charlemont.

26Have at thee.

Enter the Ghost of Montferrers.

27Revenge, to thee I'll dedicate this work.

Montferrers.

28Hold, Charlemont!
29Let him revenge my murder, and thy wrongs,
30To whom the justice of revenge belongs.

Exit.

Charlemont.

31You torture me between the passion
32Of my blood and the religion of my soul.

Sebastian rises.

Sebastian.

33A good honest fellow.

Enter D'Amville with Officers.

D'Amville.

34What, wounded? Apprehend him. Sir, is this
35Your salutation for the courtesy
36I did you when we parted last? You ha'
37Forgot I lent you a thousand crowns.
38[To the Officers] First let
39Him answer for this riot. When the law
40Is satisfy’d for that, an an action for
41His debt shall clap him up again.
42[To Charlemont] I took
43You for a spirit, and I'll conjure you
44Before I ha' done.

Charlemont.

Before I ha' done. No. I'll turn conjurer. Devil!
45Within this circle, in the midst of all
46Thy force and malice, I conjure thee do
47Thy worst.

D'Amville.

Thy worst. Away with him!

Exeunt Officers with Charlemont.

Sebastian.

Thy worst. Away with him! Sir, I have got
48A scratch or two here for your sake. I hope
49You'll give me money to pay the surgeon.

D'Amville.

50Borachio, fetch me a thousand crowns. I am
51Content to countenance the freedom of
52Your spirit when 'tis worthily employed.
53A’ God's name, give behaviour the full scope
54Of gen’rous liberty, but let it not
55Disperse and spend itself in courses of
56Unbounded licence. Here, pay for your hurts.

Exit.

Sebastian.

I thank you, sir. Gen’rous liberty —that is to say, freely to bestow my abilities to honest purposes. Methinks I should not follow that instruction now, if having the means to do an honest office for an honest fellow, I should neglect it. Charlemont lies in prison for a thousand crowns, and here I have a thousand crowns. Honesty tells me 'twere well done to release Charlemont. But discretion says I had much ado to come by this, and when this shall be gone I know not where to finger any more, especially if I employ it to this use, which is like to endanger me into my father's perpetual displeasure. And then I may go hang myself, or be forced to do that will make another save me the labour. No matter. Charlemont, thou gav'st me my life, and that's somewhat of a purer earth than gold, as fine as it is. ’Tis no courtesy I do thee, but thank- fulness. I owe thee it and I'll pay it. He fought bravely, but the officers dragged him villanously. Arrant knaves, for using him so discourteously, may the sins o' the poor people be so few that you sha' not be able to spare so much out o' your gettings as will pay for the hire of a lame starved hackney to ride to an execution, but go a-foot to the gallows and be hanged. May elder brothers turn good husbands and younger brothers get good wives, that there be no need of debt-books nor use of sergeants. May there be all peace but i' the war and all charity but i' the devil, so that prisons may be turned to hospitals, though the officers live o’ the benevolence. If this curse might come to pass, the world would say, Blessed be he that curseth.

Exit.

[III.iii]

Enter Charlemont in prison.

Charlemont.

1I grant thee, Heaven, thy goodness doth command
2Our punishments, but yet no further than
3The measure of our sins. How should they else
4Be just? Or how should that good purpose of
5Thy justice take effect, by bounding men
6Within the confines of humanity,
7When our afflictions do exceed our crimes?
8Then they do rather teach the barb’rous world
9Examples that extend her cruelties
10Beyond their own dimensions, and instruct
11Our actions to be more, more barbarous.
12O my afflicted soul, how torment swells
13Thy apprehension with profane conceit,
14Against the sacred justice of my God!
15Our own constructions are the authors of
16Our misery. We never measure our
17Conditions but with men above us in
18Estate, so while our spirits labour to
19Be higher than our fortunes, th' are more base.
20Since all those attributes which make men seem
21Superior to us are man's subjects and
22Were made to serve him, the repining man
23Is of a servile spirit to deject
24The value of himself below their estimation.

Enter Sebastian with the Keeper.

Sebastian.

Here. Take my sword. —How now, my wild swagg’rer, y' are tame enough now, are you not? The penury of a prison is like a soft consumption. 'Twill humble the pride o’ your mortality, and arm your soul in complete patience to endure the weight of affliction without feeling it. What, hast no music in thee? Th' hast trebles and bases enough, treble injury and base usage. But trebles and bases make poor music without means. Thou want'st means, dost? What, dost droop? Art dejected?

Charlemont.

34No, sir. I have a heart above the reach
35Of thy most violent maliciousness.
36A fortitude in scorn of thy contempt—
37Since Fate is pleas’d to have me suffer it—
38That can bear more than thou hast power t' inflict.
39I was a baron; that thy father has
40Depriv’d me off. Instead of that I am
41Created king. I've lost a signiory
42That was confin’d within a piece of earth,
43A wart upon the body of the world.
44But now I am an emp’ror of a world,
45This little world of man. My passions are
46My subjects, and I can command them laugh,
47Whilst thou dost tickle 'em to death with misery.

Sebastian.

'Tis bravely spoken, and I love thee for 't. Thou liest here for a thousand crowns. Here are a thousand to re- deem thee —not for the ransom o' my life thou gav'st me; that I value not at one crown. 'Tis none o' my deed; thank my father for 't. ’Tis his goodness. Yet he looks not for thanks, for he does it underhand, out of a reserved dispo- sition to do thee good without ostentation. — Out o’ great heart you'll refuse 't now, will you?

Charlemont.

56No. Since I must submit myself to Fate,
57I never will neglect the offer of
58One benefit, but entertain them as
59Her favours and th' inductions to some end
60Of better fortune, as whose instrument
61I thank thy courtesy.

Sebastian.

I thank thy courtesy. Well, come along.

Exeunt.

[III.iv]

Enter D'Amville and Castabella.

D'Amville.

1Daughter, you do not well to urge me. I
2Ha' done no more than justice. Charlemont
3Shall die and rot in prison, and 'Tis just.

Castabella.

4O father, mercy is an attribute
5As high as justice, an essential part
6Of his unbounded goodness, whose divine
7Impression, form, and image man should bear.
8And methinks man should love to imitate
9His mercy, since the only countenance
10Of justice were destruction, if the sweet
11And loving favour of his mercy did
12Not mediate between it and our weakness.

D'Amville.

13Forbear. You will displease me. He shall rot.

Castabella.

14Dear sir, since by your greatness you
15Are nearer heav’n in place, be nearer it
16In goodness. Rich men should transcend the poor,
17As clouds the earth, rais’d by the comfort of
18The sun to water dry and barren grounds.
19If neither the impression in your soul
20Of goodness, nor the duty of your place
21As goodness’ substitute can move you, then
22Let Nature, which in savages, in beasts,
23Can stir to pity, tell you that he is
24Your kinsman.

D'Amville.

Your kinsman. You expose your honesty
25To strange construction. Why should you so urge
26Release for Charlemont? Come you profess
27More nearness to him than your modesty
28Can answer. You have tempted my suspicion.
29I tell thee he shall starve, and die, and rot.

Enter Charlemont and Sebastian.

Charlemont.

30Uncle, I thank you.

D'Amville.

Uncle, I thank you. Much good do it you.
31Who did release him?

Sebastian.

Who did release him? I.

Exit Castabella.

D'Amville.

Who did release him? I. You are a villain.

Sebastian.

32Y' are my father.

Exit.

D'Amville.

Y' are my father. I must temporize.aparte
33[To Charlemont] Nephew, had not his open freedom made
34My disposition known, I would ha' borne
35The course and inclination of my love
36According to the motion of the sun,
37Invisibly enjoy’d and understood.

Charlemont.

38That shows your good works are directed to
39No other end then goodness. I was rash,
40I must confess, but —

D'Amville.

I must confess, but — I will excuse you.
41To lose a father and, as you may think,
42Be disinherited, it must be granted,
43Are motives to impatience. But for death,
44Who can avoid it? And for his estate,
45In the uncertainty of both your lives
46‘Twas done discreetly to confer't upon
47A known successor, being the next in blood,
48And one, dear nephew, whom in time to come
49You shall have cause to thank. I will not be
50Your dispossessor, but your guardian.
51I will supply your father's vacant place,
52To guide your green improvidence of youth
53And make you ripe for your inheritance.

Charlemont.

54Sir, I embrace your gen’rous promises.

[They embrace.]

Enter Rousard sick, and Castabella.

Rousard.

55Embracing? I behold the object that
56Mine eye affects. Dear cousin Charlemont!

D'Amville.

57My elder son. He meets you happily,
58For with the hand of our whole family
59We interchange th' indenture of ourLoves.

Charlemont.

60And I accept it, yet not joyfully
61Because y' are sick.

D'Amville.

Because y' are sick. Sir, his affection's sound
62Though he be sick in body.

Rousard.

Though he be sick in body. Sick indeed.
63A gen’ral weakness did surprise my health
64The very day I marry’d Castabella,
65As if my sickness were a punishment
66That did arrest me for some injury
67I then committed.
68[To Castabella] Credit me, my love,
69I pity thy ill fortune to be match’d
70With such a weak unpleasing bedfellow.

Castabella.

71Believe me, sir, it never troubles me.
72I am as much respectless to enjoy
73Such pleasure as ignorant what it is.

Charlemont.

74Thy sex's wonder. Unhappy Charlemont.

D'Amville.

75Come, let's to supper. There we will confirm
76The eternal bond of our concluded love.

Exeunt.
