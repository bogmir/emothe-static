Cyril Tourneur

The Tragedy of the Atheist

Act V

[V.i]

Music. A closet discovered. A Servant sleeping with lights and money before him. Enter D’Amville.

D'Amville.

1What, sleep'st thou?

Servant.

What, sleep'st thou? No, my lord, nor sleep nor wake,
2But in a slumber troublesome to both.

D'Amville.

3Whence comes this gold?

Servant.

Whence comes this gold? 'Tis part of the revenue
4Due to your lordship since your brother's death.

D'Amville.

5To bed. Leave me my gold.

Servant.

To bed. Leave me my gold. And me my rest.
6Two things wherewith one man is seldom blest.

Exit.

D'Amville.

7Cease that harsh music. W’ are not pleas’d with it.

He handles the gold.

8Here sounds a music whose melodious touch
9Like angels’ voices ravishes the sense.
10Behold, thou ignorant astronomer,
11Whose wand'ring speculation seeks among
12The planets for men's fortunes! With amazement,
13Behold thine error and be planet-struck.
14These are the stars whose operations make
15The fortunes and the destinies of men.
16Yond’ lesser eyes of Heav’n, like subjects reis’d
17Into their lofty houses when their prince
18Rides underneath th' ambition of their loves,
19Are mounted only to behold the face,
20Of your more rich imperious eminence,
21With unprevented sight. Unmask, fair queen;

Unpurses the gold.

22Vouchsafe their expectations may enjoy
23The gracious favour they admire to see.
24These are the stars, the ministers of fate,
25And man's high wisdom the superior power
26To which their forces are subordinate.

Sleeps.

Enter the Ghost of Montferrers.

Montferrers.

27D’Amville, with all thy wisdom th’ art a fool,
28Not like those fools that we term innocents,
29But a most wretched miserable fool,
30Which instantly, to the confusion of
31Thy projects, with despair thou shalt behold.

Exit.

D’Amville starts up.

D'Amville.

32What foolish dream dares interrupt my rest
33To my confusion? How can that be, since
34My purposes have hitherto been borne
35With prosp’rous judgement to secure success—
36Which nothing lives to dispossess me of
37But apprehended Charlemont, and him,
38This brain has made the happy instrument
39To free suspicion, to annihilate
40All interest and title of his own,
41To seal up my assurance and confirm
42My absolute possession by the law.
43Thus while the simple, honest worshipper
44Of a fantastic providence groans under
45The burden of neglected misery,
46My real wisdom has rais’d up a state,
47That shall eternize my posterity.

Enter Servants with the body of Sebastian.

48What's that?

Servant.

What's that? The body of your younger son,
49Slain by the Lord Belforest.

D'Amville.

Slain by the Lord Belforest. Slain? You lie.
50Sebastian! Speak, Sebastian! H’ has lost
51His hearing. A physician presently!
52Go, call a surgeon.

Rousard.

Go, call a surgeon. O.

Within.

D'Amville.

Go, call a surgeon. O. What groan was that?
53How does my elder son? The sound came from
54His chamber.

Servant.

His chamber. He went sick to bed, my lord.

Rousard.

55(Within.) O.

D'Amville.

56The cries of mandrakes never touch’d the ear
57With more sad horror then that voice does mine.

Enter a Servant running.

Servant.

58If ever you will see your son alive—

D'Amville.

59Nature forbid I e'er should see him dead.

A Bed drawn forth with Rousard.

60Withdraw the curtains. O how does my son?

Servant.

61Methinks he's ready to give up the ghost.

D'Amville.

62Destruction take thee and thy fatal tongue.
63Death! Where's the Doctor? Art not thou the face
64Of that prodigious apparition star’d upon
65Me in my dream?

Servant.

Me in my dream? The doctor's come, my lord.

Enter Doctor.

D'Amville.

66Doctor, behold two patients in whose cure
67Thy skill may purchase an eternal fame.
68If thou hast any reading in Hippocrates,
69Galen or Avice, if herbs or drugs
70Or minerals have any power to save,
71Now let thy practice and their sovereign use
72Raise thee to wealth and honour.

Doctor.

Raise thee to wealth and honour. If any root
73Of life remains within 'em capable
74Of physic, fear 'em not, my lord.

Rousard.

Of physic, fear 'em not, my lord. O.

D'Amville.

75His gasping sighs are like the falling noise
76Of some great building when the groundwork breaks.
77On these two pillars stood the stately frame
78And architecture of my lofty house.
79An earthquake shakes 'em; the foundation shrinks.
80Dear Nature, in whose honour I have rais’d
81A work of glory to posterity,
82O bury not the pride of that great action
83Under the fall and ruin of itself.

Doctor.

84My lord, these bodies are depriv’d of all
85The radical ability of nature.
86The heat of life is utterly extinguish’d.
87Nothing remains within the power of man
88That can restore them.

D'Amville.

That can restore them. Take this gold; extract
89The spirit of it, and inspire new life
90Into their bodies.

Doctor.

Into their bodies. Nothing can, my lord.

D'Amville.

91You ha' not yet examin’d the true state
92And constitution of their bodies. Sure,
93You ha' not. I'll reserve their waters till
94The morning. Questionless, their urines
95Will inform you better.

Doctor.

Will inform you better. Ha, ha, ha.

D'Amville.

Will inform you better. Ha, ha, ha. Dost laugh,
96Thou villain? Must my wisdom that has been
97The object of men's admiration now
98Become the subject of thy laughter?

Rousard.

Become the subject of thy laughter? O.

Dies.

All.

99He's dead.

D'Amville.

He's dead. O there expires the date
100Of my posterity. Can Nature be
101so simple or malicious to destroy
102The reputation of her proper memory?
103She cannot. Sure there is some power above
104Her that controls her force.

Doctor.

Her that controls her force. A power above Nature?
105Doubt you that, my lord? Consider but
106Whence man receives his body and his form:
107Not from corruption like some worms and flies,
108But only from the generation of
109A man, for Nature never did bring forth
110A man without a man; nor could the first
111Man, being but the passive subject, not
112The active mover, be the maker of
113Himself; so of necessity there must
114Be a superior power to Nature.

D'Amville.

115Now to myself I am ridiculous.
116Nature, thou art a traitor to my soul.
117Thou hast abus’d my trust. I will complain
118To a superior court to right my wrong.
119I'll prove thee a forger of false assurances.
120In yond’ Star chamber thou shalt answer it.
121Withdraw the bodies. O the sense of death
122Begins to trouble my distracted soul.

Exeunt.

[V.ii]

Enter Judges and Officers.

1 Judge.

1Bring forth the malefactors to the bar.

Enter Cataplasma, Soquette and Fresco.

2Are you the gentlewoman in whose house
3The murders were committed?

Cataplasma.

The murders were committed? Yes, my lord.

1 Judge.

4That worthy attribute of gentry which
5Your habit draws from ignorant respect
6Your name deserves not, nor yourself the name
7Of woman, since you are the poison that
8Infects the honour of all womanhood.

Cataplasma.

9My lord, I am a gentlewoman, yet
10I must confess my poverty compels
11My life to a condition lower than
12My birth or breeding.

2 Judge.

My birth or breeding. Tush, we know your birth.

1 Judge.

13But under colour to profess the sale
14Of tires and toys for gentlewomen's pride,
15You draw a frequentation of men's wives
16To your licentious house, and there abuse
17Their Husbands.

Fresco.

Their Husbands. Good my lord, her rent is great.
18The good gentlewoman has no other thing
19To live by but her lodgings; so she's forc’d
20To let her fore-rooms out to others, and
21Herself contented to lie backwards.

2 Judge.

Herself contented to lie backwards. So.

1 Judge.

22Here is no evidence accuses you
23For accessaries to the murder; yet
24Since from the spring of lust which you preserv’d
25And nourish’d ran th' effusion of that blood,
26Your punishment shall come as near to death
27As life can bear it. Law cannot inflict
28Too much severity upon the cause
29Of such abhorr’d effects.

2 Judge.

Of such abhorr’d effects. Receive your sentence.
30Your goods, since they were gotten by that means,
31Which brings diseases, shall be turn’d to th' use
32Of hospitals; you carted through the streets
33According to the common shame of strumpets,
34Your bodies whipp’d till with the loss of blood
35You faint under the hand of punishment.
36Then, that the necessary force of want
37May not provoke you to your former life,
38You shall be set to painful labour, whose
39Penurious gains shall only give you food
40To hold up nature, mortify your flesh,
41And make you fit for a repentant end.

All.

42O good my lord!

1 Judge.

O good my lord! No more; away with 'em.

Exeunt [Cataplasma, Soquette, and Fresco]

Enter Languebeau Snuffe

2 Judge.

43Now, Monsieur Snuffe, a man of your profession
44Found in a place of such impiety!

Languebeau.

I grant you the place is full of impurity. So much the more need of instruction and reformation. The purpose that carried me thither was with the spirit of conversion to purify their uncleanness, and I hope your lordship will say, the law cannot take hold o’ me for that.

1 Judge.

50No, Sir, it cannot; but yet give me leave
51To tell you that I hold your wary answer
52Rather premeditated for excuse
53Than spoken out of a religious purpose.
54Where took you your degrees of scholarship?

Languebeau.

I am no scholar, my lord. To speak the sincere truth, I am Snuffe the tallow-chandler.

2 Judge

How comes your habit to be alter’d thus?

Languebeau.

My Lord Belforest, taking a delight in the cleanness of my conversation, withdrew me from that unclean life and put me in a garment fit for his society and my present profession.

1 Judge.

62His lordship did but paint a rotten post,
63Or cover foulness fairly. Monsieur Snuffe,
64Back to your candle-making. You may give
65The world more light with that than either with
66Instruction or th' example of your life.

Languebeau.

67Thus the Snuffe is put out.

Exit.

Enter D’Amville distractedly, with the hearses of his two sons borne after him.

D'Amville.

68Judgement, judgement!

2 Judge.

Judgement, judgement! Judgement, my lord, in what?

D'Amville.

69Your judgements must resolve me in a case.
70Bring in the bodies. Nay, I will ha 't tried.
71This is the case, my lord: my providence,
72Ev’n in a moment, by the only hurt
73Of one, or two, or three at most —and those
74Put quickly out o’ pain too, mark me; I
75Had wisely rais’d a competent estate
76To my posterity; and is there not
77More wisdom and more charity in that,
78Than for your lordship, or your father, or
79Your grandsire to prolong the torment and
80The rack of rent from age to age upon
81Your poor penurious tenants, yet perhaps
82Without a penny profit to your heir?
83Is 't not more wise, more charitable? Speak.

1 Judge.

84He is distracted.

D'Amville.

He is distracted. How? distracted? Then
85You ha' no judgement. I can give you sense
86And solid reason for the very least
87Distinguishable syllable I speak.
88Since my thrift was more charitable, more
89Judicious than your grandsire’s, why, I would
90Fain know why your lordship lives to make
91A second generation from your father,
92And the whole fry of my posterity
93Extinguish’d in a moment, not a brat
94Left to succeed me —I would fain know that.

2 Judge.

95Grief for his children's death distempers him.

1 Judge.

96My lord, we will resolve you of your question.
97In the meantime vouchsafe your place with us.

D'Amville.

98I am contented, so you will resolve me.

Ascends.

Enter Charlemont and Castabella.

2 Judge.

99Now, Monsieur Charlemont, you are accus’d
100Of having murder’d one Borachio that
101Was servant to my Lord D’Amville. How can
102You clear yourself? Guilty or not guilty?

Charlemont.

103Guilty of killing him, but not of murder.
104My lords, I have no purpose to desire
105Remission for myself.

D’Amville descends to Charlemont.

D'Amville.

Remission for myself. Uncivil boy,
106Thou want'st humanity to smile at grief.
107Why dost thou cast a cheerful eye upon
108The object of my sorrow, my dead sons?

1 Judge.

109O good my lord, let charity forbear
110To vex the spirit of a dying man.
111A cheerful eye upon the face of death
112Is the true count’nance of a noble mind.
113For honour's sake, my lord, molest it not.

D'Amville.

114Y’ are all uncivil. O, is 't not enough
115That he unjustly hath conspir’d with Fate
116To cut off my posterity, for him
117To be the heir to my possessions, but
118He must pursue me with his presence, and
119In the ostentation of his joy
120Laugh in my face and glory in my grief?

Charlemont.

121D’Amville! To show thee with what light respect
122I value death and thy insulting pride,
123Thus, like a warlike navy on the sea,
124Bound for the conquest of some wealthy land,
125Pass’d through the stormy troubles of this life
126And now arriv’d upon the armed coast,
127In expectation of the victory
128Whose honour lies beyond this exigent,
129Through mortal danger, with an active spirit,
130Thus I aspire to undergo my death.

Leaps up the scaffold. Castabella leaps after him.

Castabella.

131And thus I second thy brave enterprise.
132Be cheerful, Charlemont. Our lives cut off
133In our young prime of years are like green herbs
134Wherewith we strew the hearses of our friends,
135For as their virtue gather’d when th' are green,
136Before they wither or corrupt, is best,
137So we in virtue are the best for death
138While yet we have not liv’d to such an age
139That the increasing canker of our sins
140Hath spread too far upon us.

D'Amville.

Hath spread too far upon us. A boon, my lords;
141I beg a boon.

1 Judge.

I beg a boon. What's that, my lord?

D'Amville.

142His body when 'tis dead
143For an anatomy.

2 Judge.

For an anatomy. For what, my lord?

D'Amville.

144Your understanding still come short o' mine.
145I would find out by his anatomy
146What thing there is in Nature more exact
147Then in the constitution of myself.
148Methinks my parts and my dimensions are
149As many, as large, as well compos’d as his,
150And yet in me the resolution wants
151To die with that assurance as he does.
152The cause of that in his anatomy
153I would find out.

1 Judge.

I would find out. Be patient and you shall.

D'Amville.

154I have bethought me of a better way.
155Nephew, we must confer. Sir, I am grown
156A wond’rous student now o' late. My wit
157Has reach’d beyond the scope of Nature; yet
158For all my learning I am still to seek
159From whence the peace of conscience should proceed.

Charlemont.

160The peace of conscience rises in itself.

D'Amville.

161Whether it be thy art or nature, I
162Admire thee, Charlemont. Why, thou hast taught
163A woman to be valiant. I will beg
164Thy life. My lords, I beg my nephew's life.
165I'll make thee my physician. Thou shalt read
166Philosophy to me. I will find out
167Th' efficient cause of a contented mind.
168But if I cannot profit in 't, then 'tis
169No more, being my physician, but infuse
170A little poison in a potion when
171Thou giv'st me physic, unawares to me.
172So I shall steal into my grave without
173The understanding or the fear of death,
174And that's the end I aim at, for the thought
175Of death is a most fearful torment; is 't not?

2 Judge.

176Your lordship interrupts the course of law.

1 Judge.

177Prepare to die.

Charlemont.

Prepare to die. My resolution's made.
178But ere I die, before this honour’d bench,
179With the free voice of a departing soul,
180I here protest this gentlewoman clear
181Of all offence the law condemns her for.

Castabella.

182I have accus’d myself. The law wants power
183To clear me. My dear Charlemont, with thee
184I will partake of all thy punishments.

Charlemont.

185Uncle, for all the wealthy benefits
186My death advances you, grant me but this:
187Your mediation for the guiltless life
188Of Castabella, whom your conscience knows
189As justly clear as harmless innocence.

D'Amville.

190Freely. My mediation for her life,
191And all my int’rest in the world to boot,
192Let her but in exchange possess me of
193The resolution that she dies withal.
194The price of things is best known in their want.
195Had I her courage, so I value it,
196The Indies should not buy 't out o' my hands.

Charlemont.

197Give me a glass of water.

D'Amville.

Give me a glass of water. Me, of wine.
198This argument of death congeals my blood.
199Cold fear, with apprehension of thy end,
200Hath frozen up the rivers of my veins.

[A Servant gives him] a glass of wine.

201I must drink wine to warm me and dissolve
202The obstruction, or an apoplexy will
203Possess me. Why, thou uncharitable knave,
204Dost bring me blood to drink? The very glass
205Looks pale and trembles at it.

Servant.

Looks pale and trembles at it. 'Tis your hand, my lord.

D'Amville.

206Canst blame me to be fearful, bearing still
207The presence of a murderer about me?

Charlemont.

208Is this water?

Servant.

Water, sir.

[Gives him] a glass of water.

Charlemont.

210Come, thou clear emblem of cool temperance,
211Be thou my witness that I use no art
212To force my courage, nor have need of helps
213To raise my spirits, like those weaker men
214Who mix their blood with wine, and out of that
215Adulterate conjunction do beget
216A bastard valour. Native courage, thanks.
217Thou lead’st me soberly to undertake
218This great hard work of magnanimity.

D'Amville.

219Brave Charlemont, at the reflection of
220Thy courage my cold fearful blood takes fire,
221And I begin to emulate thy death.

[Executioner comes forward]

222Is that thy executioner? My lords,
223You wrong the honour of so high a blood
224To let him suffer by so base a hand.

Judges.

225He suffers by the form of law, my lord.

D'Amville.

226I will reform it. Down, you shag-hair’d cur.
227The instrument that strikes my nephew's blood
228shall be as noble as his blood. I'll be
229Thy executioner myself.

1 Judge.

230Restrain his fury. Good my lord, forbear.

D'Amville.

231I'll butcher out the passage of his soul
232That dares attempt to interrupt the blow.

2 Judge.

233My lord, the office will impress a mark
234Of scandal and dishonour on your name.

Charlemont.

235The office fits him; hinder not his hand,
236But let him crown my resolution with
237An unexampled dignity of death.
238Strike home. Thus I submit me.

[Makes] ready for execution.

Castabella.

Strike home. Thus I submit me. So do I.
239In scorn of death thus hand in hand we die.

D'Amville.

240I ha' the trick on 't, nephew. You shall see
241How eas’ly I can put you out of pain. —O.

As he raises up the axe [he] strikes out his own brains, [and then] staggers off the Scaffold.

Executioner.

242In lifting up the axe, I think h’ has knock’d
243His brains out.

D'Amville.

His brains out. What murderer was he
244That lifted up my hand against my head?

1 Judge.

245None but yourself, my lord.

D'Amville.

None but yourself, my lord. I thought he was
246A murderer that did it.

1 Judge.

A murderer that did it. God forbid.

D'Amville.

247Forbid? You lie, judge; he commanded it
248To tell thee that man's wisdom is a fool.
249I came to thee for judgement, and thou think'st
250Thyself a wise man. I outreach’d thy wit
251And made thy justice murder’s instrument
252In Castabella's death and Charlemont's.
253To crown my murder of Montferrers with
254A safe possession of his wealthy state.

Charlemont.

255I claim the just advantage of his words.

2 Judge.

256Descend the scaffold and attend the rest.

D'Amville.

257There was the strength of natural understanding.
258But Nature is a fool. There is a power
259Above her that hath overthrown the pride
260Of all my projects and posterity,
261For whose surviving blood I had erected
262A proud monument and struck 'em dead
263Before me, for whose deaths I call’d to thee
264For judgement. Thou didst want discretion for
265The sentence, but yond’ power that struck me knew
266The judgement I deserv’d, and gave it. O,
267The lust of death commits a rape upon me,
268As I would ha' done on Castabella.

Dies.

1 Judge.

269Strange is his death and judgement. With the hands
270Of joy and justice I thus set you free.
271The power of that eternal providence
272Which overthrew his projects in their pride
273Hath made your griefs the instruments to raise
274Your blessings to a greater height than ever.

Charlemont.

275Only to Heav’n I attribute the work,
276Whose gracious motives made me still forbear
277To be mine own revenger. Now I see,
278That patience is the honest man's revenge.

1 judge.

279Instead of Charlemont that but e'en now
280Stood ready to be dispossess’d of all,
281I now salute you with more titles, both
282Of wealth and dignity, than you were born to.
283And you, sweet madam, Lady of Belforest
284You have that title by your father's death.

Castabella.

285With all the titles due to me increase
286The wealth and honour of my Charlemont,
287Lord of Montferrers, Lord D’Amville, Belforest,
288And for a close to make up all the rest,

Embrace[s Charlemont]

289The lord of Castabella. Now at last
290Enjoy the full possession of my love,
291As clear and pure as my first chastity.

Charlemont.

292The crown of all my blessings! I will tempt
293My stars no longer, nor protract my time
294Of marriage. When those nuptial rites are done,
295I will perform my kinsmen's funerals.

1 Judge.

296The drums and trumpets interchange the sounds
297Of death and triumph for these honour’d lives
298Succeeding their deserved tragedies.

Charlemont.

299Thus by the work of Heav’n the men that thought
300To follow our dead bodies without tears,
301Are dead themselves, and now we follow theirs.

Exeunt
