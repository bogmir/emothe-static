Cyril Tourneur

The Tragedy of the Atheist

Act I

[I.i]

Enter D’Amville, Borachio, attended.

D'Amville.

1I saw my nephew Charlemont but now
2Part from his father. Tell him I desire
3To speak with him.

Exit servant

To speak with him. Borachio, thou art read
4In Nature and her large philosophy.
5Observ’st thou not the very self same course
6Of revolution both in man and beast?

Borachio.

7The same, for birth, growth, state, decay and death;
8Only a man’s beholding to his Nature
9For th’ better composition o’ the two.

D'Amville.

10But where that favour of his Nature is
11Not full and free, you see a man becomes
12A fool, as little-knowing as a beast.

Borachio.

13Tha shows there’s nothing in a man above
14His Nature; if there were, consid’ring ’tis
15His being’s excellency, ‘twould not yield
16To Nature’s weakness.

D'Amville.

To Nature’s weakness. Then if death casts up
17Our total sum of joy and happiness,
18Let me have all my senses feasted in
19Th’ abundant fulness of delight at once,
20And with a sweet insensible increase
21Of pleasing surfeit melt into my dust.

Borachio.

22That revolution is too short methinks.
23If this life comprehends our happiness,
24How foolish to desire to die so soon!
25And if our time runs home unto the lenght
26Of Nature, how improvident it were
27To spend our substance on a minute’s pleasure,
28And after live an age in misery!

D'Amville.

29So thou conclud’st that pleasure only flows
30Upon the stream of riches.

Borachio.

Upon the stream of riches. Wealth is lord
31Of all felicity.

D'Amville.

Of all felicity. ‘Tis oracle,
32For what’s a man that’s honest without wealth?

Borachio.

33Both miserable and contemptible.

D'Amville.

34He’s worse, Borachio, For it charity
35Be an essential part of honesty
36And should be practis’d first upon ourselves,
37Wich must be granted, then your honest man
38That’s poor is most dishonest, for he is
39Uncharitable to the man whom he
40Should most respect. But what doth this touch me,
41That seem to have enough? Thanks industry,
42‘Tis true. Had not my body spread itself
43Into posterity, perhaps I should
44Desire no more increase of substance than
45Would hold proportion with mine own dimensions.
46Yet even in that sufficiency of state
47A man has reason to provide and add,
48For what is he hath such a present eye
49And so prepar’d a strenght than can foresee
50And fortify his substance and himself
51Against those accidents, the least whereof
52May rob him of an age’s husbandry?
53And for my children, they are as near to me
54As branches to the tree whereon they grow,
55And may as numerously be multiply’d.
56As they increase, so should my providence,
57For from my substance they receive the sap
58Whereby they live and flourish.

Borachio.

Whereby they live and flourish. Sir, enough.
59I undenstand the mark whereat you aim.

Enter Charlemont.

D'Amville.

60Silence. W’ are interrupted. Charlemont!

Charlemont.

61Good morrow, uncle.

D'Amville.

Good morrow, uncle. Noble Charlemont,
62Good morrow. Is not this the honour’d day
63You purpos’d to set forward to the war?

Charlemont.

64My inclination did intend it so.

D'Amville.

65And not your resolution?

Charlemont.

And not your resolution? Yes, my lord,
66Had not my father contradicted it.

D'Amville.

67O noble war, thou first original
68Of all man’s honour! How dejectedly
69The baser spirit of our present time
70Hath cast itself below the ancient worth
71Of our forefathers, from whose noble deeds
72Ignobly we derive our pedigrees.

Charlemont.

73Sir, tax not me for his unwillingness.
74By the command of his authority
75My disposition’s forc’d against itself.

D'Amville.

76Nephew, you are the honour of our blood.
77The troop of gentry whose inferior worth
78Should second your example are become
79Your leaders, and the scorn of their discourse
80Turns smiling back upon your backwardness.

Charlemont.

81You need not urge my spirit by disgrace;
82‘Tis free enough. My father hinders it.
83To curb me, he denies me maintenance
84To put me in the habit of my rank.
85Unbind me from that strong necessity,
86And call me coward if I stay behind.

D'Amville.

87For want of means? Borachio, where’s the gold?
88I’d disinherit my prosperity
89To purchase honour. ’Tis an interest
90I prize above the principal of wealth.
91I’m glad I had th’ occasion to make known
92How readily my substance shall unlock
93Itself to serve you. Here’s a thousand crowns.

Charlemont.

94My worthy uncle, in exchange for this
95I leave my bond. So I am doubly bound,
96By that for the repayment of this gold,
97And by this gold to satisfy your love.

D'Amville.

98Sir, ’tis a witness only of my love,
99And love doth always satisfy itself.
100Now to your father; labour his consent.
101My importunity shall second yours.
102We will obtain it.

Charlemont.

We will obtain it. If entreaty fail,
103The force of reputation shall prevail.

Exit

D'Amville.

104Go call my sons that they may take their leaves
105Of noble Charlemont. Now, my Borachio!

Borachio.

106The substance of our former argument
107Was wealth.

D'Amville.

108The question how to compass it.

Borachio.

109Young Charlemont is going to the war.

D'Amville.

110O, thou begin’s to take me.

Borachio.

O, thou begin’s to take me. Mark me then.
111Methinks the pregnant wit of man might make
112The happy absence of this Charlemont
113A subject for commodious providence.
114He has a wealthy father, ready ev’n
115To drop into his grave, and no man’s power
116When Charlemont is gone can interpose
117‘Twixt you and him.

D'Amville.

‘Twixt you and him. Th’ hast apprehended –both
118My meaning and my love. Now let thy trust
119For undertaking and for secrecy
120Hold measure with thy amplitude of wit,
121And thy reward shall parallel thy worth.

Borachio.

122My resolution has already bound
123Me to your service.

D'Amville.

Me to your service. And my heart to thee.

Enter Rousard and Sebastian.

124Here are my sons...
125There’s my eternity. My life in them
126And their succession shall for ever live,
127And in my reason dwells the providence
128To add to life as much of happiness.
129Let all men lose, so I increase my gain:
130I have no feeling of another’s pain.

Exeunt.

[I.ii]

Enter old Montferrers and Charlemont.

Montferrers.

1I prithee let this current of my tears
2Divert thy inclination from the war,
3For of my children thou art only left
4To promise a succession to my house,
5And all the honour thou canst get by arms
6Will give but vain addition to thy name,
7Since from thy ancestors thou dost derive
8A dignity sufficient, and as great
9As thou hast substance to maintain and bear.
10I prithee stay at home.

Charlemont.

I prithee stay at home. My noble father,
11The weakest sigh you breathe hath power to turn
12My strongest purpose, and your softest tear
13To melt my resolution to as soft
14Obedience. But my affection to the war
15Is as hereditary as my blood
16To ev’ry life of all my ancestry.
17Your predecessors were your precedents,
18And you are my example. Shall I serve
19For nothing but a vain parenthesis
20I’ th’ honour’d story of your family,
21Or hang but like an empty scutcheon
22Between the trophies of my predecessors
23And the rich arms of my posterity?
24There’s not a Frenchman of good blood and youth,
25But either out of spirit or example
26Is turn’d a soldier. Only Charlemont
27Must be reputed that same heartless thing
28That cowards will be bold to play upon.

Enter D’Amville, Rousard and Sebastian.

D'Amville.

29Good morrow, my lord.

Montferrers.

30Morrow, good brother.

Charlemont.

31Good morrow, uncle.

D'Amville.

32Morrow, kind nephew.
33What, ha’ you wash’d your eyes wi’ tears this morning?
34[To Montferrers] Come, by my soul, his purpose does deserve
35Your free consent. Your tenderness dissuades him.
36What to the father of a gentleman
37Should be more tender than the maintenance
38And the increase of honour to his house?
39My lord, here are my boys. I should be proud
40That either this were able or that inclin’d
41To be my nephew’s brave competitor.

Montferrers.

42Your importunities have overcome.
43Pray God my forc’d grant prove not ominous.

D'Amville.

44We have obtain’d it.aparte
We have obtain’d it. [To Montferrers] Ominous? in what?
45It cannot be in anything but death,
46And I am of a confident belief
47That ev’n the time, place, manner of our deaths
48Do follow fate with that necessity
49That makes us sure to die. And in a thing
50Ordain’d so certainly unalterable,
51What can the use of providence prevail?

[Enter] Belforest, Levidulcia, [and] Castabella, attended.

Belforest.

52Morrow, my Lord Montferrers, Lord D’Amville.
53Good morrow, gentlemen. Cousin Charlemont,
54Kindly good morrow. Troth, I was afear’d
55I should ha’ come too late to tell you that
56I wish your undertakings a success
57That may deserve the measure of their worth.

Charlemont.

58My lord, my duty would not let me go
59Without receiving your commandments.

Belforest.

60Accompliments are more for ornament
61Than use. We should employ no time in them
62But what our serious business will admit.

Montferrers.

63Your favour had by his duty been prevented
64If we had not withheld him in the way.

D'Amville.

65He was a-coming to present his service.
66But now no more. The cook invites to breakfast.
67Will’t please your lordship enter? Noble lady!

[Exeunt all except] Charlemont and Castabella.

Charlemont.

68My noble mistress, this accompliment
69Is like an elegant and moving speech
70Compos’d of many sweet persuasive points
71Which second one another with a fluent
72Increase and confirmation of their force,
73Reserving still the best until the last,
74To crown the strong impulsion of the rest
75With a full conquest of the hearer’s sense-
76Because th’ impression of the last we speak
77Doth always longest and most constantly
78Possess the entertainment of remembrance.
79So all that now salute my taking leave
80Have added numeously to the love
81Wherewith I did receive their courtesy.
82But you, deas mistress, being the last and best
83That speaks my farewell, like th’ imperious close
84Of a most sweet oration, wholly have
85Possess’d my liking and shall ever live
86Within the soul of my true memory.
87So, mistress, with this kiss I take my leave.

Castabella.

88My worthy servant, you mistake th’ intent
89Of kissing. ’Twas not meant to separate
90A pair of lovers, but to be the seal
91Of love, importing by the joining of
92Our mutual and incorporated breaths
93That we should breathe but one contracted life.
94Or stay at home, or let me go with you.

Charlemont.

95My Castabella! For myself to stay
96Or you to go would either tax my youth
97With a dishonourable weakness or
98Your loving purpose with immodesty.

Enter Languebeau Snuffe.

99And for the satisfaction of your love,
100Here comes a man whose knowledge I have made
101A witness to the contract of our vows,
102Wich my return, by marriage, shall confirm.

Languebeau.

I salute you both with the spirit of copulation. I am already informed of your matrimonial purposes and will be a testimony to the integrity of your promises.

Castabella.

106O the sad trouble of my fearful soul!
107My faithful servant! Did you never hear
108That when a certain great man went to th’ war
109The lovely face of Heav’n was mask’d with sorrow,
110The sighing winds did move the breast of earth,
111The heavy clouds hung down their mourning heads
112And wept sad showers the day that he went hence,
113As if that day presag’d some ill success
114That fatally should kill his happiness,
115And so it came to pass. Methinks my eyes,
116Sweet Heav’n forbid, are like those weeping clouds,
117And as their showers presag’d, so do my tears,
118Some sad event will follow my sad fears.

Charlemont.

119Fie, superstitious! Is it bad to kiss?

Castabella.

120May all my fears hurt me no more than this.

[They kiss.]

Languebeau.

Fie, fie, these carnal kisses do stir up the concu- piscences of the flesh.

Enter Belforest and Levidulcia.

Levidulcia.

123O, here’s your daughter under her servant’s lips.

Charlemont.

124Madam, there is no cause you should mistrust
125The kiss I gave; ’twas but a parting one.

Levidulcia.

126A lustry blood! Now, by the lip of Love,
127Were I to choose, your joining one for me.

Belforest.

128Your father stays to bring you on the way.
129Farewell. The Great Commander of the war
130Prosper the course you undertake. Farewell.

Charlemont.

131My lord, I humbly take my leave.
132[To Levidulcia] Madam,
133I kiss your hand.
134[To Castabella] And your sweet lip. Farewell.

[Exeunt all except] Charlemont and Languebeau [Snuffe]

135Her power to speak is perish’d in her tears.
136Something within me would persuade my stay,
137But reputation will not yield unto’t.
138Dear sir, you are the man whose honest trust
139My confidence hath chosen for my friend.
140I fear my absence will discomfort her.
141You have the power and opportunity
142To moderate her passion. Let her grief
143Receive that friendship from you, and your love
144Shall not repent itself of courtesy.

Languebeau.

Sir, I want wordds and protestation to insinuate into your credit, but in plainness and truth, I will qualify her grief with the spirit of consolation.

Charlemont.

148Sir, I will take your friendship up at use.
149And fear not that your profit shall be small;
150Your interest shall exceed your principal.

Exit.

Enter D’Amville and Borachio.

D'Amville.

Monsieur Languebeau, happily encountered. The honesty of your conversation makes me request more int’rest in your familiarity.

Languebeau.

If your lordship will be pleased to salute me without ceremony, I shall be willing to exchange my service for your favour, but this worshipping kind of entertainment is a superstitious vanity; in plainness and truth I love it not.

D'Amville.

I embrace your disposition and desire to give you as liberal assurance of my love as my Lord Belforest, your deserved favourer.

Languebeau.

His lordship is pleased with my plainness and truth of conversation.

D'Amville.

It cannot displease him. In the behaviour of his noble daughter Castabella a man may read her worth and your instruction.

Languebeau.

That gentlewoman is most sweetly modest, fair, honest, handsome, wise, well-born, and rich.

D'Amville.

You have given me her picture in small.

Languebeau.

She’s like your diamond, a temptation in every man’s eye, yet not yielding to any light impression herself.

D'Amville.

The praise is hers, but the comparison your own.

Gives him the ring.

Languebeau.

You shall forgive me that, sir.

D'Amville.

I will not do so much at your request as forgive you it. I will only give you it, sir. By – you will make me swear.

Languebeau.

O, by no means. Profane not your lips with the foulness of that sin. I will rather take it. To save your oath, you shall lose your ring –Verily, my lord, my praise came short of her worth. She exceeds a jewel. This is but only for ornament, she both for ornament and use.

D'Amville.

Yet unprofitably kept without use. She deserves a worthy husband, sir. I have often wished a match be- tween my elder son and her. The marriage would join the houses of Belforest and D’Amville into a noble alliance.

Languebeau.

And the unity of families is a work of love and charity.

D'Amville.

And that work an employment well becoming the goodness of your disposition.

Languebeau.

If your lordship please to impose it upon me, I will carry it without any second end, the surest way to satisfy your wish.

D'Amville.

Most joyfully accepted. –Rousard! Here are letters to my Lord Belforest touching my desire to that purpose. Enter Rousard, sickly. Rousard, I send you a suitor to Castabella. To this gentleman’s discretion I commit the managing of your suit. His good success shall be most thankful to your trust. Follow his instructions; he will be your leader.

Languebeau.

In plainness and truth.

Rousard.

My leader? Does your lordship think me too weak to give the onset myself?

Languebeau.

I will only assist your proceedings.

Rousard.

To say true, so I think you had need, for a sick man can hardly get a woman’s good will without help.

Languebeau.

Charlemont, thy gratuity and my promises were both but words, and both like words shall vanish into air.

204For thy poor empty hand I must be mute;
205This gives me feeling of a better suit.

Exeunt Languebeau [Snuffe] and Rousard.

D'Amville.

206Borachio, didst precisely note this man?

Borachio.

207His own profession would report him pure.

D'Amville.

208And seems to know if any benefit
209Arises of religion after death;
210Yet but compare ’s profession with his life;
211They so directly contradict themselves
212As if the end of his instructions were
213But to divert the world from sin that he
214More easily might engross it to himself.
215By that I am confirm’d an atheist.
216Well, Charlemont is gone, and here thou see’st
217His absence the foundation of my plot.

Borachio.

218He is the man whom Castabella loves.

D'Amville.

219That was the reason I propounded him
220Employment fix’d upon a foreign place,
221To draw his inclination out o’ th’ way.

Borachio.

222’T has left the passage of our practice free.

D'Amville.

223This Castabella is a wealthy heir,
224And by her marriage with my elder son
225My house is honour’d and my state increas’d.
226This work alone deserves my industry,
227But if it prosper thou shalt see my brain
228Make this but an induction to a point
229So full of profitable policy
230That it would make the soul of honesty
231Ambitious to turn villain.

Borachio.

Ambitious to turn villain. I bespeak
232Employment in ’t. I’ll be an instrument
233To grace performance with dexterity.

D'Amville.

234Thou shalt. No man shall rob thee of the honour.
235Go presently and buy a crimson scarf
236Like Charlemont’s. Prepare thee a disguise
237I’ th’ habit of a soldier, hurt and lame,
238And then be ready at the weeding feast,
239Where thou shalt have employment in a work
240Will please thy disposition.

Borachio.

Will please thy disposition. As I vow’d,
241Your instrument shall make your project proud.

D'Amville.

242This marriage will bring wealth. If that succeed,
243I will increase it though my brother bleed.

Exeunt.

[I.iii]

Enter Castabella, avoiding the importunity of Rousard.

Castabella.

Nay, good sir; in troth if you knew how little it pleases me, you would forbear it.

Rousard.

I will not leave thee till th’ hast entertained me for thy servant.

Castabella.

My servant? You are sick you say. You would tax me of indiscretion to entertain one that is not able to do me service.

Rousard.

The service of a gentlewoman consists most in chamber work, and sick men are fittest for the chamber. I prithee give me a favour.

Castabella.

Methinks you have a very sweet favour of your own.

Rousard.

I lack but your black eye.

Castabella.

If you go to buffets among the boys, they'll give you one.

Rousard.

Nay, if you grow bitter, I'll dispraise your black eye. The gray-eyed morning makes the fairest day.

Castabella.

Now that you dissemble not, I could be willing to give you a favour. What favour would you have?

Rousard.

Any toy, any light thing.

Castabella.

Fie! Will you be so uncivil to ask a light thing at a gentle-woman's hand?

Rousard.

Wilt give me a bracelet o' thy hair then?

Castabella.

Do you want hair, sir?

Rousard.

No, faith, I'll want no hair so long as I can have it for money.

Castabella.

What would you do with my hair then?

Rousard.

Wear it for thy sake, sweetheart.

Castabella.

Do you think I love to have my hair worn off?

Rousard.

Come, you are so witty now and so sensible.

Kisses her.

Castabella.

Tush, I would I wanted one o' my senses now.

Rousard.

Bitter again! What's that? Smelling?

Castabella.

No, no, no. Why now y' are satisfied, I hope. I have given you a favour.

Rousard.

What favour? A kiss? I prithee give me another.

Castabella.

Show me that I gave you then.

Rousard.

How should I show it?

Castabella.

You are unworthy of a favour if you will not bestow the keeping of it one minute.

Rousard.

Well, in plain terms, dost love me? That's the purpose of my coming.

Castabella.

Love you? Yes, very well.

Rousard.

Give me thy hand upon 't.

Castabella.

Nay, you mistake me. If I love you very well, I must not love you now, for now you are not very well; y' are sick.

Rousard.

This equivocation is for the jest now.

Castabella.

I speak't as 'tis now in fashion, in earnest. But I shall not be in quiet for you, I perceive, till I have given you a favour. Do you love me?

Rousard.

With all my heart.

Castabella.

Then with all my heart I'll give you a jewel to hang in your ear. Hark ye — I can never love you.

Exit.

Rousard.

Call you this a jewel to hang in mine ear? 'Tis no light favour, for I'll be sworn it comes somewhat heavily to me. Well, I will not leave her for all this. Methinks it animates a man to stand to 't when a woman desires to be rid of him at the first sight.

Exit.

Enter Belforest and Languebeau Snuffe.

Belforest.

1I entertain the offer of this match
2With purpose to confirm it presently.
3I have already mov’d it to my daughter.
4Her soft excuses savour’d at the first,
5Methought, but of a modest innocence
6Of blood, whose unmov’d stream was never drawn
7Into the current of affection. But when I
8Reply’d with more familiar arguments,
9Thinking to make her apprehension bold,
10Her modest blush fell to a pale dislike,
11And she refus’d it with such confidence
12As if she had been prompted by a love
13Inclining firmly to some other man,
14And in that obstinacy she remains.

Languebeau.

Verily, that disobedience doth not become a child. It proceedeth from an unsanctified liberty. You will be accessory to your own dishonour if you suffer it.

Belforest.

17Your honest wisdom has advis’d me well.
18Once more I'll move her by persuasive means.
19If she resist, all mildness set apart,
20I will make use of my authority.

Languebeau.

And instantly, lest fearing your constraint her contrary affection teach her some device that may prevent you.

Belforest.

22To cut off ev’ry opportunity
23Procrastination may assist her with,
24This instant night she shall be married.

Languebeau.

25Best.

Castabella.

26Please it your lordship, my mother attends

29Time cuts off circumstance; I must be brief.
30To your integrity did Charlemont
31Commit the contract of his love and mine,
32Which now so strong a hand seeks to divide

Languebeau.

Castabella.

39Good sir, I know your heart cannot profane
40The holiness you make profession of
41With such a vicious purpose as to break
42The vow your own consent did help to make.

Languebeau.

47Sweet fellowship, and with a purpose so
48Contracted to that absence that you see
49He purchases your separation with

53Love still desires the presence of his love.
54Verily, he is not of the Family of Love.

Castabella.

55O do not wrong him. 'Tis a generous mind
56That led his disposition to the war,
57For gentle love and noble courage are
58So near ally’d that one begets another,
59Or love is sister, and courage is the brother.
60Could I affect him better than before,
61His soldier's heart would make me love him more.

Languebeau.

62But Castabella—

Enter Levidulcia.

Levidulcia.

63Tush, you mistake the way into a woman;
64The passage lies not through her reason but her blood.

65Nay, stay! How wouldst thou call the child
66That being rais’d with cost and tenderness
67To full ability of body and means
68Denies relief unto the parents who
69Bestow’d that bringing up?

Castabella.

Bestow’d that bringing up? Unnatural.

Levidulcia.

70Then Castabella is unnatural.
71Nature, the loving mother of us all,
72Brought forth a woman for her own relief;
73By generation to revive her age.
74Which, now thou hast hability and means
75Presented, most unkindly dost deny.

Castabella.

76Believe me, mother. I do love a man.

Levidulcia.

77Prefer’st th' affection of an absent love,
78Before the sweet possession of a man,
79The barren mind before the fruitful body,
80Where our creation has no reference
81To man but in his body, being made
82Only for generation which, unless
83Our children can be gotten by conceit,
84Must from the body come. If reason were
85Our counsellor, we would neglect the work
86Of generation for the prodigal
87Expense it draws us to of that which is
88The wealth of life. Wise Nature, therefore, hath
89Reserv’d for an inducement to our sense
90Our greatest pleasure in that greatest work,
91Which being offer’d thee, thy ignorance
92Refuses for th' imaginary joy
93Of an unsatisfy’d affection to
94An absent man — whose blood once spent i' th' war,
95Then he'll come home sick, lame, and impotent,
96And wed thee to a torment, like the pain
97Of Tantalus, continuing thy desire
98With fruitless presentation of the thing
99It loves, still mov’d and still unsatisfy’d.

Belforest.

100Now, Levidulcia, hast thou yet prepar’d
101My daughter's love to entertain this man,
102Her husband here?

Levidulcia.

Her husband here? I'm but her mother i’law;
103Yet if she were my very flesh and blood,
104I could advise no better for her good.

Rousard.

105Sweet wife! Thy joyful husband thus salutes
106Thy cheek.

149As many, as large, as well compos’d as his,
150And yet in me the resolution wants
151To die with that assurance as he does.
152The cause of that in his anatomy
153I would find out.

1 Judge.

I would find out. Be patient and you shall.

D'Amville.

154I have bethought me of a better way.
155Nephew, we must confer. Sir, I am grown
156A wond’rous student now o' late. My wit
157Has reach’d beyond the scope of Nature; yet
158For all my learning I am still to seek
159From whence the peace of conscience should proceed.

Charlemont.

160The peace of conscience rises in itself.

D'Amville.

161Whether it be thy art or nature, I
162Admire thee, Charlemont. Why, thou hast taught
163A woman to be valiant. I will beg
164Thy life. My lords, I beg my nephew's life.
165I'll make thee my physician. Thou shalt read
166Philosophy to me. I will find out
167Th' efficient cause of a contented mind.
168But if I cannot profit in 't, then 'tis
169No more, being my physician, but infuse
170A little poison in a potion when
171Thou giv'st me physic, unawares to me.
172So I shall steal into my grave without
173The understanding or the fear of death,
174And that's the end I aim at, for the thought
175Of death is a most fearful torment; is 't not?

2 Judge.

176Your lordship interrupts the course of law.

1 Judge.

177Prepare to die.

Charlemont.

Prepare to die. My resolution's made.
178But ere I die, before this honour’d bench,
179With the free voice of a departing soul,
180I here protest this gentlewoman clear
181Of all offence the law condemns her for.

Castabella.

182I have accus’d myself. The law wants power
183To clear me. My dear Charlemont, with thee
184I will partake of all thy punishments.

Charlemont.

185Uncle, for all the wealthy benefits
186My death advances you, grant me but this:
187Your mediation for the guiltless life
188Of Castabella, whom your conscience knows
189As justly clear as harmless innocence.

D'Amville.

190Freely. My mediation for her life,
191And all my int’rest in the world to boot,
192Let her but in exchange possess me of
193The resolution that she dies withal.
194The price of things is best known in their want.
195Had I her courage, so I value it,
196The Indies should not buy 't out o' my hands.

Charlemont.

197Give me a glass of water.

D'Amville.

Give me a glass of water. Me, of wine.
198This argument of death congeals my blood.
199Cold fear, with apprehension of thy end,
200Hath frozen up the rivers of my veins.

[A Servant gives him] a glass of wine.

201I must drink wine to warm me and dissolve
202The obstruction, or an apoplexy will
203Possess me. Why, thou uncharitable knave,
204Dost bring me blood to drink? The very glass
205Looks pale and trembles at it.

Servant.

Looks pale and trembles at it. 'Tis your hand, my lord.

D'Amville.

206Canst blame me to be fearful, bearing still
207The presence of a murderer about me?

Charlemont.

208Is this water?

Servant.

Water, sir.

[Gives him] a glass of water.

Charlemont.

210Come, thou clear emblem of cool temperance,
211Be thou my witness that I use no art
212To force my courage, nor have need of helps
213To raise my spirits, like those weaker men
214Who mix their blood with wine, and out of that
215Adulterate conjunction do beget
216A bastard valour. Native courage, thanks.
217Thou lead’st me soberly to undertake
218This great hard work of magnanimity.

D'Amville.

219Brave Charlemont, at the reflection of
220Thy courage my cold fearful blood takes fire,
221And I begin to emulate thy death.

[Executioner comes forward]

222Is that thy executioner? My lords,
223You wrong the honour of so high a blood
224To let him suffer by so base a hand.

Judges.

225He suffers by the form of law, my lord.

D'Amville.

226I will reform it. Down, you shag-hair’d cur.
227The instrument that strikes my nephew's blood
228shall be as noble as his blood. I'll be
229Thy executioner myself.

1 Judge.

230Restrain his fury. Good my lord, forbear.

D'Amville.

231I'll butcher out the passage of his soul
232That dares attempt to interrupt the blow.

2 Judge.

233My lord, the office will impress a mark
234Of scandal and dishonour on your name.

Charlemont.

235The office fits him; hinder not his hand,
236But let him crown my resolution with
237An unexampled dignity of death.
238Strike home. Thus I submit me.

[Makes] ready for execution.

Castabella.

Strike home. Thus I submit me. So do I.
239In scorn of death thus hand in hand we die.

D'Amville.

240I ha' the trick on 't, nephew. You shall see
241How eas’ly I can put you out of pain. —O.

As he raises up the axe [he] strikes out his own brains, [and then] staggers off the Scaffold.

Executioner.

242In lifting up the axe, I think h’ has knock’d
243His brains out.

D'Amville.

His brains out. What murderer was he
244That lifted up my hand against my head?

1 Judge.

245None but yourself, my lord.

D'Amville.

None but yourself, my lord. I thought he was
246A murderer that did it.

1 Judge.

A murderer that did it. God forbid.

D'Amville.

247Forbid? You lie, judge; he commanded it
248To tell thee that man's wisdom is a fool.
249I came to thee for judgement, and thou think'st
250Thyself a wise man. I outreach’d thy wit
251And made thy justice murder’s instrument
252In Castabella's death and Charlemont's.
253To crown my murder of Montferrers with
254A safe possession of his wealthy state.

Charlemont.

255I claim the just advantage of his words.

2 Judge.

256Descend the scaffold and attend the rest.

D'Amville.

257There was the strength of natural understanding.
258But Nature is a fool. There is a power
259Above her that hath overthrown the pride
260Of all my projects and posterity,
261For whose surviving blood I had erected
262A proud monument and struck 'em dead
263Before me, for whose deaths I call’d to thee
264For judgement. Thou didst want discretion for
265The sentence, but yond’ power that struck me knew
266The judgement I deserv’d, and gave it. O,
267The lust of death commits a rape upon me,
268As I would ha' done on Castabella.

Dies.

1 Judge.

269Strange is his death and judgement. With the hands
270Of joy and justice I thus set you free.
271The power of that eternal providence
272Which overthrew his projects in their pride
273Hath made your griefs the instruments to raise
274Your blessings to a greater height than ever.

Charlemont.

275Only to Heav’n I attribute the work,
276Whose gracious motives made me still forbear
277To be mine own revenger. Now I see,
278That patience is the honest man's revenge.

1 judge.

279Instead of Charlemont that but e'en now
280Stood ready to be dispossess’d of all,
281I now salute you with more titles, both
282Of wealth and dignity, than you were born to.
283And you, sweet madam, Lady of Belforest
284You have that title by your father's death.

Castabella.

285With all the titles due to me increase
286The wealth and honour of my Charlemont,
287Lord of Montferrers, Lord D’Amville, Belforest,
288And for a close to make up all the rest,

Embrace[s Charlemont]

289The lord of Castabella. Now at last
290Enjoy the full possession of my love,
291As clear and pure as my first chastity.

Charlemont.

292The crown of all my blessings! I will tempt
293My stars no longer, nor protract my time
294Of marriage. When those nuptial rites are done,
295I will perform my kinsmen's funerals.

1 Judge.

296The drums and trumpets interchange the sounds
297Of death and triumph for these honour’d lives
298Succeeding their deserved tragedies.

Charlemont.

299Thus by the work of Heav’n the men that thought
300To follow our dead bodies without tears,
301Are dead themselves, and now we follow theirs.

Exeunt
