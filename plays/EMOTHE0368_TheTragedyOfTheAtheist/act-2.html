Act II

[II.i]

Music. A banquet. In the night.

Enter D'Amville, Belforest, Levidulcia, Rousard, Castabella, Languebeau Snuffe at one door; at the other door Cataplasma and Soquette, ushered by Fresco.

Levidulcia.

Mistress Cataplasma, I expected you an hour since.

Cataplasma.

Certain ladies at my house, madam, detained me; otherwise I had attended your ladyship sooner.

Levidulcia.

We are beholding to you for you company. My Lord, I pray you bid these gentlewomen welcome; th' are my invited friends.

D'Amville.

Gentlewomen, y' are welcome; pray sit down.

Levidulcia.

Fresco, by my Lord D'Amville's leave I prithee go into the butt’ry. Thou sha’t find some o' my men there; if they bid thee not welcome, they are very loggerheads.

Fresco.

If your loggerheads will not, your hogsheads shall, madam, if I get into the butt’ry.

Exit.

D'Amville.

That fellow's disposition to mirth should be our pre- sent example. Let's be grave and meditate when our affairs require our seriousness. 'Tis out of season to be heavily disposed.

Levidulcia.

We should be all wound up into the key of mirth.

D'Amville.

The music there!

Belforest.

Where's my lord Montferrers? Tell him here's a room attends him.

Enter Montferrers.

Montferrers.

19Heaven give your marriage that I am deprived of, joy.

D'Amville.

20My Lord Belforest! Castabella's health.

D'Amville drinks.

21Set open 'the cellar doors, and let this health
22Go freely round the house. —Another to
23Your Son, my lord, to noble Charlemont.
24He is a Soldier. Let the onstruments
25Of wary congratulate his memory.

Enter a Servant.

Servant.

My lord, here's one i' th’ habit of a soldier says he is newly returned from Ostend and has some business of import to speak.

D'Amville.

28Ostend! Let him come in. My soul foretells
29He brings the news will make our music full.
30My brother's joy would do 't, and here comes he
31Will raise it.

Montferrers.

32My tongue to question him, as if it knew
33His answer would displease.

D'Amville.

His answer would displease. Soldier, what news?
34We heard a rumour of a blow you gave
35The enemy.

Borachio.

The enemy. 'Tis very true, my lord.

Belforest.

36Canst thou relate it?

Borachio.

Canst thou relate it? Yes.

D'Amville.

Canst thou relate it? Yes. I prithee do.

Borachio.

37The enemy, defeated of a fair
38Advantage by a flatt’ring stratagem,
39Plants all th' artillery against the town,
40Whose thunder and lightning made our bulwarks shake,
41And threat’ned in that terrible report
42The storm wherewith they meant to second it.
43Th' assault was general, but for the place
44That promis’d most advantage to be forc’d,
45The pride of all their army was drawn forth
46And equally divided into front
47And rear. They march’d, and coming to a stand,
48Ready to pass our channel at an ebb,
49W' advis’d it for our safest course to draw
50Our sluices up and make't unpassable.
51Our governor oppos’d, and suffer’d 'em
52To charge us home e'en to the rampier’s foot,
53But when their front was forcing up our breach
54At push o' pike, then did his policy
55Let go the sluices and tripp’d up the heels
56Of the whole body of their troop that stood
57Within the violent current of the stream.
58Their front, beleaguer’d twixt the water and
59The town, seeing the flood was grown too deep,
60To promise them a safe retreat, expos’d
61The force of all their spirits, like the last
62Expiring gasp of a strong-hearted man,
63Upon the hazard of one charge, but were
64Oppress’d and fell. The rest that could not swim
65Were only drown’d, but those that thought to ‘scape
66By swimming were by murderers that flanker’d
67The level of the flood both drown’d and slain

D'Amville.

68Now by my soul, soldier, a brave service.

Montferrers.

69O what became of my dear Charlemont?

Borachio.

70Walking next day upon the fatal shore,
71Among the slaughter’d bodies of their men,
72Which the full-stomach’d sea had cast upon
73The sands, it was m' unhappy chance to light
74Upon a face, whose favour when it liv’d
75My astonish’d mind inform’d me I had seen.
76He lay in 's armour as if that had been
77His coffin, and the weeping sea, like one
78Whose milder temper doth lament the death
79Of him whom in his rage he slew, runs up
80The shore, embraces him, kisses his cheek,
81Goes back again, and forces up the sands
82To bury him, and ev’ry time it parts
83Sheds tears upon him, till at last, as if
84It could no longer endure to see the man
85Whom it had slain, yet loath to leave him, with
86A kind of unresolv’d unwilling pace,
87Winding her waves one in another, like
88A man that folds his arms or wrings his hands
89For grief, ebb’d from the body and descends,
90As if it would sink down into the earth
91And hide itself for shame of such a deed.

D'Amville.

92And, soldier, who was this?

Montferrers.

And, soldier, who was this? O Charlemont!

Borachio.

93Your fear hath told you that whereof my grief
94Was loath to be the messenger.

Castabella.

Was loath to be the messenger. O God.

Exit.

D'Amville.

95Charlemont drown’d? Why how could that be, since
96It was the adverse party that received
97The overthrow?

Borachio.

98His forward spirit press’d into the front,
99And being engag’d within the enemy
100When they retreated through the rising stream,
101I' the violent confusion of the throng
102Was overborne and perish’d in the flood.
103And here's the sad remembrance of his life,

[He shows] the scarf.

104Which for his sake I will for ever wear.

Montferrers.

105Torment me not with witnesses of that
106Which I desire not to believe, yet must.

D'Amville.

107Thou art a screech-owl and dost come i' night
108To be the cursed messenger of death.
109Away. Depart my house, or, by my soul,
110You'll find me a more fatal enemy
111Than ever was Ostend. Be gone. Dispatch.

Borachio.

112Sir ’twas my love.

D'Amville.

Sir ’twas my love. Your love to vex my heart
113With that I hate? Hark, do you hear, you knave?
114O th’ art a most delicate sweet eloquent villain.aparte

Borachio.

115Was't not well counterfeited?aparte

D'Amville.

116Rarely.aparte

Borachio.

117Why then, farewell. I will not trouble you.

Exit.

D'Amville.

118So. The foundation's laid. Now by degreesaparte
119The work will rise and soon be perfected.aparte
120[To the others] O this uncertain state of mortal man!

Belforest.

121What then? It is th' inevitable fate
122Of all things underneath the moon.

D'Amville.

Of all things underneath the moon. 'Tis true.
123Brother, for health's sake overcome your grief.

Montferrers.

124I cannot, sir. I am uncapable
125Of comfort. My turn will be next. I feel
126Myself not well.

D'Amville.

Myself not well. You yield too much to grief.

D'Amville.

133I have my wish.aparte
134[To the others] Lights for my brother.

Montferrers.

[To the others] Lights for my brother. I'll withdraw a while,
135And crave the honest counsel of this man.

Belforest.

136With all my heart, I pray attend him, sir.

Exeunt Montferrers and [Languebeau] Snuffe.

137This next room, please your lordship.

D'Amville.

This next room, please your lordship. Where you will.

Exeunt Belforest and D'Amville.

Levidulcia.

My daughter's gone. Come son. Mistress Cataplasma, come; we'll up into her chamber. I'd fain see how she entertains the expectation of her husband's bedfellow- ship.

Rousard.

141'Faith, howsoever she entertains it, I
142Shall hardly please her; therefore let her rest.

Levidulcia.

143Nay, please her hardly, and you please her best.

[II.ii]

1 Servant.

Boy! Fill some drink, boy.

Fresco.

Enough, good sir; not a drop more by this light.

2 Servant.

Fresco.

No, no, no, no, no.

3 Servant.

Why then take thy liquor. A health, Fresco!

Kneel[s].

Fresco.

Your health will make me sick sir.

1 Servant.

Then 'twill bring you o' your knees I hope, sir.

Fresco.

May I not stand and pledge it, sir?

2 Servant.

I hope you will do as we do.

Fresco.

Nay then, indeed I must not stand, for you cannot.

3 Servant.

Well said, old boy.

Fresco.

Old boy! You'll make me a young child anon, for if I continue this, I shall scarce be able to go alone.

1 Servant.

My body is as weak as water, Fresco.

Fresco.

Good reason, sir. The beer has sent all the malt up into your brain and left nothing but the water in your body.

[II.iii]

Enter Castabella alone.

Castabella.

1O love, thou chaste affection of the soul,
2Without th' adult’rate mixture of the blood,
3That virtue which to goodness addeth good,
4The minion of Heaven's heart. Heaven, is 't my fate
5For loving that thou lov'st, to get thy hate?
6Or was my Charlemont thy chosen love,
7And therefore hast receiv’d him to thyself?
8Then I confess thy anger's not unjust.
9I was thy rival. Yet to be divorc’d
10From love has been a punishment enough,
11Sweet Heaven, without being marry’d unto hate.
12Hadst thou been pleas’d —Oh double misery!
13Yet, since thy pleasure hath inflicted it,
14If not my heart, my duty shall submit.

Enter Levidulcia, Rousard, Cataplasma, Soquette, and Fresco, with a lantern.

Levidulcia.

Mistress Cataplasma, good night. I pray when your man has brought you home, let him return and light me to my house.

Cataplasma.

He shall instantly wait on your ladyship.

Levidulcia.

Good, Mistress Cataplasma, for my servants are all drunk; I cannot be beholding to 'em for their attendance.

Exeunt Cataplasma, Soquette and Fresco.

20O here's your bride.

Rousard.

21Methinks.

Levidulcia.

Methinks. How can she choose? your sickness will
22Distaste th' expected sweetness o' the night.
23That makes her heavy.

Rousard.

That makes her heavy. That should make her light.

Levidulcia.

24Look you to that.

Castabella.

Look you to that. What sweetness speak you of?
25The sweetness of the night consists in rest.

Rousard.

26With that sweetness thou shalt be surely blest
27Unless my groaning wake thee. Do not moan.

Levidulcia.

28Sh' had rather you would wake and make her groan.

Rousard.

29Nay 'troth, sweetheart, I will not trouble thee.
30Thou shalt not lose thy maidenhead tonight.

Castabella.

31O might that weakness ever be in force,
32I never would desire to sue divorce.

Rousard.

33Wilt go to bed?

Castabella.

Wilt go to bed? I will attend you, sir.

Rousard.

34Mother, good night.

Levidulcia.

Exeunt Rousard and Castabella.

43The air to cool it.

Sebastian.

Levidulcia.

Sebastian.

49Under the protection of his present absence.

Levidulcia.

53Is that the way? I understand you not.

Has set my blood a-boiling i' my veins, and now, like water poured upon the ground that mixes itself with ev’ry moisture it meets, I could clasp with any man.

Give me a fellow that brings not only temptation with him, but has the activity of wit and audacity of spirit to apply every word and gesture of a woman's speech and behaviour to his own desire, and make her believe she's the suitor herself, never give back till he has made her yield to it.

Fresco.

Indeed among our equals, madam, but otherwise we shall be put horribly out o 'countenance.

Levidulcia.

Thou art deceived, Fresco. Ladies are as courteous as yeomen's wives, and methinks they should be more gentle. Hot diet and soft ease makes 'em, like wax always kept warm, more easy to take impression. —Prithee untie my shoe. —What, art thou shamefaced too? Go roundly to work, man. My leg is not gouty; 'twill endure the feeling, I warrant thee. Come hither, Fresco; thine ear. — 'S dainty, I mistook the place; I missed thine ear and hit thy lip.

That shows th' art full o' lusty blood and thou knowest not how to use it. Let me see thy hand. Thou shouldst not be shamefaced by thy hand, Fresco. They are like candied suckets when they begin to perish, which I would always empty my closet of and give 'em my chambermaid. —I have some skill in palmistry; by this line that stands directly against me thou shouldst be near a good fortune, Fresco, if thou hadst the grace to en- tertain it.

Do you cry, sirrah? says he. Let me see your heel; if it be not hurt, I'll make you cry for something. So he claps my head between his legs, and pulls off my shoe. I having shifted no socks in a sennight, the gentleman cried Foh, and said my feet were base and cowardly feet; they stunk for fear. Then he knocked my shoe about my pate, and I cried O, once more. In the meantime comes a shag-haired dog by and rubs against his shins. The gentleman took the dog in shag-hair to be some watch- man in a rug gown, and swore he would hang me up at the next door with my lantern in my hand, that passengers might see their way as they went without rubbing against gentlemen's shins.
