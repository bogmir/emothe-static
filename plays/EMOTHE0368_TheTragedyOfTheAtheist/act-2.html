Act II

[II.i]

Music. A banquet. In the night.

Enter D'Amville, Belforest, Levidulcia, Rousard, Castabella, Languebeau Snuffe at one door; at the other door Cataplasma and Soquette, ushered by Fresco.

Levidulcia.

1Mistress Cataplasma, I expected you an hour since.

Cataplasma.

2Certain ladies at my house, madam, detained me; otherwise I had attended your ladyship sooner.

Levidulcia.

3We are beholding to you for you company. My Lord, I 4pray you bid these gentlewomen welcome; th' are my 5invited friends.

D'Amville.

6Gentlewomen, y' are welcome; pray sit down.

Levidulcia.

7Fresco, by my Lord D'Amville's leave I prithee go into 8the butt’ry. Thou sha’t find some o' my men there; if they 9bid thee not welcome, they are very loggerheads.

Fresco.

10If your loggerheads will not, your hogsheads shall, 11madam, if I get into the butt’ry.

Exit.

D'Amville.

12That fellow's disposition to mirth should be our pre- 13sent example. Let's be grave and meditate when our 14affairs require our seriousness. 'Tis out of season to be 15heavily disposed.

Levidulcia.

16We should be all wound up into the key of mirth.

D'Amville.

17The music there!

Belforest.

18Where's my lord Montferrers? Tell him here's a room attends him.

Enter Montferrers.

Montferrers.

19Heaven give your marriage that I am deprived of, joy.

D'Amville.

20My Lord Belforest! Castabella's health.

D'Amville drinks.

21Set open 'the cellar doors, and let this health
22Go freely round the house. —Another to
23Your Son, my lord, to noble Charlemont.
24He is a Soldier. Let the onstruments
25Of wary congratulate his memory.

Drums and Trumpets.

Enter a Servant.

Servant.

26My lord, here's one i' th’ habit of a soldier says he is newly returned from Ostend and has some business of import to 27speak.

D'Amville.

28Ostend! Let him come in. My soul foretells
29He brings the news will make our music full.
30My brother's joy would do 't, and here comes he
31Will raise it.

Enter Borachio disguised.

Montferrers.

Will raise it. O my spirit, it does dissuade
32My tongue to question him, as if it knew
33His answer would displease.

D'Amville.

His answer would displease. Soldier, what news?
34We heard a rumour of a blow you gave
35The enemy.

Borachio.

The enemy. 'Tis very true, my lord.

Belforest.

36Canst thou relate it?

Borachio.

Canst thou relate it? Yes.

D'Amville.

Canst thou relate it? Yes. I prithee do.

Borachio.

37The enemy, defeated of a fair
38Advantage by a flatt’ring stratagem,
39Plants all th' artillery against the town,
40Whose thunder and lightning made our bulwarks shake,
41And threat’ned in that terrible report
42The storm wherewith they meant to second it.
43Th' assault was general, but for the place
44That promis’d most advantage to be forc’d,
45The pride of all their army was drawn forth
46And equally divided into front
47And rear. They march’d, and coming to a stand,
48Ready to pass our channel at an ebb,
49W' advis’d it for our safest course to draw
50Our sluices up and make't unpassable.
51Our governor oppos’d, and suffer’d 'em
52To charge us home e'en to the rampier’s foot,
53But when their front was forcing up our breach
54At push o' pike, then did his policy
55Let go the sluices and tripp’d up the heels
56Of the whole body of their troop that stood
57Within the violent current of the stream.
58Their front, beleaguer’d twixt the water and
59The town, seeing the flood was grown too deep,
60To promise them a safe retreat, expos’d
61The force of all their spirits, like the last
62Expiring gasp of a strong-hearted man,
63Upon the hazard of one charge, but were
64Oppress’d and fell. The rest that could not swim
65Were only drown’d, but those that thought to ‘scape
66By swimming were by murderers that flanker’d
67The level of the flood both drown’d and slain

D'Amville.

68Now by my soul, soldier, a brave service.

Montferrers.

69O what became of my dear Charlemont?

Borachio.

70Walking next day upon the fatal shore,
71Among the slaughter’d bodies of their men,
72Which the full-stomach’d sea had cast upon
73The sands, it was m' unhappy chance to light
74Upon a face, whose favour when it liv’d
75My astonish’d mind inform’d me I had seen.
76He lay in 's armour as if that had been
77His coffin, and the weeping sea, like one
78Whose milder temper doth lament the death
79Of him whom in his rage he slew, runs up
80The shore, embraces him, kisses his cheek,
81Goes back again, and forces up the sands
82To bury him, and ev’ry time it parts
83Sheds tears upon him, till at last, as if
84It could no longer endure to see the man
85Whom it had slain, yet loath to leave him, with
86A kind of unresolv’d unwilling pace,
87Winding her waves one in another, like
88A man that folds his arms or wrings his hands
89For grief, ebb’d from the body and descends,
90As if it would sink down into the earth
91And hide itself for shame of such a deed.

D'Amville.

92And, soldier, who was this?

Montferrers.

And, soldier, who was this? O Charlemont!

Borachio.

93Your fear hath told you that whereof my grief
94Was loath to be the messenger.

Castabella.

Was loath to be the messenger. O God.

Exit.

D'Amville.

95Charlemont drown’d? Why how could that be, since
96It was the adverse party that received
97The overthrow?

Borachio.

98His forward spirit press’d into the front,
99And being engag’d within the enemy
100When they retreated through the rising stream,
101I' the violent confusion of the throng
102Was overborne and perish’d in the flood.
103And here's the sad remembrance of his life,

[He shows] the scarf.

104Which for his sake I will for ever wear.

Montferrers.

105Torment me not with witnesses of that
106Which I desire not to believe, yet must.

D'Amville.

107Thou art a screech-owl and dost come i' night
108To be the cursed messenger of death.
109Away. Depart my house, or, by my soul,
110You'll find me a more fatal enemy
111Than ever was Ostend. Be gone. Dispatch.

Borachio.

112Sir ’twas my love.

D'Amville.

Sir ’twas my love. Your love to vex my heart
113With that I hate? Hark, do you hear, you knave?
114[Aside to Borachio] O th’ art a most delicate sweet eloquent villain.aparte

Borachio.

115[Aside to D’Amville] Was't not well counterfeited?aparte

D'Amville.

116[Aside to Borachio] Rarely. [Aloud] Be gone; I will not here reply.aparte

Borachio.

117Why then, farewell. I will not trouble you.

Exit.

D'Amville.

118[Aside] So. The foundation's laid. Now by degreesaparte
119The work will rise and soon be perfected.
120[To the others] O this uncertain state of mortal man!

Belforest.

121What then? It is th' inevitable fate
122Of all things underneath the moon.

D'Amville.

Of all things underneath the moon. 'Tis true.
123Brother, for health's sake overcome your grief.

Montferrers.

124I cannot, sir. I am uncapable
125Of comfort. My turn will be next. I feel
126Myself not well.

D'Amville.

Myself not well. You yield too much to grief.

Languebeau.

127All men are mortal. The hour of death is uncertain. 128Age makes sickness the more dangerous, and grief is 129subject to distraction. You know not how soon you may 130be deprived of the benefit of sense. In my understanding, 131therefore, you shall do well if you be sick to set your 132state in present order. Make your will.

D'Amville.

133[Aside] I have my wish.aparte
134[To the others] Lights for my brother.

Montferrers.

[To the others] Lights for my brother. I'll withdraw a while,
135And crave the honest counsel of this man.

Belforest.

136With all my heart, I pray attend him, sir.

Exeunt Montferrers and [Languebeau] Snuffe.

137This next room, please your lordship.

D'Amville.

This next room, please your lordship. Where you will.

Exeunt Belforest and D'Amville.

Levidulcia.

138My daughter's gone. Come son. Mistress Cataplasma, come; we'll up into her chamber. I'd fain see how she 139entertains the expectation of her husband's bedfellow- 140ship.

Rousard.

141'Faith, howsoever she entertains it, I
142Shall hardly please her; therefore let her rest.

Levidulcia.

143Nay, please her hardly, and you please her best.

Exeunt.

[II.ii]

Enter three servants, drunk, drawing in Fresco.

1 Servant.

1Boy! Fill some drink, boy.

Fresco.

2Enough, good sir; not a drop more by this light.

2 Servant.

3Not by this light? Why then put out the candles, and 4we'll drink i' the dark and t' wou’t, old boy.

Fresco.

5No, no, no, no, no.

3 Servant.

6Why then take thy liquor. A health, Fresco!

Kneel[s].

Fresco.

7Your health will make me sick sir.

1 Servant.

8Then 'twill bring you o' your knees I hope, sir.

Fresco.

9May I not stand and pledge it, sir?

2 Servant.

10I hope you will do as we do.

Fresco.

11Nay then, indeed I must not stand, for you cannot.

3 Servant.

12Well said, old boy.

Fresco.

13Old boy! You'll make me a young child anon, for if I 14continue this, I shall scarce be able to go alone.

1 Servant.

15My body is as weak as water, Fresco.

Fresco.

16Good reason, sir. The beer has sent all the malt up into 17your brain and left nothing but the water in your body.

Enter D'Amville and Borachio, closely observing their drunkenness.

D'Amville.

18Borachio, seest those fellows?

Borachio.

Borachio, seest those fellows? Yes, my lord.

D'Amville.

19Their drunkenness that seems ridiculous,
20Shall be a serious instrument to bring
21Our sober purposes to their success.

Borachio.

22I am prepar’d for th' execution, sir.

D'Amville.

23Cast off this habit and about it straight.

Borachio.

24Let them drink healths and drown their brains i' the flood;
25I'll promise them they shall be pledg’d in blood.

Exit.

1 Servant.

26You ha' left a damnable snuff here.

2 Servant.

27Do you take that in snuff, sir?

1 Servant.

28You are a damnable rogue then.

[They fall] together by th' ears.

D'Amville.

29[Asside] Fortune, I honour thee. My plot still risesaparte
30According to the model of mine own desires.

31[To the others] Lights for my brother! What, ha' you drunk yourselves mad, you knaves.

1 Servant.

32My lord, the jacks abused me.

D'Amville.

33I think they are the jacks indeed that have abused thee. 34Dost hear? That fellow is a proud knave. He has abused 35thee. As thou goest over the fields by and by in lighting 36my brother home, I'll tell thee what sha’t do: knock him 37over the pate with thy torch; I'll bear thee out in't.

1 Servant.

38I will singe the goose by this torch.

Exit.

D'Amville.

39[to the Second Servant] Dost hear, fellow? Seest thou that proud knave? I have given him a lesson for his 40sauciness. ‘Has wrong’d thee. I'll tell thee what sha’t do: 41as we go over the fields by and by, clap him suddenly o'er 42the coxcomb with thy torch; I'll bear thee out in't.

2 Servant.

43I will make him understand as much.

Exit.

Enter Languebeau Snuffe.

D'Amville.

44Now, Monsieur Snuffe, what has my brother done?

Languebeau.

45Made his will, and by that will made you his heir, with 46this proviso, that as occasion shall hereafter move him, he 47may revoke or alter it when he pleases.

D'Amville.

48Yes, let him if he can. — (Aside) I'll make it sureaparte
49From his revoking.

Enter Montferrers and Belforest, attended with lights.

Montferrers.

From his revoking. Brother, now good night.

D'Amville.

50The sky is dark; we'll bring you o'er the fields.
51[Aside] Who can but strike wants wisdom to maintain;aparte
52He that strikes safe and sure has heart and brain.

Exeunt.

[II.iii]

Enter Castabella alone.

Castabella.

1O love, thou chaste affection of the soul,
2Without th' adult’rate mixture of the blood,
3That virtue which to goodness addeth good,
4The minion of Heaven's heart. Heaven, is 't my fate
5For loving that thou lov'st, to get thy hate?
6Or was my Charlemont thy chosen love,
7And therefore hast receiv’d him to thyself?
8Then I confess thy anger's not unjust.
9I was thy rival. Yet to be divorc’d
10From love has been a punishment enough,
11Sweet Heaven, without being marry’d unto hate.
12Hadst thou been pleas’d —Oh double misery!
13Yet, since thy pleasure hath inflicted it,
14If not my heart, my duty shall submit.

Enter Levidulcia, Rousard, Cataplasma, Soquette, and Fresco, with a lantern.

Levidulcia.

15Mistress Cataplasma, good night. I pray when your man has brought you home, let him return and light me to my 16house.

Cataplasma.

17He shall instantly wait on your ladyship.

Levidulcia.

18Good, Mistress Cataplasma, for my servants are all 19drunk; I cannot be beholding to 'em for their attendance.

Exeunt Cataplasma, Soquette and Fresco.

20O here's your bride.

Rousard.

O here's your bride. And melancholic too,
21Methinks.

Levidulcia.

Methinks. How can she choose? your sickness will
22Distaste th' expected sweetness o' the night.
23That makes her heavy.

Rousard.

That makes her heavy. That should make her light.

Levidulcia.

24Look you to that.

Castabella.

Look you to that. What sweetness speak you of?
25The sweetness of the night consists in rest.

Rousard.

26With that sweetness thou shalt be surely blest
27Unless my groaning wake thee. Do not moan.

Levidulcia.

28Sh' had rather you would wake and make her groan.

Rousard.

29Nay 'troth, sweetheart, I will not trouble thee.
30Thou shalt not lose thy maidenhead tonight.

Castabella.

31O might that weakness ever be in force,
32I never would desire to sue divorce.

Rousard.

33Wilt go to bed?

Castabella.

Wilt go to bed? I will attend you, sir.

Rousard.

34Mother, good night.

Levidulcia.

Mother, good night. Pleasure be your bedfellow.

Exeunt Rousard and Castabella.

35Why sure their generation was asleep
36When she begot those dormice, that she made
37Them up so weakly and imperfectly.
38One wants desire, the t'other ability—
39When my affection even with their cold bloods,
40As snow rubb’d through an active hand does make
41The flesh to burn, by agitation is
42Inflam’d! I could unbrace and entertain
43The air to cool it.

Enter Sebastian.

Sebastian.

The air to cool it. That but mitigates
44The heat; rather embrace and entertain
45A younger brother; he can quench the fire.

Levidulcia.

46Can you so, sir? Now I beshrew your ear.
47Why, bold Sebastian, how dare you approach
48So near the presence of your displeas’d father.

Sebastian.

49Under the protection of his present absence.

Levidulcia.

50Belike you knew he was abroad then?

Sebastian.

Belike you knew he was abroad then? Yes.
51Let me encounter you so; I'll persuade
52Your means to reconcile me to his love.

Levidulcia.

53Is that the way? I understand you not.
54But for your reconcilement, meet m' at home;
55I'll satisfy your suit.

Sebastian.

I'll satisfy your suit. Within this half hour?

Exit

Levidulcia.

56Or within this whole hour. When you will. —A lusty 57blood! Has both the presence and the spirit of a man. I 58like the freedom of his behaviour. Ho! Sebastian! Gone? 59Has set my blood a-boiling i' my veins, and now, like 60water poured upon the ground that mixes itself with 61ev’ry moisture it meets, I could clasp with any man.

Enter Fresco with a lantern.

62O Fresco, art thou come?
63If t'other fail, then thou art entertain’d.
64Lust is a spirit which whosoe’er doth raise,
65The next man that encounters boldly lays.

Exeunt.

[II.iv]

Enter Borachio warily and hastily over the stage, with a stone in either hand.

Borachio.

1Such stones men use to raise a house upon,
2But with these stones I go to ruin one.

Descends.

Enter two Servants, drunk, fighting with their torches; D'Amville, Montferrers, Belforest, and Languebeau Snuffe.

Belforest.

3Passion o' me, you drunken knaves, you'll put
4The lights out.

D'Amville.

The lights out. No, my lord, th' are but in jest.

1 Servant.

5Mine's out.

D'Amville.

6Then light it at his head; that's light enough.
7‘Fore God, th' are out. You drunken rascals, back
8And light 'em.

Belforest.

And light 'em. 'Tis exceeding dark.

Exeunt Servants.

D'Amville.

And light 'em. 'Tis exceeding dark. No matter;
9I am acquainted with the way. Your hand.
10Let's easily walk. I'll lead you till they come.

Montferrers.

11My soul's oppress’d with grief. ’T lies heavy at
12My heart. O my departed son, ere long
13I shall be with thee.

D'Amville thrusts him down into the gravel pit.

D'Amville.

I shall be with thee. Marry, God forbid!

Montferrers.

14O, O, O.

D'Amville.

15Now all the host of heaven forbid! Knaves! Rogues!

Belforest.

16Pray God he be not hurt! He's fall’n into the gravel pit.

D'Amville.

17Brother, dear brother! Rascals, villains, knaves!

Enter the Servants with lights.

18Eternal darkness damn you; come away.
19Go round about into the gravel pit,
20And help my brother up. Why what a strange
21Unlucky night is this! Is 't not, my lord?
22I think that dog that howl’d the news of grief,
23That fatal screech-owl, usher’d on this mischief.

Enter [Servants] with the murdered body.

Lan.

24Mischief indeed, my lord. Your brother's dead.

Belforest.

25He's dead.

1 Servant.

He's dead. He's dead.

D'Amville.

He's dead. He's dead. Dead be your tongues! Drop out
26Mine eye-balls, and let envious Fortune play
27At tennis with 'em. Have I liv’d to this?
28Malicious Nature! Hadst thou born me blind,
29Th' hadst yet been something favourable to me.
30No breath? No motion? Prithee tell me, Heaven,
31Hast shut thine eye to wink at murder, or
32Hast put this sable garment on to mourn
33At 's death?
34Not one poor spark in the whole spacious sky
35Of all that endless number would vouchsafe
36To shine? You viceroys to the king of Nature!
37Whose constellations govern mortal births,
38Where is that fatal planet rul’d at his
39Nativity? That might ha' pleas’d to light
40Him out, as well into th' world, unless
41It be asham’d t’ have been the instrument
42Of such a good man's cursed destiny.

Belforest.

43Passions transports you. Recollect yourself.
44Lament him not. Whether our deaths be good
45Or bad, it is not death but life that tries.
46He liv’d well, therefore questionless well dies.

D'Amville.

47Ay, ’tis an easy thing for him that has
48No pain to talk of patience. Do you think
49That Nature has no feeling?

Belforest.

That Nature has no feeling? Feeling? Yes.
50But has she purpos’d anything for nothing?
51What good receives this body by your grief?
52Whether is 't more unnatural not to grieve
53For him you cannot help with it, or hurt
54Yourself with grieving and yet grieve in vain?

D'Amville.

55Indeed, had he been taken from me like
56A piece o' dead flesh, I should neither ha' felt it
57Nor griev’d for 't. But come hither, pray look here.
58Behold the lively tincture of his blood!
59Neither the dropsy nor the jaundice in't,
60But the true freshness of a sanguine red,
61For all the fog of this black murd’rous night
62Has mix’d with it. For anything, I know,
63He might ha' liv’d till doomsday and ha' done
64More good than either you or I. O Brother!
65He was a man of such a native goodness,
66As if regeneration had been given
67Him in his mother's womb, so harmless
68That rather than ha' trod upon a worm
69He would ha' shunn’d the way, so dearly pitiful
70That ere the poor could ask his charity
71With dry eyes, he gave 'em relief wi' tears—
72With tears — yes, faith, with tears.

Belforest.

With tears — yes, faith, with tears. Take up the corpse.
73For wisdom's sake, let reason fortify
74This weakness.

D'Amville.

This weakness. Why, what would you ha' me do?
75Foolish Nature will have her course in spite
76O' wisdom. But I have e'en done.
77All these words were but a great wind, and now
78This shower of tears has laid it, I am calm
79Again. You may set forward when you will.
80I'll follow you like one that must and would not.

Languebeau.

81Our opposition will but trouble him.

Belforest.

82The grief that melts to tears by itself is spent;
83Passion resisted grows more violent.

Exeunt [all except] D’Amville. Borachio ascends.

D'Amville.

84Here's a sweet comedy. ’T begins with O
85Dolentis and concludes with ha, ha, he.

Borachio.

86Ha, ha, he.

D'Amville.

Ha, ha, he. O my echo! I could stand
87Reverberating this sweet musical air
88Of joy till I had perish’d my sound lungs
89With violent laughter. Lovely night-raven!
90Th' hast seiz’d a carcass.

Borachio.

Th' hast seiz’d a carcass. Put him out on's pain.
91I lay so fitly underneath the bank
92From whence he fell, that ere his falt’ring tongue
93Could utter double O, I knock’d out's brains
94With this fair ruby, and had another stone
95Just of this form and bigness ready; that
96I laid i' the broken skull upo' the ground
97For 's pillow, against the which they thought he fell
98And perish’d.

D'Amville.

99Upon this ground I'll build my manor house,
100And this shall be the chiefest corner-stone.

Borachio.

101‘T has crown’d the most judicious murder that
102The brain of man was e'er deliver’d of.

D'Amville.

103Ay, mark the plot. Not any circumstance
104That stood within the reach of the design
105Of persons, dispositions, matter, time,
106Or place, but by this brain of mine was made
107An instrumental help, yet nothing from
108Th' induction to th' accomplishment seem’d forc’d,
109Or done o' purpose, but by accident.

Borachio.

110First my report that Charlemont was dead,
111Though false, yet cover’d with a mask of truth.

D'Amville.

112Ay, and deliver’d in as fit a time,
113When all our minds so wholly were possess’d
114With one affair, that no man would suspect
115A thought employ’d for any second end.

Borachio.

116Then the precisian to be ready when
117Your brother spake of death, to move his will.

D'Amville.

118His business call’d him thither, and it fell
119Within his office, unrequested to 't.
120From him it came religiously and sav’d
121Our project from suspicion, which if I
122Had mov’d had been endanger’d.

Borachio.

Had mov’d had been endanger’d. Then your healths,
123Though seeming but the ordinary rites
124And ceremonies due to festivals—

D'Amville.

125Yet us’d by me to make the servants drunk,
126An instrument the plot could not have miss’d.
127‘Twas easy to set drunkards by the ears;
128Th’ had nothing but their torches to fight with,
129And when those lights were out—

Borachio.

And when those lights were out— Then darkness did
130Protect the execution of the work
131Both from prevention and discovery.

D'Amville.

132Here was a murder bravely carry’d through
133The eye of observation, unobserv’d.

Borachio.

134And those that saw the passage of it made
135The Instruments, yet knew not what they did.

D'Amville.

136That power of rule philosophers ascribe
137To him they call the supreme of the stars,
138Making their influences governors
139Of sublunary creatures, when their selves
140Are senseless of their operations—

Thunder and lightning.

Are senseless of their operations— What!
141Dost start at thunder? Credit my belief,
142'Tis a mere effect of Nature,
143An exhalation hot and dry, involv’d
144Within a wat’ry vapour i' the middle
145Region of the air, whose coldness
146Congealing that thick moisture to a cloud,
147The angry exhalation shut within
148A prison of contrary quality,
149Strives to be free, and with the violent
150Eruption through the grossness of that cloud
151Makes this noise we hear.

Borachio.

Makes this noise we hear. 'Tis a fearful noise.

D'Amville.

152'Tis a brave noise,
153And methinks graces our accomplished
154Project as a peal of ordnance
155Does a triumph; it speaks encouragement.
156Now Nature shows thee how it favour’d our
157Performance, to forbear this noise when we
158Set forth because it should not terrify
159My brother’s going home, which would have dash’d
160Our purpose —to forbear this lightning
161In our passage, lest it should ha' warn’d him
162O' the pitfall. Then propitious Nature wink’d
163At our proceedings; now it doth express
164How that forbearance favour’d our success.

Borachio.

165You have confirm’d me, for it follows well

Enter the ghost of Montferrers.

Montferrers.

17Return to France, for thy old father's dead
18And thou by murder disinherited.
19Attend with patience the success of things,
20But leave revenge unto the King of kings.

Exit.

Charlemont starts and wakes.

Charlemont.

21O my affrighted soul, what fearful dream
22Was this that wak’d me? Dreams are but the rais’d
23Impressions of premeditated things,
24By serious apprehension left upon
25Our minds, or else th' imaginary shapes
26Of objects proper to th' complexion or
27The dispositions of our bodies. These
28Can neither of them be the cause why I
29Should dream thus, for my mind has not been mov’d
30With any one conception of a thought
31To such a purpose, nor my nature wont
32To trouble me with fantasies of terror.
33It must be something that my Genius would
34Inform me of. Now gracious heaven forbid!
35O, let my spirit be depriv’d of all
36Foresight and knowledge ere it understand
37That vision acted, or divine that act
38To come. Why should I think so? Left I not
39My worthy father i' the kind regard
40Of a most loving uncle? Soldier, saw'st
41No apparition of a man?

Musketeer.

No apparition of a man? You dream,
42Sir; I saw nothing.

Charlemont.

Sir; I saw nothing. Tush, these idle dreams
43Are fabulous. Our boiling fantasies
44Like troubled waters falsify the shapes
45Of things retain’d in them, and make 'em seem
46Confounded when they are distinguish’d. So
47My actions daily conversant with war,
48The argument of blood and death, had left,
49Perhaps, th' imaginary presence of
50Some bloody accident upon my mind,
51Which mix’d confusedly with other thoughts,
52Whereof th' remembrance of my father might
53Be one, presented all together seem
54Incorporate, as if his body were
55The owner of that blood, the subject of
56That death, when he's at Paris and that blood
57Shed here. It may be thus. I would not leave
58The war, for reputation's sake, upon
59An idle apprehension, a vain dream.
