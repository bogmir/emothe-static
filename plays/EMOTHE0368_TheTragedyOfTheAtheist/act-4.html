Act IV

[IV.i]

Enter Cataplasma and Soquette with needlework.

Cataplasma.

Come, Soquette, your work; let's examine your work. What's here? A medlar with a plum tree growing hard by it, the leaves o’ the plum tree falling off, the gum issuing out o’ the perished joints, and the branches some of 'em dead and some rotten, and yet but a young plum tree. In good sooth, very pretty.

Soquette.

The plum tree, forsooth, grows so near the medlar that the medlar sucks and draws all the sap from it and the natural strength o’ the ground, so that it cannot prosper.

Cataplasma.

How conceited you are! But here th’ hast made a tree to bear no fruit. Why's that?

Soquette.

There grows a savin tree next it, forsooth.

Cataplasma.

Forsooth, you are a little too witty in that.

Enter Sebastian.

Sebastian.

[Embracing her] But this honeysuckle winds about this whitethorn very prettily and lovingly, sweet Mistress Cataplasma.

Cataplasma.

Monsieur Sebastian! In good sooth, very uprightly welcome this evening.

Sebastian.

What, moralizing upon this gentlewoman's needlework? Let's see.

Cataplasma.

No, sir, only examining whether it be done to the true nature and life o’ the thing.

Sebastian.

Here y’ have set a medlar with a bachelor’s button o’ one side, and a snail o’ th' t’other. The bachelor’s button should have held his head up more pertly towards the medlar; the snail o’ th' t’other side should ha' been wrought with an artificial laziness, doubling his tail and putting out his horn but half the length, and then the medlar falling, as it were, from the lazy snail and inclining towards the pert bachelor’s button, their branches spreading and winding one within another as if they did embrace. But here's a moral. A popp’ring pear tree growing upon the bank of a river, seeming continually to look downwards into the water as if it were enamoured of it, and ever as the fruit ripens lets it fall for love, as it were, into her lap; which the wanton stream, like a strumpet, no sooner receives but she carries it away and bestows it upon some other creature she maintains, still seeming to play and dally under the popp’ring so long that it has almost washed away the earth from the root, and now the poor tree stands as if it were ready to fall and perish by that whereon it spent all the substance it had.

Cataplasma.

Moral for you that love those wanton running waters.

Sebastian.

But is not my Lady Levidulcia come yet?

Cataplasma.

Her purpose promised us her company ere this. Lirie, your lute and your book.

Sebastian.

Well said. A lesson o’ th' lute to entertain the time with till she comes.

Cataplasma.

Sol, fa, mi, la — Mi, mi mi. — Precious! Dost not see mi between the two crochets? Strike me full there. —So — forward. —This is a sweet strain, and thou finger’st it beastly. Mi is a large there, and the prick that stands before mi , a long; always halve your note. Now —run your division pleasingly with those quavers. Observe all your graces i' the touch. Here's a sweet close —strike it full; it sets off your music delicately.

Enter Languebeau Snuffe and Levidulcia.

Languebeau.

Purity be in this house.

Cataplasma.

'Tis now entered, and welcome with your good lady- ship.

Sebastian.

Cease that music. Here's a sweeter instrument.

[Goes to embrace her.]

Levidulcia.

Restrain your liberty. See you not Snuffe?

Sebastian.

What does the stinkard here? Put Snuffe out. He's offensive.

Levidulcia.

No. The credit of his company defends my being abroad from the eye of suspicion.

Cataplasma.

Wil 't please your ladyship go up into the closet? There are those falls and tires I told you of.

Levidulcia.

Monsieur Snuffe, I shall request your patience. My stay will not be long.

Exit with Sebastian.

Languebeau.

My duty, madam. Falls and tires? I begin to suspect what falls and tires you mean. My lady and Sebastian the fall and the tire, and I the shadow. I perceive the purity of my conversation is used but for a property to cover the uncleanness of their purposes. The very contemplation o’ the thing makes the spirit of the flesh begin to wriggle in my blood. And here my desire has met with an object already. This gentlewoman, methinks, should be swayed with the motion, living in a house where moving example is so common. Temptation has prevailed over me, and I will attempt to make it overcome her. —Mistress Cata- plasma, my lady, it seems, has some business that requires her stay. The fairness o’ the evening invites me into the air; will it please you give this gentlewoman leave to leave her work, and walk a turn or two with me for honest recreation?

Cataplasma.

With all my heart, sir. Go, Soquette, give ear to his instructions. You may get understanding by his com- pany, I can tell you.

Languebeau.

81In the way of holiness, Mistress Cataplasma.

Cataplasma.

82Good Monsieur Snuffe! I will attend your return.

Languebeau.

83[To Soquette] Your hand, gentlewoman.
84The flesh is humble till the spirit move it,aparte
85But when 'tis rais’d it will command above it.aparte

Exeunt.

[IV.ii]

Enter D'Amville, Charlemont, and Borachio.

D'Amville.

1Your sadness and the sickness of my son
2Have made our company and conference
3Less free and pleasing then I purpos’d it.

Charlemont.

4Sir, for the present I am much unfit
5For conversation or society.
6With pardon I will rudely take my leave.

D'Amville.

7Good night, dear nephew.

Exit Charlemont.

Good night, dear nephew. Seest thou that same man?

Borachio.

8Your meaning, sir?

D'Amville.

Your meaning, sir? That fellow's life, Borachio,
9Like a superfluous letter in the Law,
10Endangers our assurance.

Borachio.

Endangers our assurance. Scrape him out.

D'Amville.

11Wou’t do ’t?

Borachio.

Wou’t do ’t? Give me your purpose; I will do 't.

D'Amville.

12Sad melancholy has drawn Charlemont
13With meditation on his father's death,
14Into the solitary walk behind the Church.

Borachio.

15The churchyard? This the fittest place for death.
16Perhaps he's praying. Then he's fit to die.
17We'll send him charitably to his grave.

D'Amville.

18No matter how thou tak'st him. First take this.

[Gives him a] pistol.

19Thou know’st the place. Observe his passages,
20And with the most advantage make a stand,
21That favour’d by the darkness of the night,
22His breast may fall upon thee at so near
23A distance that he sha' not shun the blow.
24The deed once done, thou may'st retire with safety.
25The place is unfrequented, and his death
26Will be imputed to th' attempt of thieves.

Borachio.

27Be careless. Let your mind be free and clear.
28This pistol shall discharge you of your fear.

Exit.

D'Amville.

29But let me call my projects to account,
30For what effect and end I have engag’d
31Myself in all this blood. To leave a state
32To the succession of my proper blood.
33But how shall that succession be continu’d?
34Not in my elder son, I fear. Disease
35And weakness have disabled him for issue.
36For th' t’other, his loose humour will endure
37No bond of marriage. And I doubt his life;
38His spirit is so boldly dangerous.
39O pity that the profitable end
40Of such a prosp’rous murder should be lost!
41Nature forbid. I hope I have a body
42That will not suffer me to lose my labour
43For want of issue yet. But then 't must be
44A Bastard. Tush, they only father bastards
45That father other men's begettings. Daughter!
46Be it mine own, let it come whence it will.
47I am resolv’d. Daughter!

Enter Servant.

Servant.

I am resolv’d. Daughter! My lord.

D'Amville.

48I prithee call my daughter.

Enter Castabella.

Castabella.

49Your pleasure, sir?

D'Amville.

Your pleasure, sir? Is thy husband i' bed?

Castabella.

50Yes, my lord.

D'Amville.

Yes, my lord. The evening's fair. I prithee
51Walk a turn or two.

Castabella.

Walk a turn or two. Come, Jaspar.

D'Amville.

Walk a turn or two. Come, Jaspar. No.
52We'll walk but to the corner o' the church,
53And I have something to speak privately.

Castabella.

54No matter; stay.

Exit Servant.

D'Amville.

No matter; stay. This falls out happily.

Exeunt.

[IV.iii]

Enter Charlemont, Borachio, dogging him in the churchyard. The clock strikes twelve.

Charlemont.

Twelve.

Borachio.

'Tis a good hour; 'twill strike one anon.

Charlemont.

How fit a place for contemplation is this dead of night, among the dwellings of the dead. — This grave —perhaps th' inhabitant was in his lifetime the possessor of his own desires. Yet in the midst of all his greatness and his wealth he was less rich and less contented than in this poor piece of earth, lower and lesser than a cottage, for here he neither wants nor cares.

9Now that his body favours of corruption,
10He enjoys a sweeter rest than e'er he did
11Amongst the sweetest pleasures of this life,
12For here, there's nothing troubles him. —And there—
13In that grave lies another. He, perhaps,
14Was in his life as full of misery
15As this of happiness, and here's an end
16Of both. Now both their states are equal. O
17That man with so much labour should aspire
18To worldly height, when in the humble earth
19The world's condition's at the best! Or scorn
20Inferior men, since to be lower than
21A worm is to be higher than a king.

Borachio.

22Then fall and rise.

Discharges [the pistol, wich] gives false fire.

Charlemont.

Then fall and rise. What villain's hand was that?
23Save thee or thou shalt perish.

They fight.

Borachio.

Save thee or thou shalt perish. Zounds, unsav’d, I think.

Fall[s].

Charlemont.

24What, have I kill'd him? Whatsoe'er thou beest,
25I would thy hand had prosper’d, for I was
26Unfit to live and well prepar’d to die.
27What shall I do? Accuse myself, submit
28Me to the law, and that will quickly end
29This violent increase of misery.
30But 'tis a murder to be accessory
31To mine own death. I will not. I will take
32This opportunity to ‘scape. It may
33Be Heav’n reserves me to some better end.

Exit.

Enter [Languebeau] Snuffe and Soquette into the churchyard.

Soquette.

Nay, good sir, I dare not. In good sooth I come of a generation both by father and mother that were all as fruitful as costermonger’s wives.

Languebeau.

Tush, then a tympany is the greatest danger can be feared. Their fruitfulness turns but to a certain kind of phlegmatic windy disease.

Soquette.

I must put my understanding to your trust, sir. I would be loath to be deceived.

Languebeau.

No, conceive, thou sha’t not. Yet thou shalt profit by my instruction too. My body is not every day drawn dry, wench.

Soquette.

Yet methinks, sir, your want of use should rather make your body like a well: the lesser 'tis drawn, the sooner it grows dry.

Languebeau.

Thou shalt try that instantly.

Soquette.

But we want place and opportunity.

Languebeau.

We have both. This is the back side of the house which the superstitious call Saint Winifred's church, and is verily a convenient unfrequented place,

48Where under the close curtains of the night —

Soquette.

49You purpose i' the dark to make me light.

[Snuffe] pulles out a sheet, a hair, and a beard.

50But what ha' you there?

Languebeau.

This disguise is for security sake, wench. There's a talk, thou know’st, that the ghost of old Montferrers walks. In this church he was buried. Now if any stranger fall upon us before our business be ended, in this disguise I shall be taken for that ghost and never be called to examination, I warrant thee. Thus we shall ‘scape both prevention and discovery. How do I look in this habit, wench?

Soquette.

So like a ghost that, notwithstanding I have some fore- knowledge of you, you make my hair stand almost on end.

Languebeau.

I will try how I can kiss in this beard. —Oh fie, fie, fie. I will put it off, and then kiss, and then put it on. I can do the rest without kissing.

Enter Charlemont doubtfully, with his sword drawn. [He] is upon them before they are aware. They run out divers ways and leave the disguise.

Charlemont.

64What ha' we here? A sheet, a hair, a beard?
65What end was this disguise intended for?
66No matter what. I'll not expostulate
67The purpose of a friendly accident.
68Perhaps it may accommodate my ‘scape.
69I fear I am pursu’d. For more assurance,
70I'll hide me here i' th' charnel house,
71This convocation-house of dead men'sskulls.

To get into the charnel house he takes hold of a death's head; it slips and staggers him.

72Death's head, deceiv'st my hold?
73Such is the trust to all mortality.

Hides himself in the charnel house.

Enter D'Amville and Castabella.

Castabella.

74My lord, the night grows late. Your lordship spake
75Of something you desir’d to move in private.

D'Amville.

76Yes, now I'll speak it. Th' argument is love.
77The smallest ornament of thy sweet form,
78That abstract of all pleasure, can command
79The senses into passion, and thy entire
80Perfection is my object; yet I love
81Thee with the freedom of my reason. I
82Can give thee reason for my love.

Castabella.

Can give thee reason for my love. Love me,
83My lord? I do believe it, for I am
84The wife of him you love.

D'Amville.

The wife of him you love. 'Tis true. By my
85Persuasion thou wert forc’d to marry one
86Unable to perform the office of
87A husband. I was author of the wrong.
88My conscience suffers under't, and I would
89Disburden it by satisfaction.

Castabella.

Disburden it by satisfaction. How?

D'Amville.

90I will supply that pleasure to thee which
91He cannot.

Castabella.

He cannot. Are y' a devil or a man?

D'Amville.

92A man, and such a man as can return
93Thy entertainment with as prodigal
94A body as the covetous desire
95Of woman ever was delighted with;
96So that, besides the full performance of
97Thy empty husband's duty, thou shalt have
98The joy of children to continue the
99Succession of thy blood; for the appetite
100That steals her pleasure, draws the forces of
101The body to an united strength and puts
102'Em altogether into action,
103Never fails of procreation.
104All the purposes of man
105Aim but at one of these two ends, pleasure
106Or profit, and in this one sweet conjunction
107Of our loves they both will meet. Would it
108Not grieve thee, that a stranger to thy blood
109Should lay the first foundation of his house
110Upon the ruins of thy family?

Castabella.

111Now Heav’n defend me! May my memory
112Be utterly extinguish’d, and the heir
113Of him that was my father's enemy
114Raise his eternal monument upon
115Our ruins, ere the greatest pleasure or
116The greatest profit ever tempt me to
117Continue it by incest.

D'Amville.

Continue it by incest. Incest, tush.
118These distances affinity observes
119Are articles of bondage cast upon
120Our freedoms by our own subjections.
121Nature allows a gen’ral liberty
122Of generation to all creatures else.
123Shall man, to whose command and use all creatures
124Were made subject, be less free than they?

Castabella.

125O God,
126Is thy unlimited and infinite
127Omnipotence less free because thou dost
128No ill? Or if you argue merely out
129Of Nature, do you not degenerate
130From that, and are you not unworthy the
131Prerogative of Nature's masterpiece,
132When basely you prescribe yourself
133Authority and law from their examples
134Whom you should command? I could confute
135You, but the horror of the argument
136Confounds my understanding. — Sir, I know
137You do but try me in your son's behalf,
138Suspecting that my strength and youth of blood
139Cannot contain themselves with impotence.
140Believe me, sir,
141I never wrong’d him. If it be your lust,
142O quench it on their prostituted flesh,
143Whose trade of sin can please desire with more
144Delight and less offence. —The poison of
145Your breath, evaporated from so foul a soul,
146Infects the air more than the damps that rise
147From bodies but half rotten in their graves.

D'Amville.

148Kiss me. I warrant thee my breath is sweet.
149These dead men's bones lie here of purpose to
150Invite us to supply the number of
151The living. Come, we'll get young bones and do 't.
152I will enjoy thee. No? Nay then invoke
153Your great suppos’d protector. I will do't.

Castabella.

154Suppos’d protector? Are y' an atheist? Then
155I know my prayers and tears are spent in vain.
156O patient Heav’n, why dost thou not express
157Thy wrath in thunderbolts, to tear the frame
158Of man in pieces? How can earth endure
159The burden of this wickedness without
160An earthquake, or the angry face of Heav’n
161Be not inflam’d with lightning?

D'Amville.

Be not inflam’d with lightning? Conjure up
162The devil and his dam; cry to the graves;
163The dead can hear thee; invocate their help.

Castabella.

164O would this grave might open, and my body
165Were bound to the dead carcass of a man
166For ever, ere it entertain the lust
167Of this detested villain.

D'Amville.

Of this detested villain. Tereus-like,
168Thus I will force my passage to—

Charlemont.

Thus I will force my passage to— The devil!

Charlemont rises in the disguise and frights D'Amville away.

169Now Lady, with the hand of Charlemont
170I thus redeem you from the arm of lust.
171My Castabella!

Castabella.

My Castabella! My dear Charlemont!

Charlemont.

172For all my wrongs I thank thee, gracious Heav’n;
173Th’ hast made me satisfaction, to reserve
174Me for this blessed purpose. Now, sweet death,
175I'll bid thee welcome. Come, I'll guard thee home,
176And then I'll cast myself into the arms
177Of apprehension, that the law may make
178This worthy work the crown of all my actions,
179Being the best and last.

Castabella.

Being the best and last. The last? The law?
180Now Heav’n forbid, what ha' you done?

Charlemont.

Now Heav’n forbid, what ha' you done? Why, I have kill’d
181A man, not murder’d him, my Castabella;
182He would ha' murdered me.

Castabella.

He would ha' murdered me. Then, Charlemont,
183The hand of Heav’n directed thy defence.
184That wicked atheist, I suspect his plot.

Charlemont.

185My life he seeks. I would he had it, since
186He has depriv’d me of those blessings that
187Should make me love it. Come, I'll give it him.

Castabella.

188You sha' not. I will first expose myself
189To certain danger than for my defence
190Destroy the man that sav’d me from destruction.

Charlemont.

191Thou canst not satisfy me better than
192To be the instrument of my release
193From misery.

Castabella.

From misery. Then work it by escape.
194Leave me to this protection that still guards
195The innocent, or I will be a partner
196In your destiny.

Charlemont.

197My soul is heavy. Come, lie down to rest;
198These are the pillows whereon men sleep best.

They lie down with either of them a death's head for a pillow.

Enter [Languebeau] Snuffe seeking Soquette.

Languebeau.

Soquette, Soquette, Soquette! O art thou there? He mistakes the body of Borachio for Soquette. Verily thou liest in a fine premeditate readiness for the purpose. Come, kiss me, sweet Soquette. —Now purity defend me from the sin of Sodom! This is a creature of the masculine gender. —Verily the man is blasted. —Yea, cold and stiff! —Murder, murder, murder.

Exit.

D'Amville.

205Why dost thou stare upon me? Thou art not
206The skull of him I murder’d. What hast thou
207To do to vex my conscience? Sure thou wert
208The head of a most dogged usurer,
209Th’ art so uncharitable. And that bawd,
210The sky there, she could shut the windows and
211The doors of this great chamber of the world,
212And draw the curtains of the clouds between
213Those lights and me about this bed of earth,
214When that same strumpet, Murder, and myself
215Committed sin together. Then she could
216Leave us i’ the dark till the close deed
217Was done, but now that I begin to feel
218The loathsome horror of my sin and, like
219A lecher empty’d of his lust, desire
220To bury my face under my eyebrows and
221Would steal from my shame unseen, she meets me
222I’ the face with all her light corrupted eyes
223To challenge payment o’ me. O behold!
224Yonder's the ghost of old Montferrers in
225A long white sheet, climbing yond’ lofty mountain
226To complain to Heav’n of me. Montferrers!
227'Pox o’ fearfulness. ’Tis nothing but
228A fair white cloud. Why, was I born a coward?
229He lies that says so. Yet the count’nance of
230A bloodless worm might ha' the courage now
231To turn my blood to water. The trembling motion
232Of an aspen leaf, would make me, like
233The shadow of that leaf, lie shaking under't.
234I could now commit a murder, were
235It but to drink the fresh warm blood of him
236I murder’d, to supply the want and weakness
237O'mine own, 'Tis grown so cold and phlegmatic.

Languebeau.

238(Within.) Murder, murder, murder.

D'Amville.

239Mountains o’erwhelm me, the ghost of old Montferrers haunts me.

Languebeau.

240Murder, murder, murder.

D'Amville.

241O were my body circumvolv’d
242Within that cloud, that when the thunder tears
243His passage open, it might scatter me
244To nothing in the air!

Enter Languebeau Snuffe with the Watch.

Languebeau.

To nothing in the air! Here you shall find
245The murder’d body.

D'Amville.

The murder’d body. Black Beelzebub
246And all his hell-hounds come to apprehend me?

Languebeau.

247No my good lord, we come to apprehend
248The murderer.

D'Amville.

The murderer. The ghost, great Pluto, was
249A fool, unfit to be employ’d in any
250Serious business for the state of hell.
251Why could not he ha' suffer’d me to raise
252The mountain o’ my sins with one as damnable
253As all the rest, and then ha' tumbled me t
254To ruin? But apprehend me e'en between
255The purpose and the act, before it was
256Committed!

Watchman.

Is this the murderer? He speaks suspiciously.

Languebeau.

No verily. This is my Lord D'Amville, and his dis- traction, I think, grows out of his grief for the loss of a faithful servant, for surely I take him to be Borachio that is slain.

D'Amville.

Ha! Borachio slain? Thou look'st like Snuffe, dost not?

Languebeau.

Yes, in sincerity, my lord.

D'Amville.

Hark thee? —sawest thou not a ghost?

Languebeau.

I smell a fox.

D'Amville.

Here i' the churchyard.

Languebeau.

Tush, tush, their walking spirits are mere imaginary fables. There's no such thing in rerum natura . Here is a man slain, and with the spirit of consideration I rather think him to be the murderer got into that disguise than any such fantastic toy.

D'Amville.

My brains begin to put themselves in order. I appre- hend thee now. ’Tis e’en so. —Borachio!— I will search the centre, but I'll find the murderer.

Watchman.

Here, here, here.

D'Amville.

Stay. Asleep? So soundly? And so sweetly upon death's heads? And in a place so full of fear and horror? Sure there is some other happiness within the freedom of the conscience than my knowledge e'er attained to. — Ho, ho, ho!

Charlemont.

281Y' are welcome, uncle. Had you sooner come,
282You had been sooner welcome. I'm the man
283You seek. You sha' not need examine me.

D'Amville.

284My nephew! And my daughter! O my dear
285Lamented blood, what fate has cast you thus
286Unhappily upon this accident?

Charlemont.

287You know, sir, she's as clear as chastity.

D'Amville.

288As her own chastity. The time, the place,
289All circumstances argue that unclear.

Castabella.

290Sir, I confess it, and repentantly
291Will undergo the selfsame punishment
292That justice shall inflict on Charlemont.

D'Amville.

293Unjustly she betrays her innocence.

Watchman.

294But, sir, she's taken with you, and she must
295To prison with you.

D'Amville.

To prison with you. There's no remedy,
296Yet were it not my son's bed she abus’d,
297My land should fly but both should be excus’d.

[IV.iv]

Enter Belforest and a Servant.

Belforest.

1Is not my wife come in yet?

Servant.

Is not my wife come in yet? No, my lord.

Belforest.

2Methinks she's very affectedly inclin’d
3To young Sebastian's company o' late,
4But jealousy is such a torment that
5I am afraid to entertain it. Yet
6The more I shun by circumstance to meet
7Directly with it, the more ground I find
8To circumvent my apprehension. First
9I know sh' has a perpetual appetite,
10Which being so oft encounter’d with a man
11Of such a bold luxurious freedom as
12Sebastian is; and of so promising
13A body, her own blood, corrupted, will
14Betray her to temptation.

Enter Fresco closely.

Fresco.

'Precious! I was sent by his lady to see if her lord were in bed. I should ha' done 't slyly without dis- covery, and now I am blurted upon 'em before I was aware.

Exit.

Belforest.

Know not you the gentlewoman my wife brought home?

Servant.

By sight, my lord. Her man was here but now.

Belforest.

Her man? I prithee run and call him quickly. [Exit servant] This villain, I suspect him ever since I found him hid behind the tapestry. [Enter Fresco with Servant] Fresco! Th’ art welcome, Fresco. Leave us. [Exit Servant] Dost hear, Fresco? Is not my wife at thy mistress’s?

Fresco.

I know not, my lord.

Belforest.

I prithee tell me, Fresco, we are private, tell me. Is not thy mistress a good wench?

Fresco.

How means your lordship that? A wench o' the trade?

Belforest.

Yes faith, Fresco, e'en a wench o' the trade.

Fresco.

O no, my lord. Those falling diseases cause baldness, and my mistress recovers the loss of hair, for she is a peri- wig maker.

Belforest.

And nothing else?

Fresco.

Sells falls and tires and bodies for ladies, or so.

Belforest.

So, sir, and she helps my lady to falls and bodies now and then, does she not?

Fresco.

At her ladyship's pleasure, my lord.

Belforest.

Her pleasure, you rogue? You are the pander to her pleasure, you varlet, are you not? You know the conveyances between Sebastian and my wife. Tell me the truth, or by this hand I'll nail thy bosom to the earth. Stir not, you dog, but quickly tell the truth.

O yes!

O yes!

32Commit with many.

Commit with many. So it seems to me.—

Why, how now, Sebastian, making love to that gentle- woman? How many mistresses ha' you i' faith?

Sebastian.

In faith, none, for I think none of 'em are faithful, but otherwise, as many as clean shirts. The love of a woman is like a mushroom; it grows in one night and will serve somewhat pleasingly next morning to breakfast, but afterwards waxes fulsome and unwholesome.

Cataplasma.

Nay, by Saint Winifred, a woman's love lasts as long as winter fruit.

Sebastian.

'Tis true. —till new come in, by my experience no longer.

Pay dear? For what?

Wil 't not be a chargeable reckoning, think you, when here are half a dozen fellows coming to call us to account, with ev’ry man a several bill in his hand that we are not able to discharge.

Levidulcia.

59O God! My husband! My Sebastian! Husband!
60Neither can speak; yet both report my shame.
61Is this the saving of my honour, when
62Their blood runs out in rivers, and my lust
63The fountain whence it flows? Dear husband, let
64Not thy departed spirit be displeas’d
65If with adult’rate lips I kiss thy cheek.
66Here I behold the hatefulness of lust,
67Which brings me kneeling to embrace him dead,
68Whose body living I did loathe to touch.
69Now I can weep. But what can tears do good?
70When I weep only water, they weep blood.
71But could I make an ocean with my tears,
72That on the flood this broken vessel of
73My body, laden heavy with light lust,
74Might suffer shipwreck, and so drown my shame,
75Then weeping were to purpose; but alas,
76The Sea wants water enough to wash away
77The foulness of my name. O, in their wounds
78I feel my honour wounded to the death.
79Shall I outlive my honour? Must my life
80Be made the world's example? Since it must,
81Then thus in detestation of my deed,
82To make th' example move more forcibly
83To virtue, thus I seal it with a death
84As full of horror as my life of sin.

85Hold, madam! Lord, what a strange night is this!

86May not Snuffe be suffered to go out of himself?

87Nor you, nor any. All must go with us.
88Oh with what virtue lust should be withstood,
89Since 'tis a fire quench’d seldom without blood.
