2.1

Enter Montano, [Governor of Cyprus, with] two [other] Gentlemen.

Montano

611What from the cape can you discern at sea?

1 Gentleman

612Nothing at all; it is a high-wrought flood.
613I cannot ’twixt the heaven and the main
614Descry a sail.

Montano

615Methinks the wind hath spoke aloud at land.
616A fuller blast ne’er shook our battlements;
617If it hath ruffianed so upon the sea,
618What ribs of oak, when mountains melt on them,
619Can hold the mortise? What shall we hear of this?

2 Gentleman

620A segregation of the Turkish fleet—
621For do but stand upon the foaming shore,
622The chiding billow seems to pelt the clouds,
623The wind-shaked surge with high and monstrous mane
624Seems to cast water on the burning Bear
625And quench the guards of th’ever-fixèd Pole.
626I never did like molestation view
627On the enchafèd flood.

Montano

On the enchafèd flood. If that the Turkish fleet
628Be not ensheltered and embayed, they are drowned;
629It is impossible to bear it out.

Enter a [third] Gentleman.

3 Gentleman

630News, lads! Our wars are done.
631The desperate tempest hath so banged the Turks
632That their designment halts. A noble ship of Venice
633Hath seen a grievous wreck and sufferance
634On most part of their fleet.

Montano

On most part of their fleet. How? Is this true?

3 Gentleman

635The ship is here put in,
636A Veronnesa. Michael Cassio,
637Lieutenant to the warlike Moor, Othello,
638Is come on shore; the Moor himself at sea,
639And is in full commission here for Cyprus.

Montano

640I am glad on’t; ’tis a worthy governor.

3 Gentleman

641But this same Cassio, though he speak of comfort
642Touching the Turkish loss, yet he looks sadly
643And prays the Moor be safe, for they were parted
644With foul and violent tempest.

Montano

With foul and violent tempest. Pray heavens he be,
645For I have served him, and the man commands
646Like a full soldier. Let’s to the seaside, ho!
647As well to see the vessel that’s come in
648As to throw out our eyes for brave Othello,
649Even till we make the main and th’aerial blue
650An indistinct regard.

3 Gentleman

An indistinct regard. Come, let’s do so;
651For every minute is expectancy
652Of more arrivancy.

Enter Cassio.

Cassio

653Thanks, you the valiant of this warlike isle
654That so approve the Moor. Oh, let the heavens
655Give him defense against the elements,
656For I have lost him on a dangerous sea.

Montano

657Is he well shipped?

Cassio

658His bark is stoutly timbered, and his pilot
659Of very expert and approved allowance;
660Therefore my hopes, not surfeited to death,
661Stand in bold cure.

Voices Within

Stand in bold cure. A sail! A sail! A sail!

Cassio

Stand in bold cure. A sail! A sail! A sail! What noise?

1 Gentleman

662The town is empty; on the brow o’th’sea
663Stand ranks of people, and they cry “A sail”!

Cassio

664My hopes do shape him for the governor.

[A shot]

2 Gentleman

665They do discharge their shot of courtesy;
666Our friends at least.

Cassio

Our friends at least. I pray you sir, go forth
667And give us truth who ’tis that is arrived.

2 Gentleman

668I shall.

Exit [2 Gentleman].

Montano

669But good lieutenant, is your general wived?

Cassio

670Most fortunately; he hath achieved a maid
671That paragons description and wild fame,
672One that excels the quirks of blazoning pens
673And in th’essential vesture of creation
674Does tire the ingener.

Enter [2] Gentleman.

Does tire the ingener. How now? Who has put in?

2 Gentleman

675’Tis one Iago, ancient to the general.

Cassio

676He’s had most favorable and happy speed.
677Tempests themselves, high seas and howling winds,
678The guttered rocks and congregated sands,
679Traitors ensteeped to enclog the guiltless keel,
680As having sense of beauty, do omit
681Their mortal natures, letting go safely by
682The divine Desdemona.

Montano

The divine Desdemona. What is she?

Cassio

683She that I spake of, our great captain’s captain,
684Left in the conduct of the bold Iago,
685Whose footing here anticipates our thoughts
686A sennight’s speed. Great Jove, Othello guard,
687And swell his sail with thine own powerful breath
688That he may bless this bay with his tall ship,
689Make love’s quick pants in Desdemona’s arms,
690Give renewed fire to our extincted spirits
691And bring all Cyprus comfort—

(Enter Desdemona, Iago, Roderigo, and Emilia.)

And bring all Cyprus comfort— Oh, behold,
692The riches of the ship is come on shore!
693You men of Cyprus, let her have your knees.
694Hail to thee, lady, and the grace of heaven,
695Before, behind thee, and on every hand
696Enwheel thee round.

Desdemona

Enwheel thee round. I thank you, valiant Cassio.
697What tidings can you tell me of my lord?

Cassio

698He is not yet arrived, nor know I aught
699But that he’s well and will be shortly here.

Desdemona

700Oh, but I fear—how lost you company?

Cassio

701The great contention of the sea and skies
702Parted our fellowship.

Voices Within

Parted our fellowship. A sail! A sail!

Cassio

Parted our fellowship. A sail! A sail! But hark, a sail.

[A shot]

2 Gentleman

703They give this greeting to the citadel;
704This likewise is a friend.

Cassio

This likewise is a friend. See for the news.
705Good ancient, you are welcome. Welcome, mistress.

[He kisses Emilia.]

706Let it not gall your patience, good Iago,
707That I extend my manners. ’Tis my breeding
708That gives me this bold show of courtesy.

Iago

709Sir, would she give you so much of her lips
710As of her tongue she oft bestows on me,
711You’d have enough.

Desdemona

You’d have enough. Alas, she has no speech!

Iago

712In faith, too much;
713I find it still when I have leave to sleep.
714Marry, before your ladyship, I grant,
715She puts her tongue a little in her heart
716And chides with thinking.

Emilia

717You have little cause to say so.

Iago

Come on, come on! You are pictures out of doors, bells in your parlors, wildcats in your kitchens, saints in your injuries, devils being offended, players in your housewifery, and housewives in your beds.

Desdemona

719Oh, fie upon thee, slanderer!

Iago

720Nay, it is true, or else I am a Turk.
721You rise to play and go to bed to work.

Emilia

722You shall not write my praise.

Iago

You shall not write my praise. No, let me not.

Desdemona

723What wouldst write of me, if thou shouldst praise me?

Iago

724Oh, gentle lady, do not put me to’t,
725For I am nothing if not critical.

Desdemona

726Come on, assay—there’s one gone to the harbor?

Iago

727Ay, madam.

Desdemona

728I am not merry, but I do beguile
729The thing I am by seeming otherwise.
730Come, how wouldst thou praise me?

Iago

I am about it, but indeed my invention comes from my pate as birdlime does from frieze; it plucks out brains and all. But my muse labors, and thus she is delivered:

732If she be fair and wise, fairness and wit;
733The one’s for use, the other useth it.

Desdemona

734Well praised! How if she be black and witty?

Iago

735If she be black and thereto have a wit,
736She’ll find a white that shall her blackness hit.

Desdemona

737Worse and worse.

Emilia

Worse and worse. How if fair and foolish?

Iago

738She never yet was foolish that was fair,
739For even her folly helped her to an heir.

Desdemona

These are old fond paradoxes to make fools laugh i’th’alehouse. What miserable praise hast thou for her that’s foul and foolish?

Iago

741There’s none so foul and foolish thereunto,
742But does foul pranks which fair and wise ones do.

Desdemona

O heavy ignorance! Thou praisest the worst best. But what praise couldst thou bestow on a deserving woman indeed? One that in the authority of her merit did justly put on the vouch of very malice itself.

Iago

744She that was ever fair and never proud,
745Had tongue at will, and yet was never loud,
746Never lacked gold, and yet went never gay,
747Fled from her wish, and yet said “now I may”.
748She that being angered, her revenge being nigh,
749Bade her wrong stay and her displeasure fly.
750She that in wisdom never was so frail
751To change the cod’s head for the salmon’s tail.
752She that could think, and ne’er disclose her mind,
753See suitors following, and not look behind.
754She was a wight, if ever such wights were—

Desdemona

To do what?

Iago

756To suckle fools and chronicle small beer.

Desdemona

O most lame and impotent conclusion! Do not learn of him Emilia, though he be thy husband. How say you, Cassio, is he not a most profane and liberal counselor?

Cassio

He speaks home, madam. You may relish him more in the soldier than in the scholar.

[Cassio takes Desdemona by the hand.]

Iago

He takes her by the palm. Ay, well said, whisper! With as little a web as this will I ensnare as great a fly as Cassio. Ay, smile upon her, do! I will gyve thee in thine own courtship. You say true; ’tis so indeed. If such tricks as these strip you out of your lieutenantry, it had been better you had not kissed your three fingers so oft, which now again you are most apt to play the sir in. Very good! Well kissed and excellent courtesy! ’Tis so indeed. Yet again, your fingers to your lips? Would they were clysterpipes for your sake.

Cassio

761’Tis truly so.

Desdemona

762Let’s meet him and receive him.

Cassio

763Lo, where he comes.

Enter Othello and attendants.

Othello

764O my fair warrior!

Desdemona

O my fair warrior! My dear Othello.

Othello

765It gives me wonder great as my content
766To see you here before me. O my soul’s joy!
767If after every tempest come such calms,
768May the winds blow till they have wakened death,
769And let the laboring bark climb hills of seas
770Olympus-high and duck again as low
771As hell’s from heaven. If it were now to die,
772’Twere now to be most happy, for I fear
773My soul hath her content so absolute
774That not another comfort like to this
775Succeeds in unknown fate.

Desdemona

Succeeds in unknown fate. The heavens forbid
776But that our loves and comforts should increase
777Even as our days do grow.

Othello

Even as our days do grow. Amen to that, sweet powers.
778I cannot speak enough of this content;
779It stops me here; it is too much of joy.
780And this, and this—

[They kiss.]

And this, and this— —the greatest discords be
781That ere our hearts shall make.

Iago

Oh, you are well tuned now! But I’ll set down the pegs that make this music, as honest as I am.

Othello

783Come, let us to the castle.
784News, friends: our wars are done; the Turks are drowned.
785How does my old acquaintance of this isle?
786Honey, you shall be well desired in Cyprus;
787I have found great love amongst them. O my sweet,
788I prattle out of fashion and I dote
789In mine own comforts. I prithee, good Iago,
790Go to the bay and disembark my coffers.
791Bring thou the master to the citadel;
792He is a good one, and his worthiness
793Does challenge much respect.—Come, Desdemona,
794Once more well met at Cyprus.

Exeunt [all but Iago and Roderigo].

Iago

Do thou meet me presently at the harbor. Come thither, if thou be’st valiant—as they say base men being in love have then a nobility in their natures more than is native to them—list me: the lieutenant tonight watches on the court of guard. First, I must tell thee this: Desdemona is directly in love with him.

Roderigo

With him? Why, ’tis not possible.

Iago

Lay thy finger thus, and let thy soul be instructed. Mark me with what violence she first loved the Moor, but for bragging and telling her fantastical lies. To love him still for prating? Let not thy discreet heart think it. Her eye must be fed. And what delight shall she have to look on the devil? When the blood is made dull with the act of sport, there should be a game to enflame it, and, to give satiety a fresh appetite, loveliness in favor, sympathy in years, manners, and beauties—all which the Moor is defective in. Now, for want of these required conveniences her delicate tenderness will find itself abused, begin to heave the gorge, disrelish and abhor the Moor. Very nature will instruct her in it and compel her to some second choice. Now sir, this granted (as it is a most pregnant and unforced position) who stands so eminent in the degree of this fortune as Cassio does—a knave very voluble, no further conscionable than in putting on the mere form of civil and humane seeming for the better compass of his salt and most hidden loose affection? Why none, why none! A slipper and subtle knave, a finder of occasion that has an eye can stamp and counterfeit advantages though true advantage never present itself. A devilish knave! Besides, the knave is handsome, young, and hath all those requisites in him that folly and green minds look after—a pestilent complete knave, and the woman hath found him already.

Roderigo

I cannot believe that in her; she’s full of most blessed condition.

Iago

Blessed fig’s-end! The wine she drinks is made of grapes. If she had been blessed, she would never have loved the Moor. Blessed pudding! Didst thou not see her paddle with the palm of his hand? Didst not mark that?

Roderigo

Yes, that I did; but that was but courtesy.

Iago

Lechery, by this hand—an index and obscure prologue to the history of lust and foul thoughts. They met so near with their lips that their breaths embraced together. Villainous thoughts, Roderigo, when these mutualities so marshal the way, hard at hand comes the master and main exercise, th’incorporate conclusion—pish! But sir, be you ruled by me. I have brought you from Venice. Watch you tonight; for the command, I’ll lay’t upon you. Cassio knows you not; I’ll not be far from you. Do you find some occasion to anger Cassio, either by speaking too loud, or tainting his discipline, or from what other course you please which the time shall more favorably minister.

Roderigo

Well.

Iago

Sir, he’s rash and very sudden in choler, and haply may strike at you. Provoke him that he may, for even out of that will I cause these of Cyprus to mutiny, whose qualification shall come into no true taste again but by the displanting of Cassio. So shall you have a shorter journey to your desires by the means I shall then have to prefer them, and the impediment most profitably removed without the which there were no expectation of our prosperity.

Roderigo

I will do this, if you can bring it to any opportunity.

Iago

I warrant thee. Meet me by and by at the citadel. I must fetch his necessaries ashore. Farewell.

Roderigo

Adieu.

Exit [Roderigo].

Iago

807That Cassio loves her, I do well believ’t;
808That she loves him, ’tis apt and of great credit.
809The Moor, howbeit that I endure him not,
810Is of a constant, loving, noble nature,
811And I dare think he’ll prove to Desdemona
812A most dear husband. Now I do love her too,
813Not out of absolute lust (though peradventure
814I stand accountant for as great a sin),
815But partly led to diet my revenge,
816For that I do suspect the lusty Moor
817Hath leaped into my seat—the thought whereof
818Doth like a poisonous mineral gnaw my inwards,
819And nothing can or shall content my soul
820Till I am evened with him, wife for wife;
821Or failing so, yet that I put the Moor
822At least into a jealousy so strong
823That judgment cannot cure; which thing to do,
824If this poor trash of Venice, whom I trace
825For his quick hunting, stand the putting on,
826I’ll have our Michael Cassio on the hip,
827Abuse him to the Moor in the right garb
828(For I fear Cassio with my nightcap too),
829Make the Moor thank me, love me, and reward me
830For making him egregiously an ass
831And practicing upon his peace and quiet
832Even to madness. ’Tis here, but yet confused;
833Knavery’s plain face is never seen till used.

Exit [Iago].

Herald

[Reading] It is Othello’s pleasure, our noble and valiant general, that upon certain tidings now arrived importing the mere perdition of the Turkish fleet, every man put himself into triumph: some to dance, some to make bonfires, each man to what sport and revels his addition leads him. Iago

Cassio

993And to defend ourselves it be a sin
994When violence assails us.

Othello

When violence assails us. Now, by heaven,
995My blood begins my safer guides to rule,
996And passion, having my best judgment collied,
997Assays to lead the way. Zounds, if I stir,
998Or do but lift this arm, the best of you
999Shall sink in my rebuke. Give me to know
1000How this foul rout began, who set it on,
1001And he that is approved in this offense,
1002Though he had twinned with me both at a birth,
1003Shall lose me. What, in a town of war
1004Yet wild, the people’s hearts brimful of fear,
1005To manage private and domestic quarrel
1006In night and on the court and guard of safety?
1007’Tis monstrous! Iago, who began’t?

Montano

1008If, partially affined or leagued in office,
1009Thou dost deliver more or less than truth,
1010Thou art no soldier.

Iago

Thou art no soldier. Touch me not so near.
1011I had rather have this tongue cut from my mouth
1012Than it should do offense to Michael Cassio;
1013Yet I persuade myself to speak the truth
1014Shall nothing wrong him. This it is, general:
1015Montano and myself being in speech,
1016There comes a fellow crying out for help,
1017And Cassio following with determined sword
1018To execute upon him. Sir, this gentleman
1019Steps in to Cassio and entreats his pause;
1020Myself the crying fellow did pursue,
1021Lest by his clamor, as it so fell out,
1022The town might fall in fright. He, swift of foot,
1023Outran my purpose, and I returned then, rather
1024For that I heard the clink and fall of swords,
1025And Cassio high in oath, which till tonight
1026I ne’er might say before. When I came back—
1027For this was brief—I found them close together
1028At blow and thrust, even as again they were
1029When you yourself did part them.
1030More of this matter cannot I report,
1031But men are men; the best sometimes forget.
1032Though Cassio did some little wrong to him,
1033As men in rage strike those that wish them best,
1034Yet surely Cassio, I believe, received
1035From him that fled some strange indignity
1036Which patience could not pass.

Othello

Which patience could not pass. I know, Iago,
1037Thy honesty and love doth mince this matter,
1038Making it light to Cassio.—Cassio, I love thee,
1039But never more be officer of mine.

(Enter Desdemona, attended.)

1040Look if my gentle love be not raised up.
1041I’ll make thee an example.

Desdemona

1042What is the matter, dear?

Othello

What is the matter, dear? All’s well, sweeting.
1043Come away to bed.
Come away to bed. [To Montano] Sir, for your hurts
1044Myself will be your surgeon. Lead him off.

[Montano is led off.]

1045Iago, look with care about the town,
1046And silence those whom this vile brawl distracted.
1047Come, Desdemona; ’tis the soldier’s life
1048To have their balmy slumbers waked with strife.

Exeunt [Othello, Desdemona, and attendants.]

Iago

What, are you hurt, lieutenant?

Cassio

Ay, past all surgery.

Iago

Marry, God forbid.

Cassio

Reputation, reputation, reputation! Oh, I have lost my reputation! I have lost the immortal part of myself, and what remains is bestial. My reputation, Iago, my reputation.

Iago

As I am an honest man, I had thought you had received some bodily wound; there is more sense in that than in reputation. Reputation is an idle and most false imposition, oft got without merit and lost without deserving. You have lost no reputation at all unless you repute yourself such a loser. What, man, there are more ways to recover the general again. You are but now cast in his mood, a punishment more in policy than in malice—even so as one would beat his offenseless dog to affright an imperious lion. Sue to him again and he’s yours.

Cassio

I will rather sue to be despised than to deceive so good a commander with so slight, so drunken, and so indiscreet an officer. Drunk? and speak parrot? and squabble? swagger? swear? and discourse fustian with one’s own shadow? O thou invisible spirit of wine, if thou hast no name to be known by, let us call thee devil!

Iago

What was he that you followed with your sword? What had he done to you?

Cassio

I know not.

Iago

Is’t possible?

Cassio

I remember a mass of things, but nothing distinctly; a quarrel, but nothing wherefore. O God, that men should put an enemy in their mouths to steal away their brains! that we should with joy, pleasance, revel and applause transform ourselves into beasts!

Iago

Why, but you are now well enough. How came you thus recovered?

Cassio

It hath pleased the devil drunkenness to give place to the devil wrath; one unperfectness shows me another to make me frankly despise myself.

Iago

Come, you are too severe a moraler. As the time, the place, and the condition of this country stands, I could heartily wish this had not befallen; but since it is as it is, mend it for your own good.

Cassio

I will ask him for my place again; he shall tell me I am a drunkard. Had I as many mouths as Hydra, such an answer would stop them all. To be now a sensible man, by and by a fool, and presently a beast—Oh, strange! Every inordinate cup is unblessed, and the ingredient is a devil.

Iago

Come, come, good wine is a good familiar creature if it be well used; exclaim no more against it. And, good lieutenant, I think you think I love you.

Cassio

I have well approved it, sir—I drunk?

Iago

You, or any man living, may be drunk at a time, man. I tell you what you shall do: our general’s wife is now the general. I may say so in this respect, for that he hath devoted and given up himself to the contemplation, mark, and devotement of her parts and graces. Confess yourself freely to her; importune her help to put you in your place again. She is of so free, so kind, so apt, so blessed a disposition she holds it a vice in her goodness not to do more than she is requested. This broken joint between you and her husband entreat her to splinter, and, my fortunes against any lay worth naming, this crack of your love shall grow stronger than it was before.

Cassio

You advise me well.

Iago

I protest in the sincerity of love and honest kindness.

Cassio

I think it freely; and betimes in the morning I will beseech the virtuous Desdemona to undertake for me. I am desperate of my fortunes if they check me.

Iago

You are in the right. Goodnight, lieutenant. I must to the watch.

Cassio

Goodnight, honest Iago.

Exit Cassio.

Iago

1071And what’s he, then, that says I play the villain,
1072When this advice is free I give, and honest,
1073Probal to thinking, and, indeed, the course
1074To win the Moor again? For ’tis most easy
1075Th’inclining Desdemona to subdue
1076In any honest suit. She’s framed as fruitful
1077As the free elements. And then for her
1078To win the Moor were to renounce his baptism,
1079All seals and symbols of redeemèd sin;
1080His soul is so enfettered to her love
1081That she may make, unmake, do what she list
1082Even as her appetite shall play the god
1083With his weak function. How am I then a villain
1084To counsel Cassio to this parallel course
1085Directly to his good? Divinity of hell!
1086When devils will the blackest sins put on,
1087They do suggest at first with heavenly shows,
1088As I do now. For whiles this honest fool
1089Plies Desdemona to repair his fortune,
1090And she for him pleads strongly to the Moor,
1091I’ll pour this pestilence into his ear:
1092That she repeals him for her body’s lust;
1093And by how much she strives to do him good,
1094She shall undo her credit with the Moor.
1095So will I turn her virtue into pitch,
1096And out of her own goodness make the net
1097That shall enmesh them all.

Enter Roderigo.

That shall enmesh them all. How now, Roderigo?

Roderigo

I do follow here in the chase, not like a hound that hunts, but one that fills up the cry. My money is almost spent, I have been tonight exceedingly well cudgeled, and I think the issue will be I shall have so much experience for my pains, and so, with no money at all and a little more wit, return again to Venice.

Iago

1099How poor are they that have not patience?
1100What wound did ever heal but by degrees?
1101Thou know’st we work by wit and not by witchcraft,
1102And wit depends on dilatory time.
1103Does’t not go well? Cassio hath beaten thee,
1104And thou by that small hurt hath cashiered Cassio.
1105Though other things grow fair against the sun,
1106Yet fruits that blossom first will first be ripe.
1107Content thyself awhile. By the mass, ’tis morning;
1108Pleasure and action make the hours seem short.
1109Retire thee; go where thou art billeted.
1110Away, I say; thou shalt know more hereafter.
1111Nay, get thee gone.

(Exit Roderigo.)

Nay, get thee gone. Two things are to be done:
1112My wife must move for Cassio to her mistress;
1113I’ll set her on.
1114Myself awhile to draw the Moor apart
1115And bring him jump when he may Cassio find
1116Soliciting his wife. Ay, that’s the way;
1117Dull not device by coldness and delay.

Exit [Iago].
