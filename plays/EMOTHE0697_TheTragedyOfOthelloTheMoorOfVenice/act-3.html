3.1

Enter Cassio, Musicians, and Clown.

Cassio

Masters, play here—I will content your pains—something that’s brief, and bid “Good morrow, general”.

[The musicians play.]

Clown

Why, masters, have your instruments been in Naples that they speak i’th’nose thus?

Musician

How, sir? How?

Clown

Are these, I pray you, wind instruments?

Musician

Ay, marry, are they, sir.

Clown

Oh, thereby hangs a tale.

Musician

Whereby hangs a tale, sir?

Clown

Marry, sir, by many a wind instrument that I know. But, masters—here’s money for you—and the general so likes your music that he desires you for love’s sake to make no more noise with it.

Musician

Well, sir, we will not.

Clown

If you have any music that may not be heard, to’t again. But, as they say, to hear music the general does not greatly care.

Musician

We have none such, sir.

Clown

Then put up your pipes in your bag, for I’ll away. Go, vanish into air, away.

Exit Musicians.

Cassio

Dost thou hear, mine honest friend?

Clown

No, I hear not your honest friend; I hear you.

Cassio

Prithee keep up thy quillets. There’s a poor piece of gold for thee. If the gentlewoman that attends the general’s wife be stirring, tell her there’s one Cassio entreats her a little favor of speech. Wilt thou do this?

Clown

She is stirring, sir. If she will stir hither, I shall seem to notify unto her.

Cassio

1136Do, my good friend.

Exit Clown.

Enter Iago.

Cassio

Do, my good friend. In happy time, Iago.

Iago

1137You have not been abed then?

Cassio

1138Why, no; the day had broke before we parted.
1139I have made bold, Iago, to send in to your wife.
1140My suit to her is that she will to virtuous Desdemona
1141Procure me some access.

Iago

Procure me some access. I’ll send her to you presently,
1142And I’ll devise a mean to draw the Moor
1143Out of the way, that your converse and business
1144May be more free.

Exit [Iago].

Cassio

May be more free. I humbly thank you for’t.
1145I never knew a Florentine more kind and honest.

Enter Emilia.

Emilia

1146Good morrow, good lieutenant. I am sorry
1147For your displeasure, but all will sure be well.
1148The general and his wife are talking of it,
1149And she speaks for you stoutly. The Moor replies
1150That he you hurt is of great fame in Cyprus
1151And great affinity, and that in wholesome wisdom
1152He might not but refuse you. But he protests he loves you
1153And needs no other suitor but his likings
1154To take the safest occasion by the front
1155To bring you in again.

Cassio

To bring you in again. Yet I beseech you,
1156If you think fit, or that it may be done,
1157Give me advantage of some brief discourse
1158With Desdemon alone.

Emilia

With Desdemon alone. Pray you come in.
1159I will bestow you where you shall have time
1160To speak your bosom freely.

Cassio

To speak your bosom freely. I am much bound to you.

[Exeunt.]

3.3

Enter Desdemona, Cassio, and Emilia.

Desdemona

1167Be thou assured, good Cassio, I will do
1168All my abilities in thy behalf.

Emilia

1169Good madam, do. I warrant it grieves my husband
1170As if the cause were his.

Desdemona

1171Oh, that’s an honest fellow. Do not doubt, Cassio,
1172But I will have my lord and you again
1173As friendly as you were.

Cassio

As friendly as you were. Bounteous madam,
1174Whatever shall become of Michael Cassio,
1175He’s never anything but your true servant.

Desdemona

1176I know’t. I thank you. You do love my lord;
1177You have known him long, and be you well assured
1178He shall in strangeness stand no farther off
1179Than in a politic distance.

Cassio

Than in a politic distance. Ay, but, lady,
1180That policy may either last so long,
1181Or feed upon such nice and waterish diet,
1182Or breed itself so out of circumstances
1183That I being absent and my place supplied,
1184My general will forget my love and service.

Desdemona

1185Do not doubt that. Before Emilia here,
1186I give thee warrant of thy place. Assure thee,
1187If I do vow a friendship, I’ll perform it
1188To the last article. My lord shall never rest:
1189I’ll watch him tame and talk him out of patience;
1190His bed shall seem a school, his board a shrift;
1191I’ll intermingle everything he does
1192With Cassio’s suit. Therefore be merry, Cassio,
1193For thy solicitor shall rather die
1194Than give thy cause away.

Enter Othello and Iago.

Emilia

Than give thy cause away. Madam, here comes my lord.

Cassio

1195Madam, I’ll take my leave.

Desdemona

Madam, I’ll take my leave. Why stay, and hear me speak.

Cassio

1196Madam, not now. I am very ill at ease,
1197Unfit for mine own purposes.

Desdemona

1198Well, do your discretion.

Exit Cassio.

Iago

1199Ha? I like not that.

Othello

Ha? I like not that. What dost thou say?

Iago

1200Nothing, my lord; or if—I know not what.

Othello

1201Was not that Cassio parted from my wife?

Iago

1202Cassio, my lord? No sure, I cannot think it
1203That he would steal away so guilty-like,
1204Seeing your coming.

Othello

Seeing your coming. I do believe ’twas he.

Desdemona

1205How now, my lord?
1206I have been talking with a suitor here,
1207A man that languishes in your displeasure.

Othello

1208Who is’t you mean?

Desdemona

1209Why, your lieutenant, Cassio. Good my lord,
1210If I have any grace or power to move you,
1211His present reconciliation take;
1212For if he be not one that truly loves you,
1213That errs in ignorance and not in cunning,
1214I have no judgment in an honest face.
1215I prithee call him back.

Othello

1216Went he hence now?

Desdemona

Went he hence now? Aye, sooth, so humbled
1217That he hath left part of his grief with me
1218To suffer with him. Good love, call him back.

Othello

1219Not now, sweet Desdemon—some other time.

Desdemona

1220But shall’t be shortly?

Othello

But shall’t be shortly? The sooner, sweet, for you.

Desdemona

1221Shall’t be tonight, at supper?

Othello

Shall’t be tonight, at supper? No, not tonight.

Desdemona

1222Tomorrow dinner, then?

Othello

Tomorrow dinner, then? I shall not dine at home;
1223I meet the captains at the citadel.

Desdemona

1224Why then, tomorrow night, or Tuesday morn,
1225On Tuesday noon or night, or Wednesday morn.
1226I prithee name the time, but let it not
1227Exceed three days. In faith, he’s penitent;
1228And yet his trespass, in our common reason—
1229Save that they say the wars must make example
1230Out of her best—is not almost a fault
1231T’incur a private check. When shall he come?
1232Tell me, Othello. I wonder in my soul
1233What you would ask me that I should deny
1234Or stand so mamm’ring on? What? Michael Cassio,
1235That came a-wooing with you, and so many a time
1236When I have spoke of you dispraisingly
1237Hath ta’en your part—to have so much to do
1238To bring him in? By’r Lady, I could do much—

Othello

1239Prithee, no more. Let him come when he will;
1240I will deny thee nothing.

Desdemona

I will deny thee nothing. Why, this is not a boon;
1241’Tis as I should entreat you wear your gloves,
1242Or feed on nourishing dishes, or keep you warm,
1243Or sue to you to do a peculiar profit
1244To your own person. Nay, when I have a suit
1245Wherein I mean to touch your love indeed,
1246It shall be full of poise and difficult weight,
1247And fearful to be granted.

Othello

And fearful to be granted. I will deny thee nothing.
1248Whereon, I do beseech thee, grant me this:
1249To leave me but a little to myself.

Desdemona

1250Shall I deny you? No! Farewell, my lord.

Othello

1251Farewell, my Desdemona. I’ll come to thee straight.

Desdemona

1252Emilia, come.
Emilia, come. [To Othello] Be as your fancies teach you.
1253Whate’er you be, I am obedient.

Exeunt [Desdemona and Emilia].

Othello

1254Excellent wretch! Perdition catch my soul
1255But I do love thee! And when I love thee not,
1256Chaos is come again.

Iago

1257My noble lord—

Othello

My noble lord— What dost thou say, Iago?

Iago

1258Did Michael Cassio, when you wooed my lady,
1259Know of your love?

Othello

1260Why dost thou ask?

Iago

1261But for a satisfaction of my thought,
1262No further harm.

Othello

No further harm. Why of thy thought, Iago?

Iago

1263I did not think he had been acquainted with her.

Othello

1264Oh yes, and went between us very oft.

Iago

1265Indeed?

Othello

1266Indeed? Ay, indeed. Discern’st thou aught in that?
1267Is he not honest?

Iago

1268Honest, my lord?

Othello

1269Honest? Ay, honest.

Iago

1270My lord, for aught I know.

Othello

1271What dost thou think?

Iago

1272Think, my lord?

Othello

1273“Think, my lord”? By heaven, thou echo’st me
1274As if there were some monster in thy thought
1275Too hideous to be shown. Thou dost mean something.
1276I heard thee say even now, thou lik’st not that
1277When Cassio left my wife. What didst not like?
1278And when I told thee he was of my counsel
1279Of my whole course of wooing, thou cried’st “Indeed”?
1280And didst contract and purse thy brow together
1281As if thou then hadst shut up in thy brain
1282Some horrible conceit. If thou dost love me,
1283Show me thy thought.

Iago

1284My lord, you know I love you.

Othello

My lord, you know I love you. I think thou dost;
1285And for I know thou’rt full of love and honesty,
1286And weigh’st thy words before thou giv’st them breath,
1287Therefore these stops of thine fright me the more.
1288For such things in a false disloyal knave
1289Are tricks of custom, but in a man that’s just,
1290They’re close dilations, working from the heart,
1291That passion cannot rule.

Iago

That passion cannot rule. For Michael Cassio,
1292I dare be sworn, I think that he is honest.

Othello

1293I think so too.

Iago

I think so too. Men should be what they seem,
1294Or those that be not, would they might seem none.

Othello

1295Certain, men should be what they seem.

Iago

1296Why then, I think Cassio’s an honest man.

Othello

1297Nay, yet there’s more in this.
1298I prithee speak to me as to thy thinkings,
1299As thou dost ruminate, and give thy worst of thoughts
1300The worst of words.

Iago

The worst of words. Good my lord, pardon me.
1301Though I am bound to every act of duty,
1302I am not bound to that all slaves are free to.
1303Utter my thoughts? Why, say they are vile and false—
1304As where’s that palace whereinto foul things
1305Sometimes intrude not? Who has that breast so pure
1306Wherein uncleanly apprehensions
1307Keep leets and law-days, and in sessions sit
1308With meditations lawful?

Othello

1309Thou dost conspire against thy friend, Iago,
1310If thou but think’st him wronged and mak’st his ear
1311A stranger to thy thoughts.

Iago

A stranger to thy thoughts. I do beseech you,
1312Though I perchance am vicious in my guess—
1313As I confess it is my nature’s plague
1314To spy into abuses, and oft my jealousy
1315Shapes faults that are not—that your wisdom
1316From one that so imperfectly conceits
1317Would take no notice, nor build yourself a trouble
1318Out of his scattering and unsure observance.
1319It were not for your quiet nor your good,
1320Nor for my manhood, honesty, and wisdom
1321To let you know my thoughts.

Othello

To let you know my thoughts. What dost thou mean?

Iago

1322Good name in man and woman, dear my lord,
1323Is the immediate jewel of their souls.
1324Who steals my purse, steals trash—’tis something, nothing;
1325’Twas mine, ’tis his, and has been slave to thousands—
1326But he that filches from me my good name
1327Robs me of that which not enriches him,
1328And makes me poor indeed.

Othello

And makes me poor indeed. I’ll know thy thoughts.

Iago

1329You cannot, if my heart were in your hand,
1330Nor shall not, whilst ’tis in my custody.

Othello

1331Ha?

Iago

Ha? Oh, beware, my lord, of jealousy.
1332It is the green-eyed monster, which doth mock
1333The meat it feeds on. That cuckold lives in bliss
1334Who, certain of his fate, loves not his wronger;
1335But oh, what damnèd minutes tells he o’er
1336Who dotes yet doubts, suspects yet soundly loves?

Othello

1337Oh, misery!

Iago

1338Poor and content is rich, and rich enough,
1339But riches fineless is as poor as winter
1340To him that ever fears he shall be poor.
1341Good God, the souls of all my tribe defend
1342From jealousy.

Othello

From jealousy. Why? Why is this?
1343Think’st thou I’d make a life of jealousy,
1344To follow still the changes of the moon
1345With fresh suspicions? No! To be once in doubt
1346Is to be resolved. Exchange me for a goat
1347When I shall turn the business of my soul
1348To such exsuffilate and blowed surmises
1349Matching thy inference. ’Tis not to make me jealous
1350To say my wife is fair, feeds well, loves company,
1351Is free of speech, sings, plays, and dances;
1352Where virtue is, these are more virtuous.
1353Nor from mine own weak merits will I draw
1354The smallest fear or doubt of her revolt,
1355For she had eyes and chose me. No, Iago,
1356I’ll see before I doubt; when I doubt, prove;
1357And on the proof, there is no more but this:
1358Away at once with love or jealousy.

Iago

1359I am glad of this, for now I shall have reason
1360To show the love and duty that I bear you
1361With franker spirit. Therefore, as I am bound,
1362Receive it from me. I speak not yet of proof.
1363Look to your wife; observe her well with Cassio.
1364Wear your eyes thus, not jealous nor secure.
1365I would not have your free and noble nature
1366Out of self-bounty be abused. Look to’t.
1367I know our country disposition well.
1368In Venice they do let God see the pranks
1369They dare not show their husbands; their best conscience
1370Is not to leave’t undone, but keep’t unknown.

Othello

1371Dost thou say so?

Iago

1372She did deceive her father, marrying you;
1373And when she seemed to shake and fear your looks,
1374She loved them most.

1403May fall to match you with her country forms,

Iago

1423This fellow’s of exceeding honesty,
1424And knows all qualities with a learned spirit
1425Of human dealings. If I do prove her haggard,
1426Though that her jesses were my dear heartstrings,

1508I am sorry to hear this.

1549I think that thou art just, and think thou art not.
1550I’ll have some proof. Her name, that was as fresh
1551As Dian’s visage, is now begrimed and black
1552As mine own face. If there be cords or knives,
1553Poison, or fire, or suffocating streams,
1554I’ll not endure it. Would I were satisfied.

Iago

1555I see you are eaten up with passion;
1556I do repent me that I put it to you.
1557You would be satisfied?

Othello

You would be satisfied? Would? Nay, and I will.

Iago

1558And may—but how? how satisfied, my lord?
1559Would you, the supervisor, grossly gape on?
1560Behold her topped?

Othello

Behold her topped? Death and damnation! Oh!

Iago

1561It were a tedious difficulty, I think,
1562To bring them to that prospect. Damn them then,
1563If ever mortal eyes do see them bolster
1564More than their own. What then? How then?
1565What shall I say? Where’s satisfaction?
1566It is impossible you should see this
1567Were they as prime as goats, as hot as monkeys,
1568As salt as wolves in pride, and fools as gross
1569As ignorance made drunk. But yet, I say,
1570If imputation and strong circumstances,
1571Which lead directly to the door of truth,
1572Will give you satisfaction, you might have’t.

Othello

1573Give me a living reason she’s disloyal.

Iago

1574I do not like the office;
1575But sith I am entered in this cause so far—
1576Pricked to’t by foolish honesty and love—
1577I will go on: I lay with Cassio lately,
1578And, being troubled with a raging tooth,
1579I could not sleep. There are a kind of men
1580So loose of soul that in their sleeps will mutter
1581Their affairs; one of this kind is Cassio.
1582In sleep I heard him say "Sweet Desdemona”,
1583"Let us be wary; let us hide our loves”.
1584And then, sir, would he gripe and wring my hand,
1585Cry “O sweet creature”!, then kiss me hard,
1586As if he plucked up kisses by the roots
1587That grew upon my lips, then laid his leg
1588O’er my thigh, and sighed, and kissed, and then
1589Cried “Cursèd fate that gave thee to the Moor”!

Othello

1590Oh, monstrous! monstrous!

Iago

Oh, monstrous! monstrous! Nay, this was but his dream.

Othello

1591But this denoted a foregone conclusion;
1592’Tis a shrewd doubt, though it be but a dream.

Iago

1593And this may help to thicken other proofs
1594That do demonstrate thinly.

Othello

That do demonstrate thinly. I’ll tear her all to pieces!

Iago

1595Nay, yet be wise, yet we see nothing done;
1596She may be honest yet. Tell me but this:
1597Have you not sometimes seen a handkerchief
1598Spotted with strawberries in your wife’s hand?

Othello

1599I gave her such a one; ’twas my first gift.

Iago

1600I know not that, but such a handkerchief—
1601I am sure it was your wife’s—did I today
1602See Cassio wipe his beard with.

Othello

See Cassio wipe his beard with. If it be that—

Iago

1603If it be that, or any, it was hers.
1604It speaks against her with the other proofs.

Othello

1605Oh, that the slave had forty thousand lives!
1606One is too poor, too weak for my revenge.
1607Now do I see ’tis true. Look here, Iago:
1608All my fond love thus do I blow to heaven—’tis gone!
1609Arise, black vengeance from the hollow hell.
1610Yield up, O love, thy crown and hearted throne
1611To tyrannous hate. Swell, bosom, with thy fraught,
1612For ’tis of aspics’ tongues.

Iago

For ’tis of aspics’ tongues. Yet be content.

[Othello kneels.]

Othello

1613Oh, blood, blood, blood!

Iago

1614Patience, I say. Your mind may change.

Othello

1615Never, Iago. Like to the Pontic Sea,
1616Whose icy current and compulsive course
1617Ne’er keeps retiring ebb but keeps due on
1618To the Propontic and the Hellespont,
1619Even so my bloody thoughts with violent pace
1620Shall ne’er look back, ne’er ebb to humble love,
1621Till that a capable and wide revenge
1622Swallow them up. Now, by yon marble heaven,
1623In the due reverence of a sacred vow,
1624I here engage my words.

Iago

I here engage my words. Do not rise yet.
1625Witness, you ever-burning lights above,
1626You elements that clip us round about,

[Iago kneels.]

1627Witness that here Iago doth give up
1628The execution of his wit, hands, heart
1629To wronged Othello’s service. Let him command,
1630And to obey shall be in me remorse,
1631What bloody business ever.

[Othello and Iago rise.]

Othello

What bloody business ever. I greet thy love
1632Not with vain thanks but with acceptance bounteous,
1633And will upon the instant put thee to’t.
1634Within these three days let me hear thee say
1635That Cassio’s not alive.

Iago

That Cassio’s not alive. My friend is dead;
1636’Tis done at your request. But let her live.

Othello

1637Damn her, lewd minx! Oh, damn her, damn her!
1638Come, go with me apart; I will withdraw
1639To furnish me with some swift means of death
1640For the fair devil. Now art thou my lieutenant.

Iago

1641I am your own forever.

Exeunt.

3.4

Enter Desdemona, Emilia, and Clown.

Desdemona

Do you know, sirrah, where Lieutenant Cassio lies?

Clown

I dare not say he lies anywhere.

Desdemona

Why, man?

Clown

He’s a soldier, and for me to say a soldier lies, ’tis stabbing.

Desdemona

Go to! Where lodges he?

Clown

To tell you where he lodges is to tell you where I lie.

Desdemona

Can anything be made of this?

Clown

I know not where he lodges, and for me to devise a lodging, and say he lies here or he lies there, were to lie in mine own throat.

Desdemona

Can you inquire him out and be edified by report?

Clown

I will catechize the world for him—that is, make questions and by them answer.

Desdemona

Seek him, bid him come hither, tell him I have moved my lord on his behalf, and hope all will be well.

Clown

To do this is within the compass of man’s wit, and therefore I will attempt the doing it.

Exit Clown.

Desdemona

1654Where should I lose the handkerchief, Emilia?

Emilia

1655I know not, madam.

Desdemona

1656Believe me, I had rather lose my purse
1657Full of crusadoes; and but my noble Moor
1658Is true of mind and made of no such baseness
1659As jealous creatures are, it were enough
1660To put him to ill thinking.

Emilia

To put him to ill thinking. Is he not jealous?

Desdemona

1661Who, he? I think the sun where he was born
1662Drew all such humors from him.

Emilia

Drew all such humors from him. Look where he comes.

Enter Othello.

Desdemona

1663I will not leave him now till Cassio
1664Be called to him. How is’t with you, my lord?

Othello

1665– Oh, hardness to dissemble!aparte
1666How do you, Desdemona?

Desdemona

How do you, Desdemona? Well, my good lord.

Othello

1667Give me your hand.

[Othello takes Desdemona’s hand.]

Give me your hand. This hand is moist, my lady.

Desdemona

1668It hath felt no age, nor known no sorrow.

Othello

1669This argues fruitfulness and liberal heart—
1670Hot, hot and moist. This hand of yours requires
1671A sequester from liberty—fasting and prayer,
1672Much castigation, exercise devout—
1673For here’s a young and sweating devil here
1674That commonly rebels. ’Tis a good hand,
1675A frank one.

Desdemona

A frank one. You may indeed say so,
1676For ’twas that hand that gave away my heart.

Othello

1677A liberal hand. The hearts of old gave hands,
1678But our new heraldry is hands, not hearts.

Desdemona

1679I cannot speak of this. Come, now your promise.

Othello

1680What promise, chuck?

Desdemona

1681I have sent to bid Cassio come speak with you.

Othello

1682I have a salt and sorry rheum offends me;
1683Lend me thy handkerchief.

Desdemona

Lend me thy handkerchief. Here, my lord.

Othello

1684That which I gave you.

Desdemona

That which I gave you. I have it not about me.

Othello

1685Not?

Desdemona

1686No, faith, my lord.

Othello

1687That’s a fault. That handkerchief
1688Did an Egyptian to my mother give.
1689She was a charmer and could almost read
1690The thoughts of people. She told her while she kept it
1691’Twould make her amiable and subdue my father
1692Entirely to her love, but if she lost it,
1693Or made a gift of it, my father’s eye
1694Should hold her loathed, and his spirits should hunt
1695After new fancies. She, dying, gave it me,
1696And bid me, when my fate would have me wived,
1697To give it her. I did so; and, take heed on’t,
1698Make it a darling like your precious eye.
1699To lose’t or give’t away were such perdition
1700As nothing else could match.

Desdemona

As nothing else could match. Is’t possible?

Othello

1701’Tis true. There’s magic in the web of it:
1702A sybil that had numbered in the world
1703The sun to course two hundred compasses,
1704In her prophetic fury sewed the work;
1705The worms were hallowed that did breed the silk,
1706And it was dyed in mummy, which the skillful
1707Conserved of maidens’ hearts.

Desdemona

Conserved of maidens’ hearts. I’faith, is’t true?

Othello

1708Most veritable; therefore look to’t well.

Desdemona

1709Then would to God that I had never seen’t!

Othello

1710Ha? Wherefore?

Desdemona

1711Why do you speak so startingly and rash?

Othello

1712Is’t lost? Is’t gone? Speak, is’t out o’th’way?

Desdemona

1713Heaven bless us!

Othello

1714Say you?

Desdemona

1715It is not lost; but what and if it were?

Othello

1716How?

Desdemona

1717I say it is not lost.

Othello

1718Fetch’t, let me see’t.

Desdemona

1719Why so I can; but I will not now.

1721Pray you, let Cassio be received again.

Othello

1722Fetch me the handkerchief, my mind misgives.

Desdemona

1723Come, come!

Othello

1725The handkerchief.

Desdemona

1728I’faith, you are to blame.

Othello

I’faith, you are to blame. Zounds!

Emilia

1729Is not this man jealous?

1741Exist and be a member of his love,
1742Whom I, with all the office of my heart,
1743Entirely honor. I would not be delayed.
1744If my offense be of such mortal kind
1745That nor my service past, nor present sorrows,
1746Nor purposed merit in futurity
1747Can ransom me into his love again,
1748But to know so must be my benefit;
1749So shall I clothe me in a forced content
1750And shut myself up in some other course
1751To fortune’s alms.

Desdemona

To fortune’s alms. Alas, thrice-gentle Cassio,
1752My advocation is not now in tune;
1753My lord is not my lord, nor should I know him
1754Were he in favor as in humor altered.
1755So help me every spirit sanctified

1757And stood within the blank of his displeasure

1770Either from Venice or some unhatched practise
1771Made demonstrable here in Cyprus to him,
1772Hath puddled his clear spirit; and in such cases
1773Men’s natures wrangle with inferior things,
1774Though great ones are their object. ’Tis even so.
1775For let our finger ache and it endues
1776Our other healthful members even to a sense
1777Of pain. Nay, we must think men are not gods,
1778Nor of them look for such observancy
1779As fits the bridal. Beshrew me much, Emilia.
1780I was, unhandsome warrior as I am,
1781Arraigning his unkindness with my soul;
1782But now I find I had suborned the witness,

Emilia

And he’s indicted falsely. Pray heaven it be

Desdemona

1786Alas the day! I never gave him cause.

Emilia

1787But jealous souls will not be answered so;

Emilia

1792Lady, amen.
