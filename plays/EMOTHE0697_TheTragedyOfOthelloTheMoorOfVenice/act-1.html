William Shakespeare

The Tragedy of Othello, the Moor of Venice

Act →

1.1

Enter Roderigo and Iago.

Roderigo

1Tush, never tell me! I take it much unkindly
2That thou, Iago, who hast had my purse
3As if the strings were thine, shouldst know of this.

Iago

4’Sblood, but you’ll not hear me! If ever I
5Did dream of such a matter, abhor me.

Roderigo

6Thou told’st me thou didst hold him in thy hate.

Iago

7Despise me if I do not. Three great ones of the city,
8In personal suit to make me his lieutenant,
9Off-capped to him—and by the faith of man
10I know my price; I am worth no worse a place—
11But he, as loving his own pride and purposes,
12Evades them with a bombast circumstance
13Horribly stuffed with epithets of war,
14Non-suits my mediators. For “Certes”, says he,
15“I have already chose my officer”.
16And what was he?
17Forsooth, a great arithmetician,
18One Michael Cassio, a Florentine—
19A fellow almost damned in a fair wife—
20That never set a squadron in the field,
21Nor the division of a battle knows
22More than a spinster, unless the bookish theoric
23Wherein the tonguèd consuls can propose
24As masterly as he. Mere prattle without practice
25Is all his soldiership. But he, sir, had th’election;
26And I—of whom his eyes had seen the proof
27At Rhodes, at Cyprus, and on other grounds,
28Christened and heathen—must be beleed and calmed
29By debitor and creditor. This countercaster,
30He, in good time, must his lieutenant be,
31And I, God bless the mark, his Moorship’s ancient.

Roderigo

32By heaven, I rather would have been his hangman.

Iago

33Why, there’s no remedy. ’Tis the curse of service;
34Preferment goes by letter and affection,
35And not by old gradation, where each second
36Stood heir to th’first. Now sir, be judge yourself
37Whether I in any just term am affined
38To love the Moor.

Roderigo

39I would not follow him then.

Iago

40Oh, sir, content you.
41I follow him to serve my turn upon him.
42We cannot all be masters, nor all masters
43Cannot be truly followed. You shall mark
44Many a duteous and knee-crooking knave
45That, doting on his own obsequious bondage,
46Wears out his time, much like his master’s ass,
47For naught but provender, and when he’s old—cashiered.
48Whip me such honest knaves! Others there are
49Who, trimmed in forms and visages of duty,
50Keep yet their hearts attending on themselves,
51And, throwing but shows of service on their lords,
52Do well thrive by them—and, when they have lined their coats,
53Do themselves homage. These fellows have some soul,
54And such a one do I profess myself. For, sir,
55It is as sure as you are Roderigo,
56Were I the Moor, I would not be Iago.
57In following him, I follow but myself.
58Heaven is my judge, not I for love and duty,
59But seeming so for my peculiar end.
60For when my outward action doth demonstrate
61The native act and figure of my heart
62In complement extern, ’tis not long after
63But I will wear my heart upon my sleeve
64For daws to peck at: I am not what I am.

Roderigo

65What a full fortune does the thick-lips owe
66If he can carry’t thus!

Iago

If he can carry’t thus! Call up her father.
67Rouse him, make after him, poison his delight,
68Proclaim him in the streets. Incense her kinsmen,
69And, though he in a fertile climate dwell,
70Plague him with flies; though that his joy be joy,
71Yet throw such chances of vexation on’t
72As it may lose some color.

Roderigo

73Here is her father’s house. I’ll call aloud.

Iago

74Do, with like timorous accent and dire yell
75As when, by night and negligence, the fire
76Is spied in populous cities.

Roderigo

77What ho! Brabantio, Signor Brabantio, ho!

Iago

78Awake! What ho, Brabantio! Thieves, thieves!
79Look to your house, your daughter, and your bags!
80Thieves, thieves!

[Enter Brabantio above at a window.]

Brabantio

81What is the reason of this terrible summons?
82What is the matter there?

Roderigo

83Signor, is all your family within?

Iago

84Are your doors locked?

Brabantio

Are your doors locked? Why? Wherefore ask you this?

Iago

85Zounds, sir, you’re robbed! For shame, put on your gown!
86Your heart is burst; you have lost half your soul.
87Even now, now, very now, an old black ram
88Is tupping your white ewe. Arise, arise!
89Awake the snorting citizens with the bell,
90Or else the devil will make a grandsire of you.
91Arise, I say!

Brabantio

Arise, I say! What, have you lost your wits?

Roderigo

92Most reverend signor, do you know my voice?

Brabantio

93Not I. What are you?

Roderigo

94My name is Roderigo.

Brabantio

95The worser welcome.
96I have charged thee not to haunt about my doors.
97In honest plainness thou hast heard me say
98My daughter is not for thee. And now in madness,
99Being full of supper and distempering drafts,
100Upon malicious bravery dost thou come
101To start my quiet.

Roderigo

To start my quiet. Sir, sir, sir—

Brabantio

To start my quiet. Sir, sir, sir— But thou must needs be sure
102My spirit and my place have in their power
103To make this bitter to thee.

Roderigo

To make this bitter to thee. Patience, good sir.

Brabantio

104What tell’st thou me of robbing? This is Venice;
105My house is not a grange.

Roderigo

My house is not a grange. Most grave Brabantio,
106In simple and pure soul, I come to you.

Iago

Zounds, sir, you are one of those that will not serve God if the devil bid you. Because we come to do you service and you think we are ruffians, you’ll have your daughter covered with a Barbary horse, you’ll have your nephews neigh to you, you’ll have coursers for cousins and jennets for germans.

Brabantio

108What profane wretch art thou?

Iago

I am one, sir, that comes to tell you your daughter and the Moor are making the beast with two backs.

Brabantio

110Thou art a villain.

Iago

Thou art a villain. You are a senator.

Brabantio

111This thou shalt answer. I know thee, Roderigo.

Roderigo

112Sir, I will answer anything. But I beseech you,
113If’t be your pleasure and most wise consent,
114As partly I find it is, that your fair daughter,
115At this odd-even and dull watch o’th’night,
116Transported with no worse nor better guard
117But with a knave of common hire, a gondolier,
118To the gross clasps of a lascivious Moor—
119If this be known to you and your allowance,
120We then have done you bold and saucy wrongs.
121But if you know not this, my manners tell me
122We have your wrong rebuke. Do not believe
123That from the sense of all civility
124I thus would play and trifle with your reverence.
125Your daughter, if you have not given her leave,
126I say again, hath made a gross revolt,
127Tying her duty, beauty, wit, and fortunes
128In an extravagant and wheeling stranger
129Of here and everywhere. Straight satisfy yourself:
130If she be in her chamber or your house,
131Let loose on me the justice of the state
132For thus deluding you.

Brabantio

For thus deluding you. Strike on the tinder, ho!
133Give me a taper. Call up all my people.
134This accident is not unlike my dream;
135Belief of it oppresses me already.
136Light, I say, light!

Exit [Brabantio].

Iago

Light, I say, light! Farewell, for I must leave you.
137It seems not meet nor wholesome to my place
138To be produced—as, if I stay, I shall—
139Against the Moor. For I do know the state,
140However this may gall him with some check,
141Cannot with safety cast him, for he’s embarked
142With such loud reason to the Cyprus wars,
143Which even now stands in act, that for their souls
144Another of his fathom they have none
145To lead their business. In which regard,
146Though I do hate him as I do hell pains,
147Yet, for necessity of present life,
148I must show out a flag and sign of love—
149Which is indeed but sign. That you shall surely find him,
150Lead to the Sagittary the raisèd search,
151And there will I be with him. So farewell.

Exit [Iago].

Enter Brabantio in his nightgown, and servants with torches.

Brabantio

152It is too true an evil. Gone she is,
153And what’s to come of my despisèd time
154Is naught but bitterness. Now Roderigo,
155Where didst thou see her?—Oh, unhappy girl!—
156With the Moor sayst thou?—Who would be a father?—
157How didst thou know ’twas she?—Oh, she deceives me
158Past thought!—What said she to you? Get more tapers;
159Raise all my kindred! Are they married think you?

Roderigo

160Truly, I think they are.

Brabantio

161O heaven! How got she out? Oh, treason of the blood!
162Fathers, from hence trust not your daughters’ minds
163By what you see them act. Is there not charms
164By which the property of youth and maidhood
165May be abused? Have you not read, Roderigo,
166Of some such thing?

Roderigo

Of some such thing? Yes, sir, I have indeed.

Brabantio

167Call up my brother.—Oh, would you had had her!—
168Some one way, some another.—Do you know
169Where we may apprehend her and the Moor?

Roderigo

170I think I can discover him, if you please
171To get good guard and go along with me.

Brabantio

172Pray you, lead on. At every house I’ll call—
173I may command at most. Get weapons, ho,
174And raise some special officers of night!
175On, good Roderigo; I will deserve your pains.

Exeunt.

1.2

Enter Othello, Iago, [and] attendants, with torches.

Iago

176Though in the trade of war I have slain men,
177Yet do I hold it very stuff o’th’conscience
178To do no contrived murder; I lack iniquity
179Sometime to do me service. Nine or ten times
180I had thought t’have yerked him here under the ribs.

Othello

181’Tis better as it is.

Iago

’Tis better as it is. Nay, but he prated,
182And spoke such scurvy and provoking terms
183Against your honor
184That with the little godliness I have
185I did full hard forbear him. But I pray you, sir,
186Are you fast married? Be assured of this,
187That the magnifico is much beloved,
188And hath in his effect a voice potential
189As double as the duke’s. He will divorce you,
190Or put upon you what restraint or grievance
191The law, with all his might to enforce it on,
192Will give him cable.

Othello

Will give him cable. Let him do his spite;
193My services, which I have done the signory,
194Shall out-tongue his complaints. ’Tis yet to know—
195Which, when I know that boasting is an honor,
196I shall promulgate—I fetch my life and being
197From men of royal siege; and my demerits
198May speak unbonneted to as proud a fortune
199As this that I have reached. For know, Iago,
200But that I love the gentle Desdemona,
201I would not my unhousèd free condition
202Put into circumscription and confine
203For the sea’s worth. But look, what lights come yond?

Enter Cassio [and officers] with torches.

Iago

204Those are the raisèd father and his friends;
205You were best go in.

Othello

You were best go in. Not I. I must be found.
206My parts, my title, and my perfect soul
207Shall manifest me rightly. Is it they?

Iago

208By Janus, I think no.

Othello

209The servants of the duke? And my lieutenant?
210The goodness of the night upon you, friends.
211What is the news?

Cassio

What is the news? The duke does greet you, general,
212And he requires your haste-post-haste appearance,
213Even on the instant.

Othello

Even on the instant. What is the matter, think you?

Cassio

214Something from Cyprus, as I may divine.
215It is a business of some heat. The galleys
216Have sent a dozen sequent messengers
217This very night at one another’s heels,
218And many of the consuls, raised and met,
219Are at the duke’s already. You have been hotly called for,
220When, being not at your lodging to be found,
221The Senate hath sent about three several quests
222To search you out.

Othello

To search you out. ’Tis well I am found by you.
223I will but spend a word here in the house
224And go with you.

[Exit Othello.]

Cassio

And go with you. Ancient, what makes he here?

Iago

225Faith, he tonight hath boarded a land carrack.
226If it prove lawful prize, he’s made forever.

Cassio

227I do not understand.

Iago

I do not understand. He’s married.

Cassio

I do not understand. He’s married. To who?

Iago

228Marry to—

[Enter Othello.]

Marry to— Come captain, will you go?

Othello

Marry to— Come captain, will you go? Have with you.

Cassio

229Here comes another troop to seek for you.

Enter Brabantio, Roderigo, [and] officers [with] torches [and weapons].

Iago

230It is Brabantio. General, be advised;
231He comes to bad intent.

Othello

He comes to bad intent. Holla, stand there.

Roderigo

232Signor, it is the Moor.

Brabantio

Signor, it is the Moor. Down with him, thief.

[Both sides draw their swords.]

Iago

233You, Roderigo? Come, sir, I am for you.

Othello

Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them. Good signor, you shall more command with years than with your weapons.

Brabantio

235O thou foul thief, where hast thou stowed my daughter?
236Damned as thou art, thou hast enchanted her;
237For I’ll refer me to all things of sense
238If she in chains of magic were not bound,
239Whether a maid so tender, fair, and happy,
240So opposite to marriage that she shunned
241The wealthy curled darlings of our nation,
242Would ever have, t’incur a general mock,
243Run from her guardage to the sooty bosom
244Of such a thing as thou—to fear, not to delight.
245Judge me the world, if ’tis not gross in sense
246That thou hast practiced on her with foul charms,
247Abused her delicate youth with drugs or minerals
248That weaken motion. I’ll have’t disputed on—
249’Tis probable and palpable to thinking.
250I therefore apprehend and do attach thee
251For an abuser of the world, a practicer
252Of arts inhibited and out of warrant.
253Lay hold upon him; if he do resist,
254Subdue him at his peril.

Othello

Subdue him at his peril. Hold your hands,
255Both you of my inclining and the rest.
256Were it my cue to fight, I should have known it
257Without a prompter. Whither will you that I go
258To answer this your charge?

Brabantio

To answer this your charge? To prison, till fit time
259Of law and course of direct session
260Call thee to answer.

Othello

Call thee to answer. What if I do obey?
261How may the duke be therewith satisfied,
262Whose messengers are here about my side
263Upon some present business of the state
264To bring me to him?

Officer

To bring me to him? ’Tis true, most worthy signor.
265The duke’s in council, and your noble self
266I am sure is sent for.

Brabantio

I am sure is sent for. How? The duke in council?
267In this time of the night? Bring him away!
268Mine’s not an idle cause. The duke himself,
269Or any of my brothers of the state,
270Cannot but feel this wrong as ’twere their own;
271For if such actions may have passage free,
272Bondslaves and pagans shall our statesmen be.

Exeunt.

1.3

Enter Duke [and] Senators [at a table, with lights] and officers.

Duke

273There is no composition in this news
274That gives them credit.

1 Senator

That gives them credit. Indeed, they are disproportioned;
275My letters say a hundred and seven galleys.

Duke

276And mine a hundred forty.

2 Senator

And mine a hundred forty. And mine two hundred.
277But though they jump not on a just account—
278As in these cases where the aim reports
279’Tis oft with difference—yet do they all confirm
280A Turkish fleet, and bearing up to Cyprus.

Duke

281Nay, it is possible enough to judgment;
282I do not so secure me in the error,
283But the main article I do approve
284In fearful sense.

Sailor

285(Within) What ho, what ho, what ho!

Enter Sailor.

Officer

286A messenger from the galleys.

Duke

287Now, what’s the business?

Sailor

288The Turkish preparation makes for Rhodes,
289So was I bid report here to the state
290By Signor Angelo.

Duke

291How say you by this change?

1 Senator

How say you by this change? This cannot be,
292By no assay of reason. ’Tis a pageant
293To keep us in false gaze. When we consider
294Th’importancy of Cyprus to the Turk,
295And let ourselves again but understand
296That as it more concerns the Turk than Rhodes,
297So may he with more facile question bear it,
298For that it stands not in such warlike brace,
299But altogether lacks th’abilities
300That Rhodes is dressed in. If we make thought of this,
301We must not think the Turk is so unskillful
302To leave that latest which concerns him first,
303Neglecting an attempt of ease and gain
304To wake and wage a danger profitless.
305Nay, in all confidence he’s not for Rhodes.

Officer

306Here is more news.

Enter a Messenger.

Messenger

307The Ottomites, reverend and gracious,
308Steering with due course toward the isle of Rhodes,
309Have there injointed them with an after fleet.

1 Senator

310Ay, so I thought. How many, as you guess?

Messenger

311Of thirty sail; and now they do restem
312Their backward course, bearing with frank appearance
313Their purposes toward Cyprus. Signor Montano,
314Your trusty and most valiant servitor,
315With his free duty, recommends you thus
316And prays you to believe him.

Duke

317’Tis certain then for Cyprus.
318Marcus Luccicos—is not he in town?

1 Senator

319He’s now in Florence.

Duke

320Write from us to him; post-post-haste, dispatch.

1 Senator

321Here comes Brabantio and the valiant Moor.

Enter Brabantio, Othello, Cassio, Iago, Roderigo, and officers.

Duke

322Valiant Othello, we must straight employ you
323Against the general enemy Ottoman.
324[To Brabantio] I did not see you. Welcome, gentle signor.
325We lacked your counsel and your help tonight.

Brabantio

326So did I yours. Good your grace, pardon me.
327Neither my place nor aught I heard of business
328Hath raised me from my bed; nor doth the general care
329Take hold on me, for my particular grief
330Is of so floodgate and o’erbearing nature
331That it engluts and swallows other sorrows
332And it is still itself.

Duke

And it is still itself. Why? What’s the matter?

Brabantio

333My daughter! Oh, my daughter!

1 Senator

My daughter! Oh, my daughter! Dead?

Brabantio

My daughter! Oh, my daughter! Dead? Ay, to me.
334She is abused, stolen from me, and corrupted
335By spells and medicines bought of mountebanks;
336For nature so preposterously to err—
337Being not deficient, blind, or lame of sense—
338Sans witchcraft could not.

Duke

339Whoe’er he be that in this foul proceeding
340Hath thus beguiled your daughter of herself,
341And you of her, the bloody book of law
342You shall yourself read in the bitter letter
343After your own sense, yea, though our proper son
344Stood in your action.

Brabantio

Stood in your action. Humbly I thank your grace.
345Here is the man—this Moor, whom now it seems
346Your special mandate for the state affairs
347Hath hither brought.
Hath hither brought. We are very sorry for’t.

Duke

348[To Othello] What, in your own part, can you say to this?

Brabantio

349Nothing but “This is so”.

Othello

350Most potent, grave, and reverend signors,
351My very noble and approved good masters,
352That I have ta’en away this old man’s daughter,
353It is most true; true, I have married her.
354The very head and front of my offending
355Hath this extent, no more. Rude am I in my speech,
356And little blessed with the soft phrase of peace—
357For since these arms of mine had seven year’s pith,
358Till now some nine moons wasted, they have used
359Their dearest action in the tented field—
360And little of this great world can I speak
361More than pertains to feats of broils and battle,
362And therefore little shall I grace my cause
363In speaking for myself. Yet, by your gracious patience,
364I will a round, unvarnished tale deliver
365Of my whole course of love—what drugs, what charms,
366What conjuration, and what mighty magic—
367For such proceeding I am charged withal—
368I won his daughter.

Brabantio

I won his daughter. A maiden never bold,
369Of spirit so still and quiet that her motion
370Blushed at herself, and she—in spite of nature,
371Of years, of country, credit, everything—
372To fall in love with what she feared to look on?
373It is a judgment maimed and most imperfect
374That will confess perfection so could err
375Against all rules of nature, and must be driven
376To find out practices of cunning hell
377Why this should be. I therefore vouch again
378That with some mixtures powerful o’er the blood,
379Or with some dram conjured to this effect,
380He wrought upon her.

Duke

He wrought upon her. To vouch this is no proof
381Without more wider and more overt test
382Than these thin habits and poor likelihoods
383Of modern seeming do prefer against him.

1 Senator

384But, Othello, speak:
385Did you by indirect and forcèd courses
386Subdue and poison this young maid’s affections?
387Or came it by request and such fair question
388As soul to soul affordeth?

Othello

As soul to soul affordeth? I do beseech you,
389Send for the lady to the Sagittary
390And let her speak of me before her father;
391If you do find me foul in her report,
392The trust, the office I do hold of you
393Not only take away, but let your sentence
394Even fall upon my life.

Duke

Even fall upon my life. Fetch Desdemona hither.

[Exeunt two or three officers.]

Othello

395Ancient, conduct them; you best know the place.

[Exit Iago.]

396And till she come, as truly as to heaven
397I do confess the vices of my blood,
398So justly to your grave ears I’ll present
399How I did thrive in this fair lady’s love,
400And she in mine.

Duke

And she in mine. Say it, Othello.

Othello

401Her father loved me, oft invited me,
402Still questioned me the story of my life
403From year to year—the battle, sieges, fortune
404That I have passed.
405I ran it through, even from my boyish days
406To th’very moment that he bade me tell it,
407Wherein I spoke of most disastrous chances,
408Of moving accidents by flood and field,
409Of hairbreadth scapes i’th’imminent deadly breach;
410Of being taken by the insolent foe
411And sold to slavery, of my redemption thence
412And portance in my traveler’s history,
413Wherein of antars vast and deserts idle,
414Rough quarries, rocks, hills whose heads touch heaven,
415It was my hint to speak—such was my process—
416And of the cannibals that each other eat,
417The anthropophagi, and men whose heads
418Do grow beneath their shoulders. These things to hear
419Would Desdemona seriously incline,
420But still the house affairs would draw her thence,
421Which ever as she could with haste dispatch,
422She’d come again and with a greedy ear
423Devour up my discourse; which I, observing,
424Took once a pliant hour and found good means
425To draw from her a prayer of earnest heart
426That I would all my pilgrimage dilate,
427Whereof by parcels she had something heard,
428But not intentively. I did consent,
429And often did beguile her of her tears
430When I did speak of some distressful stroke
431That my youth suffered. My story being done,
432She gave me for my pains a world of kisses;
433She swore in faith ’twas strange, ’twas passing strange;
434’Twas pitiful, ’twas wondrous pitiful.
435She wished she had not heard it, yet she wished
436That heaven had made her such a man. She thanked me,
437And bade me, if I had a friend that loved her,
438I should but teach him how to tell my story
439And that would woo her. Upon this hint I spake.
440She loved me for the dangers I had passed,
441And I loved her that she did pity them.
442This only is the witchcraft I have used.
443Here comes the lady; let her witness it.

Enter Desdemona, Iago, [and] attendants.

Duke

444I think this tale would win my daughter too.
445Good Brabantio, take up this mangled matter at the best.
446Men do their broken weapons rather use
447Than their bare hands.

Brabantio

Than their bare hands. I pray you hear her speak.
448If she confess that she was half the wooer,
449Destruction on my head if my bad blame
450Light on the man. Come hither, gentle mistress.
451Do you perceive in all this noble company
452Where most you owe obedience?

Desdemona

Where most you owe obedience? My noble father,
453I do perceive here a divided duty.
454To you I am bound for life and education;
455My life and education both do learn me
456How to respect you. You are the lord of duty;
457I am hitherto your daughter. But here’s my husband,
458And so much duty as my mother showed
459To you, preferring you before her father,
460So much I challenge that I may profess
461Due to the Moor my lord.

Brabantio

462God be with you! I have done.
463Please it your grace, on to the state affairs.
464I had rather to adopt a child than get it.
465Come hither, Moor.
466[To Othello] I here do give thee that with all my heart
467Which, but thou hast already, with all my heart
468I would keep from thee.
I would keep from thee. [To Desdemona] For your sake, jewel,
469I am glad at soul I have no other child,
470For thy escape would teach me tyranny
471To hang clogs on them.
To hang clogs on them. [To the Duke] I have done, my lord.

Duke

472Let me speak like yourself and lay a sentence,
473Which as a grise or step may help these lovers
474Into your favor.
475When remedies are past, the griefs are ended
476By seeing the worst, which late on hopes depended.
477To mourn a mischief that is past and gone
478Is the next way to draw new mischief on.
479What cannot be preserved when fortune takes,
480Patience her injury a mockery makes.
481The robbed that smiles steals something from the thief;
482He robs himself that spends a bootless grief.

Brabantio

483So let the Turk of Cyprus us beguile;
484We lose it not so long as we can smile.
485He bears the sentence well that nothing bears,
486But the free comfort which from thence he hears;
487But he bears both the sentence and the sorrow
488That, to pay grief, must of poor patience borrow.
489These sentences to sugar or to gall,
490Being strong on both sides, are equivocal.
491But words are words; I never yet did hear
492That the bruised heart was piercèd through the ears.
493I humbly beseech you proceed to th’affairs of state.

Duke

The Turk with a most mighty preparation makes for Cyprus. Othello, the fortitude of the place is best known to you, and though we have there a substitute of most allowed sufficiency, yet opinion, a more sovereign mistress of effects, throws a more safer voice on you. You must therefore be content to slubber the gloss of your new fortunes with this more stubborn and boisterous expedition.

Othello

495The tyrant custom, most grave senators,
496Hath made the flinty and steel couch of war
497My thrice-driven bed of down. I do agnize
498A natural and prompt alacrity
499I find in hardness, and do undertake
500This present war against the Ottomites.
501Most humbly therefore bending to your state,
502I crave fit disposition for my wife,
503Due reference of place and exhibition,
504With such accommodation and besort
505As levels with her breeding.

Duke

506Why, at her father’s.

Brabantio

Why, at her father’s. I will not have it so.

Othello

507Nor I.

Desdemona

508Nor would I there reside
509To put my father in impatient thoughts
510By being in his eye. Most gracious duke,
511To my unfolding lend your prosperous ear,
512And let me find a charter in your voice
513T’assist my simpleness.

Duke

T’assist my simpleness. What would you, Desdemona?

Desdemona

514That I did love the Moor to live with him,
515My downright violence and storm of fortunes
516May trumpet to the world. My heart’s subdued
517Even to the very quality of my lord;
518I saw Othello’s visage in his mind,
519And to his honors and his valiant parts
520Did I my soul and fortunes consecrate.
521So that, dear lords, if I be left behind,
522A moth of peace, and he go to the war,
523The rites for why I love him are bereft me,
524And I a heavy interim shall support
525By his dear absence. Let me go with him.

Othello

526Let her have your voice.
527Vouch with me, heaven; I therefore beg it not
528To please the palate of my appetite,
529Nor to comply with heat the young affects
530In my defunct and proper satisfaction,
531But to be free and bounteous to her mind;
532And heaven defend your good souls that you think
533I will your serious and great business scant
534When she is with me. No, when light-winged toys
535Of feathered Cupid seel with wanton dullness
536My speculative and officed instruments
537That my disports corrupt and taint my business,
538Let housewives make a skillet of my helm
539And all indign and base adversities
540Make head against my estimation.

Duke

541Be it as you shall privately determine,
542Either for her stay or going. Th’affair cries haste,
543And speed must answer it.

1 Senator

And speed must answer it. You must away tonight.

Desdemona

544Tonight, my lord?

Duke

Tonight, my lord? This night.

Othello

Tonight, my lord? This night. With all my heart.

Duke

545At nine i’th’morning here we’ll meet again.
546Othello, leave some officer behind
547And he shall our commission bring to you,
548And such things else of quality and respect
549As doth import you.

Othello

As doth import you. So please your grace, my ancient—
550A man he is of honesty and trust—
551To his conveyance I assign my wife,
552With what else needful your good grace shall think
553To be sent after me.

Duke

To be sent after me. Let it be so.
554Goodnight to everyone—and, noble signor,
555If virtue no delighted beauty lack,
556Your son-in-law is far more fair than black.

1 Senator

557Adieu, brave Moor, use Desdemona well.

Brabantio

558Look to her, Moor, if thou hast eyes to see;
559She has deceived her father, and may thee.

Exeunt [Duke, Senators, Brabantio, Cassio, Sailor, Messenger, officers, and attendants.]

Othello

560My life upon her faith!—Honest Iago,
561My Desdemona must I leave to thee.
562I prithee let thy wife attend on her,
563And bring them after in the best advantage.
564Come Desdemona, I have but an hour
565Of love, of worldly matter and direction
566To spend with thee. We must obey the time.

Exeunt [Othello and Desdemona].

Roderigo

Iago.

Iago

What sayst thou, noble heart?

Roderigo

What will I do, think’st thou?

Iago

Why, go to bed and sleep.

Roderigo

I will incontinently drown myself.

Iago

If thou dost, I shall never love thee after. Why, thou silly gentleman?

Roderigo

It is silliness to live when to live is torment; and then have we a prescription to die, when death is our physician.

Iago

Oh, villainous! I have looked upon the world for four times seven years, and, since I could distinguish betwixt a benefit and an injury, I never found man that knew how to love himself. Ere I would say I would drown myself for the love of a guinea-hen, I would change my humanity with a baboon.

Roderigo

What should I do? I confess it is my shame to be so fond, but it is not in my virtue to amend it.

Iago

Virtue? A fig! ’Tis in ourselves that we are thus or thus. Our bodies are our gardens to the which our wills are gardeners, so that if we will plant nettles or sow lettuce, set hyssop and weed up thyme, supply it with one gender of herbs or distract it with many, either to have it sterile with idleness or manured with industry—why, the power and corrigible authority of this lies in our wills. If the beam of our lives had not one scale of reason to poise another of sensuality, the blood and baseness of our natures would conduct us to most preposterous conclusions. But we have reason to cool our raging motions, our carnal stings, our unbitted lusts—whereof I take this that you call love to be a sect or scion.

Roderigo

It cannot be.

Iago

It is merely a lust of the blood and a permission of the will. Come, be a man! Drown thyself? Drown cats and blind puppies. I have professed me thy friend, and I confess me knit to thy deserving with cables of perdurable toughness. I could never better stead thee than now. Put money in thy purse. Follow thou the wars; defeat thy favor with an usurped beard. I say, put money in thy purse. It cannot be long that Desdemona should continue her love to the Moor—put money in thy purse—nor he his to her. It was a violent commencement in her, and thou shalt see an answerable sequestration—put but money in thy purse. These Moors are changeable in their wills—fill thy purse with money. The food that to him now is as luscious as locusts shall be to him shortly as acerb as coloquintida. She must change for youth; when she is sated with his body, she will find the errors of her choice. Therefore, put money in thy purse. If thou wilt needs damn thyself, do it a more delicate way than drowning. Make all the money thou canst. If sanctimony and a frail vow betwixt an erring barbarian and a super-subtle Venetian be not too hard for my wits and all the tribe of hell, thou shalt enjoy her. Therefore make money. A pox of drowning thyself. It is clean out of the way. Seek thou rather to be hanged in compassing thy joy than to be drowned and go without her.

Roderigo

Wilt thou be fast to my hopes, if I depend on the issue?

Iago

Thou art sure of me—go, make money—I have told thee often, and I retell thee again and again: I hate the Moor. My cause is hearted; thine hath no less reason. Let us be conjunctive in our revenge against him. If thou canst cuckold him, thou dost thyself a pleasure, me a sport. There are many events in the womb of time which will be delivered. Traverse, go, provide thy money. We will have more of this tomorrow. Adieu.

Roderigo

Where shall we meet i’th’morning?

Iago

At my lodging.

Roderigo

I’ll be with thee betimes.

Iago

Go to, farewell. Do you hear, Roderigo?

Roderigo

What say you?

Iago

No more of drowning, do you hear?

Roderigo

I am changed.

Iago

Go to, farewell. Put money enough in your purse.

Roderigo

I’ll sell all my land.

Exit [Roderigo].

Iago

590Thus do I ever make my fool my purse;
591For I mine own gained knowledge should profane
592If I would time expend with such a snipe
593But for my sport and profit. I hate the Moor,
594And it is thought abroad that ’twixt my sheets
595He’s done my office. I know not if’t be true,
596But I, for mere suspicion in that kind,
597Will do as if for surety. He holds me well;
598The better shall my purpose work on him.
599Cassio’s a proper man—let me see now:
600To get his place and to plume up my will
601In double knavery—How? How? Let’s see:
602After some time to abuse Othello’s ears
603That he is too familiar with his wife.
604He hath a person and a smooth dispose
605To be suspected, framed to make women false.
606The Moor is of a free and open nature
607That thinks men honest that but seem to be so,
608And will as tenderly be led by’th’nose as asses are.
609I have’t. It is engendered. Hell and night
610Must bring this monstrous birth to the world’s light.

Exit [Iago].
