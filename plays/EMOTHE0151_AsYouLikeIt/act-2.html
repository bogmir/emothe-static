William Shakespeare

As You Like It

← Act I Act III →

[2.1]

Enter Duke Senior, Amiens, and two or three Lords, like foresters.

DUKE SENIOR

169Now, my co-mates and brothers in exile,
170Hath not old custom made this life more sweet
171Than that of painted pomp? Are not these woods
172More free from peril than the envious court?
173Here feel we not the penalty of Adam,
174The seasons' difference, as the icy fang
175And churlish chiding of the winter's wind,
176Which when it bites and blows upon my body
177Even till I shrink with cold, I smile and say
178"This is no flattery; these are counselors
179That feelingly persuade me what I am."
180Sweet are the uses of adversity,
181Which, like the toad, ugly and venomous,
182Wears yet a precious jewel in his head;
183And this our life, exempt from public haunt,
184Finds tongues in trees, books in the running brooks,
185Sermons in stones, and good in everything.

AMIENS

186I would not change it. Happy is Your Grace,
187That can translate the stubbornness of fortune
188Into so quiet and so sweet a style.

DUKE SENIOR

189Come, shall we go and kill us venison?
190And yet it irks me the poor dappled fools,
191Being native burghers of this desert city,
192Should in their own confines with forkèd heads
193Have their round haunches gored.

FIRST LORD

Have their round haunches gored. Indeed, my lord,
194The melancholy Jaques grieves at that,
195And in that kind swears you do more usurp
196Than doth your brother that hath banished you.
197Today my Lord of Amiens and myself
198Did steal behind him as he lay along
199Under an oak whose antique root peeps out
200Upon the brook that brawls along this wood,
201To the which place a poor sequestered stag
202That from the hunter's aim had ta'en a hurt
203Did come to languish. And indeed, my lord,
204The wretched animal heaved forth such groans
205That their discharge did stretch his leathern coat
206Almost to bursting, and the big round tears
207Coursed one another down his innocent nose
208In piteous chase. And thus the hairy fool,
209Much markèd of the melancholy Jaques,
210Stood on th'extremest verge of the swift brook,
211Augmenting it with tears.

DUKE SENIOR

Augmenting it with tears. But what said Jaques?
212Did he not moralize this spectacle?

FIRST LORD

213Oh, yes, into a thousand similes.
214First, for his weeping into the needless stream:
215"Poor deer," quoth he, "thou mak'st a testament
216As worldlings do, giving thy sum of more
217To that which had too much." Then, being there alone,
218Left and abandoned of his velvet friends:
219"'Tis right," quoth he, "thus misery doth part
220The flux of company." Anon, a careless herd,
221Full of the pasture, jumps along by him
222And never stays to greet him. "Ay," quoth Jaques,
223"Sweep on, you fat and greasy citizens;
224'Tis just the fashion. Wherefore do you look
225Upon that poor and broken bankrupt there?"
226Thus most invectively he pierceth through
227The body of the country, city, court,
228Yea, and of this our life, swearing that we
229Are mere usurpers, tyrants, and what's worse,
230To fright the animals and to kill them up
231In their assigned and native dwelling place.

DUKE SENIOR

232And did you leave him in this contemplation?

SECOND LORD

233We did, my lord, weeping and commenting
234Upon the sobbing deer.

DUKE SENIOR

Upon the sobbing deer. Show me the place.
235I love to cope him in these sullen fits,
236For then he's full of matter.

FIRST LORD

237I'll bring you to him straight.

Exeunt.

[2.2]

Enter Duke [Frederick], with Lords.

DUKE FREDERICK

238Can it be possible that no man saw them?
239It cannot be. Some villains of my court
240Are of consent and sufferance in this.

FIRST LORD

241I cannot hear of any that did see her.
242The ladies, her attendants of her chamber,
243Saw her abed, and in the morning early
244They found the bed untreasured of their mistress.

SECOND LORD

245My lord, the roinish clown, at whom so oft
246Your Grace was wont to laugh, is also missing.
247Hisperia, the princess' gentlewoman,
248Confesses that she secretly o'erheard
249Your daughter and her cousin much commend
250The parts and graces of the wrestler
251That did but lately foil the sinewy Charles,
252And she believes, wherever they are gone,
253That youth is surely in their company.

DUKE FREDERICK

254Send to his brother; fetch that gallant hither.
255If he be absent, bring his brother to me;
256I'll make him find him. Do this suddenly,
257And let not search and inquisition quail
258To bring again these foolish runaways.

Exeunt.

[2.3]

Enter Orlando and Adam,[ meeting].

ORLANDO

259Who's there?

ADAM

260What, my young master? Oh, my gentle master!
261Oh, my sweet master, oh, you memory
262Of old Sir Rowland! Why, what make you here?
263Why are you virtuous? Why do people love you?
264And wherefore are you gentle, strong, and valiant?
265Why would you be so fond to overcome
266The bonny prizer of the humorous Duke?
267Your praise is come too swiftly home before you.
268Know you not, master, to some kind of men
269Their graces serve them but as enemies?
270No more do yours. Your virtues, gentle master,
271Are sanctified and holy traitors to you.
272Oh, what a world is this, when what is comely
273Envenoms him that bears it!

ORLANDO

274Why, what's the matter?

ADAM

Why, what's the matter? O unhappy youth,
275Come not within these doors! Within this roof
276The enemy of all your graces lives.
277Your brother — no, no brother; yet the son —
278Yet not the son; I will not call him son
279Of him I was about to call his father —
280Hath heard your praises, and this night he means
281To burn the lodging where you use to lie,
282And you within it. If he fail of that,
283He will have other means to cut you off.
284I overheard him and his practices.
285This is no place; this house is but a butchery.
286Abhor it, fear it, do not enter it.

ORLANDO

287Why, whither, Adam, wouldst thou have me go?

ADAM

288No matter whither, so you come not here.

ORLANDO

289What, wouldst thou have me go and beg my food,
290Or with a base and boist'rous sword enforce
291A thievish living on the common road?
292This I must do, or know not what to do;
293Yet this I will not do, do how I can.
294I rather will subject me to the malice
295Of a diverted blood and bloody brother.

ADAM

296But do not so. I have five hundred crowns,
297The thrifty hire I saved under your father,
298Which I did store to be my foster nurse
299When service should in my old limbs lie lame
300And unregarded age in corners thrown.
301Take that, and He that doth the ravens feed,
302Yea, providently caters for the sparrow,
303Be comfort to my age! Here is the gold;

[Offering money]

304All this I give you. Let me be your servant.
305Though I look old, yet I am strong and lusty,
306For in my youth I never did apply
307Hot and rebellious liquors in my blood,
308Nor did not with unbashful forehead woo
309The means of weakness and debility;
310Therefore my age is as a lusty winter,
311Frosty, but kindly. Let me go with you;
312I'll do the service of a younger man
313In all your business and necessities.

ORLANDO

314Oh, good old man, how well in thee appears
315The constant service of the antique world,
316When service sweat for duty, not for meed!
317Thou art not for the fashion of these times,
318Where none will sweat but for promotion,
319And having that do choke their service up
320Even with the having. It is not so with thee.
321But, poor old man, thou prun'st a rotten tree
322That cannot so much as a blossom yield
323In lieu of all thy pains and husbandry.
324But come thy ways. We'll go along together,
325And ere we have thy youthful wages spent
326We'll light upon some settled low content.

ADAM

327Master, go on, and I will follow thee
328To the last gasp, with truth and loyalty.
329From seventeen years till now almost fourscore
330Here livèd I, but now live here no more.
331At seventeen years many their fortunes seek,
332But at fourscore it is too late a week;
333Yet fortune cannot recompense me better
334Than to die well and not my master's debtor.

Exeunt.

[2.4]

Enter Rosalind for Ganymede, Celia for Aliena, and Clown, alias Touchstone.

ROSALIND

Oh, Jupiter, how weary are my spirits!

TOUCHSTONE

I care not for my spirits, if my legs were not weary.

ROSALIND

I could find in my heart to disgrace my man's apparel and to cry like a woman; but I must comfort the weaker vessel, as doublet and hose ought to show itself courageous to petticoat. Therefore, courage, good Aliena!

CELIA

I pray you, bear with me; I cannot go no further.

TOUCHSTONE

For my part, I had rather bear with you than bear you; yet I should bear no cross if I did bear you, for I think you have no money in your purse.

ROSALIND

Well, this is the Forest of Arden.

TOUCHSTONE

Ay, now am I in Arden; the more fool I. When I was at home I was in a better place, but travelers must be content.

Enter Corin and Silvius.

ROSALIND

Ay, be so, good Touchstone. — Look you, who comes here, a young man and an old in solemn talk.

[They stand aside and listen.]

CORIN

335[To Silvius] That is the way to make her scorn you still.

SILVIUS

336Oh, Corin, that thou knew'st how I do love her!

CORIN

337I partly guess; for I have loved ere now.

SILVIUS

338No, Corin, being old, thou canst not guess,
339Though in thy youth thou wast as true a lover
340As ever sighed upon a midnight pillow.
341But if thy love were ever like to mine —
342As sure I think did never man love so —
343How many actions most ridiculous
344Hast thou been drawn to by thy fantasy?

CORIN

345Into a thousand that I have forgotten.

SILVIUS

346Oh, thou didst then never love so heartily!
347If thou rememb'rest not the slightest folly
348That ever love did make thee run into,
349Thou hast not loved.
350Or if thou hast not sat as I do now,
351Wearing thy hearer in thy mistress' praise,
352Thou hast not loved.
353Or if thou hast not broke from company
354Abruptly, as my passion now makes me,
355Thou hast not loved.
356O Phoebe, Phoebe, Phoebe!

Exit Silvius

ROSALIND

357Alas, poor shepherd! Searching of thy wound,
358I have by hard adventure found mine own.

TOUCHSTONE

And I mine. I remember, when I was in love, I broke my sword upon a stone, and bid him take that for coming a-night to Jane Smile; and I remember the kissing of her batler, and the cow's dugs that her pretty chapped hands had milked; and I remember the wooing of a peascod instead of her, from whom I took two cods, and giving her them again, said with weeping tears, "Wear these for my sake." We that are true lovers run into strange capers; but as all is mortal in nature, so is all nature in love mortal in folly.

ROSALIND

Thou speak'st wiser than thou art ware of.

TOUCHSTONE

Nay, I shall ne'er be ware of mine own wit till I break my shins against it.

ROSALIND

359Jove, Jove! This shepherd's passion
360Is much upon my fashion.

TOUCHSTONE

361And mine; but it grows something stale with me.

CELIA

362I pray you, one of you question yond man
363If he for gold will give us any food.
364I faint almost to death.

TOUCHSTONE

I faint almost to death. [To Corin] Holla, you clown!

ROSALIND

365Peace, fool! He's not thy kinsman.

CORIN

Peace, fool! He's not thy kinsman. Who calls?

TOUCHSTONE

366Your betters, sir.

CORIN

Your betters, sir. Else are they very wretched.

ROSALIND

367Peace, I say. — Good even to you, friend.

CORIN

368And to you, gentle sir, and to you all.

ROSALIND

369I prithee, shepherd, if that love or gold
370Can in this desert place buy entertainment,
371Bring us where we may rest ourselves and feed.
372Here's a young maid with travel much oppressed,
373And faints for succor.

CORIN

And faints for succor. Fair sir, I pity her,
374And wish, for her sake more than for mine own,
375My fortunes were more able to relieve her;
376But I am shepherd to another man,
377And do not shear the fleeces that I graze.
378My master is of churlish disposition,
379And little recks to find the way to heaven
380By doing deeds of hospitality.
381Besides, his cote, his flocks, and bounds of feed
382Are now on sale, and at our sheepcote now,
383By reason of his absence, there is nothing
384That you will feed on. But what is, come see,
385And in my voice most welcome shall you be.

ROSALIND

386What is he that shall buy his flock and pasture?

CORIN

387That young swain that you saw here but erewhile,
388That little cares for buying anything.

ROSALIND

389I pray thee, if it stand with honesty,
390Buy thou the cottage, pasture, and the flock,
391And thou shalt have to pay for it of us.

CELIA

392And we will mend thy wages. I like this place,
393And willingly could waste my time in it.

CORIN

394Assuredly the thing is to be sold.
395Go with me. If you like upon report
396The soil, the profit, and this kind of life,
397I will your very faithful feeder be,
398And buy it with your gold right suddenly.

Exeunt.

[2.5]

Enter Amiens, Jaques, and others.

[A table is set out.]

Song

AMIENS

399[Sings] Under the greenwood tree
400Who loves to lie with me,
401And turn his merry note
402Unto the sweet bird's throat,
403Come hither, come hither, come hither.
404Here shall he see
405No enemy
406But winter and rough weather.

JAQUES

More, more, I prithee, more.

AMIENS

It will make you melancholy, Monsieur Jaques.

JAQUES

I thank it. More, I prithee, more. I can suck melancholy out of a song as a weasel sucks eggs. More, I prithee, more.

AMIENS

My voice is ragged. I know I cannot please you.

JAQUES

I do not desire you to please me, I do desire you to sing. Come, more; another stanzo. Call you 'em "stanzos"?

AMIENS

What you will, Monsieur Jaques.

JAQUES

Nay, I care not for their names; they owe me nothing. Will you sing?

AMIENS

More at your request than to please myself.

JAQUES

Well then, if ever I thank any man, I'll thank you. But that they call "compliment" is like th'encounter of two dog-apes; and when a man thanks me heartily, methinks I have given him a penny, and he renders me the beggarly thanks. Come, sing; and you that will not, hold your tongues.

AMIENS

Well, I'll end the song. — Sirs, cover the while; the Duke will drink under this tree. — He hath been all this day to look you.

[Food and drink are set out.]

JAQUES

And I have been all this day to avoid him. He is too disputable for my company. I think of as many matters as he, but I give heaven thanks and make no boast of them. Come, warble, come.

Song

AMIENS

407[Sings] Who doth ambition shun,
408And loves to live i'th' sun,
409Seeking the food he eats,
410And pleased with what he gets,

All together here

411Come hither, come hither, come hither.
412Here shall he see
413No enemy
414But winter and rough weather.

JAQUES

I'll give you a verse to this note that I made yesterday in despite of my invention.

AMIENS

And I'll sing it.

JAQUES

Thus it goes:

415If it do come to pass
416That any man turn ass,
417Leaving his wealth and ease
418A stubborn will to please,
419Ducdame, ducdame, ducdame.
420Here shall he see
421Gross fools as he,
422An if he will come to me.

AMIENS

What's that "ducdame"?

JAQUES

'Tis a Greek invocation, to call fools into a circle. I'll go sleep, if I can; if I cannot, I'll rail against all the first-born of Egypt.

AMIENS

And I'll go seek the Duke. His banquet is prepared.

Exeunt [separately].

[2.6]

Enter Orlando and Adam.

ADAM

Dear master, I can go no further. Oh, I die for food! Here lie I down, and measure out my grave. Farewell, kind master.

[He lies down.]

ORLANDO

Why, how now, Adam? No greater heart in thee? Live a little, comfort a little, cheer thyself a little. If this uncouth forest yield anything savage, I will either be food for it or bring it for food to thee. Thy conceit is nearer death than thy powers. For my sake be comfortable; hold death awhile at the arm's end. I will here be with thee presently, and if I bring thee not something to eat, I will give thee leave to die; but if thou diest before I come, thou art a mocker of my labor. Well said! Thou look'st cheerly, and I'll be with thee quickly. Yet thou liest in the bleak air. Come, I will bear thee to some shelter; and thou shalt not die for lack of a dinner, if there live anything in this desert. [He picks up Adam.] Cheerly, good Adam!

Exeunt

[2.7]

Enter Duke Senior, [Amiens], and Lords, like outlaws

DUKE SENIOR

423I think he be transformed into a beast,
424For I can nowhere find him like a man.

AMIENS

425My lord, he is but even now gone hence.
426Here was he merry, hearing of a song.

DUKE SENIOR

427If he, compact of jars, grow musical,
428We shall have shortly discord in the spheres.
429Go seek him. Tell him I would speak with him.

Enter Jaques.

AMIENS

430He saves my labor by his own approach.

DUKE SENIOR

431Why, how now, monsieur, what a life is this,
432That your poor friends must woo your company?
433What, you look merrily.

JAQUES

434A fool, a fool! I met a fool i'th'forest,
435A motley fool. A miserable world!
436As I do live by food, I met a fool,
437Who laid him down and basked him in the sun,
438And railed on Lady Fortune in good terms,
439In good set terms, and yet a motley fool.
440"Good morrow, fool," quoth I; "No, sir," quoth he,
441"Call me not fool till heaven hath sent me fortune."
442And then he drew a dial from his poke,
443And, looking on it with lack-luster eye,
444Says very wisely, "It is ten o'clock.
445Thus we may see," quoth he, "how the world wags:
446'Tis but an hour ago since it was nine,
447And after one hour more 'twill be eleven;
448And so, from hour to hour, we ripe and ripe,
449And then, from hour to hour, we rot and rot,
450And thereby hangs a tale." When I did hear
451The motley fool thus moral on the time,
452My lungs began to crow like Chanticleer
453That fools should be so deep-contemplative,
454And I did laugh sans intermission
455An hour by his dial. Oh, noble fool!
456A worthy fool! Motley's the only wear.

DUKE SENIOR

457What fool is this?

JAQUES

458Oh, worthy fool! One that hath been a courtier,
459And says, if ladies be but young and fair,
460They have the gift to know it. And in his brain,
461Which is as dry as the remainder biscuit
462After a voyage, he hath strange places crammed
463With observation, the which he vents
464In mangled forms. Oh, that I were a fool!
465I am ambitious for a motley coat.

DUKE SENIOR

466Thou shalt have one.

JAQUES

Thou shalt have one. It is my only suit,
467Provided that you weed your better judgments
468Of all opinion that grows rank in them
469That I am wise. I must have liberty
470Withal, as large a charter as the wind,
471To blow on whom I please, for so fools have.
472And they that are most gallèd with my folly,
473They most must laugh. And why, sir, must they so?
474The "why" is plain as way to parish church:
475He that a fool doth very wisely hit
476Doth very foolishly, although he smart,
477Not to seem senseless of the bob; if not,
478The wise man's folly is anatomized
479Even by the squand'ring glances of the fool.
480Invest me in my motley; give me leave
481To speak my mind, and I will through and through
482Cleanse the foul body of th'infected world,
483If they will patiently receive my medicine.

DUKE SENIOR

484Fie on thee! I can tell what thou wouldst do.

JAQUES

485What, for a counter, would I do but good?

DUKE SENIOR

486Most mischievous foul sin, in chiding sin.
487For thou thyself hast been a libertine,
488As sensual as the brutish sting itself;
489And all th'embossèd sores and headed evils
490That thou with license of free foot hast caught
491Wouldst thou disgorge into the general world.

JAQUES

492Why, who cries out on pride
493That can therein tax any private party?
494Doth it not flow as hugely as the sea,
495Till that the weary very means do ebb?
496What woman in the city do I name
497When that I say the city woman bears
498The cost of princes on unworthy shoulders?
499Who can come in and say that I mean her,
500When such a one as she, such is her neighbor?
501Or what is he of basest function
502That says his bravery is not on my cost,
503Thinking that I mean him, but therein suits
504His folly to the mettle of my speech?
505There then, how then? What then? Let me see wherein
506My tongue hath wronged him: if it do him right,
507Then he hath wronged himself; if he be free,
508Why then my taxing like a wild goose flies,
509Unclaimed of any man. But who come here?

Enter Orlando [with his sword drawn].

ORLANDO

510Forbear, and eat no more!

JAQUES

511Why, I have eat none yet.

ORLANDO

512Nor shalt not, till necessity be served.

JAQUES

513Of what kind should this cock come of?

DUKE SENIOR

514[To Orlando] Art thou thus boldened, man, by thy distress?
515Or else a rude despiser of good manners,
516That in civility thou seem'st so empty?

ORLANDO

517You touched my vein at first. The thorny point
518Of bare distress hath ta'en from me the show
519Of smooth civility; yet am I inland bred,
520And know some nurture. But forbear, I say.
521He dies that touches any of this fruit
522Till I and my affairs are answerèd.

JAQUES

523An you will not be answered with reason, I must die.

DUKE SENIOR

524What would you have? Your gentleness shall force
525More than your force move us to gentleness.

ORLANDO

526I almost die for food, and let me have it!

DUKE SENIOR

527Sit down and feed, and welcome to our table.

ORLANDO

528Speak you so gently? Pardon me, I pray you.
529I thought that all things had been savage here,
530And therefore put I on the countenance
531Of stern commandment. But whate'er you are
532That in this desert inaccessible,
533Under the shade of melancholy boughs,
534Lose and neglect the creeping hours of time;
535If ever you have looked on better days,
536If ever been where bells have knolled to church,
537If ever sat at any good man's feast,
538If ever from your eyelids wiped a tear,
539And know what 'tis to pity and be pitied,
540Let gentleness my strong enforcement be;
541In the which hope I blush, and hide my sword.

[He sheathes his sword.]

DUKE SENIOR

542True is it that we have seen better days,
543And have with holy bell been knolled to church,
544And sat at good men's feasts, and wiped our eyes
545Of drops that sacred pity hath engendered;
546And therefore sit you down in gentleness,
547And take upon command what help we have
548That to your wanting may be ministered.

ORLANDO

549Then but forbear your food a little while,
550Whiles, like a doe, I go to find my fawn,
551And give it food. There is an old poor man
552Who after me hath many a weary step
553Limped in pure love. Till he be first sufficed,
554Oppressed with two weak evils, age and hunger,
555I will not touch a bit.

DUKE SENIOR

I will not touch a bit. Go find him out,
556And we will nothing waste till you return.

ORLANDO

557I thank ye; and be blest for your good comfort!

[Exit.]

DUKE SENIOR

558Thou see'st we are not all alone unhappy:
559This wide and universal theater
560Presents more woeful pageants than the scene
561Wherein we play in.

JAQUES

Wherein we play in. All the world's a stage,
562And all the men and women merely players.
563They have their exits and their entrances,
564And one man in his time plays many parts,
565His acts being seven ages. At first the infant,
566Mewling and puking in the nurse's arms.
567Then the whining schoolboy, with his satchel
568And shining morning face, creeping like snail
569Unwillingly to school. And then the lover,
570Sighing like furnace, with a woeful ballad
571Made to his mistress' eyebrow. Then a soldier,
572Full of strange oaths, and bearded like the pard,
573Jealous in honor, sudden, and quick in quarrel,
574Seeking the bubble reputation
575Even in the cannon's mouth. And then the justice,
576In fair round belly with good capon lined,
577With eyes severe and beard of formal cut,
578Full of wise saws and modern instances;
579And so he plays his part. The sixth age shifts
580Into the lean and slippered pantaloon,
581With spectacles on nose and pouch on side,
582His youthful hose, well saved, a world too wide
583For his shrunk shank; and his big manly voice,
584Turning again toward childish treble, pipes
585And whistles in his sound. Last scene of all,
586That ends this strange eventful history,
587Is second childishness and mere oblivion,
588Sans teeth, sans eyes, sans taste, sans everything.

Enter Orlando with Adam.

DUKE SENIOR

589Welcome. Set down your venerable burden,
590And let him feed.

ORLANDO

591I thank you most for him.

ADAM

592So had you need;
593I scarce can speak to thank you for myself.

DUKE SENIOR

594Welcome. Fall to. I will not trouble you
595As yet to question you about your fortunes. —
596Give us some music; and, good cousin, sing.

Song

AMIENS

597[Sings] Blow, blow, thou winter wind,
598Thou art not so unkind
599As man's ingratitude.
600Thy tooth is not so keen,
601Because thou art not seen,
602Although thy breath be rude.
603Heigh-ho! Sing heigh-ho! unto the green holly.
604Most friendship is feigning, most loving mere folly.
605Then, heigh-ho, the holly!
606This life is most jolly.
607Freeze, freeze, thou bitter sky,
608That dost not bite so nigh
609As benefits forgot;
610Though thou the waters warp,
611Thy sting is not so sharp
612As friend remembered not.
613Heigh-ho! Sing heigh-ho! unto the green holly.
614Most friendship is feigning, most loving mere folly.
615Then, heigh-ho, the holly!
616This life is most jolly.

DUKE SENIOR

617If that you were the good Sir Rowland's son,
618As you have whispered faithfully you were,
619And as mine eye doth his effigies witness
620Most truly limned and living in your face,
621Be truly welcome hither. I am the Duke
622That loved your father. The residue of your fortune,
623Go to my cave and tell me. [To Adam] Good old man,
624Thou art right welcome as thy master is.
625[To the others] Support him by the arm. [To Orlando] Give me your hand,
626And let me all your fortunes understand.

Exeunt.
