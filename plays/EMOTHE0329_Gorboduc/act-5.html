Gorboduc

[An open plain]

ACT V SCENE I

[Enter Clotyn, Mandud, Gwenard, Fergus, and Eubulus.]

clotyn

1Did ever age bring forth such tyrants’s hearts?
2The brother hath bereft the brother’s life,
3The mother she hath dy’d her cruel hands
4In blood of her own son, and now at last
5The people, lo, forgetting truth and love,
6Contemning quite both law and loyal heart,
7Even they have slain their sovereign lord and Queen.

mandud

8Shall this their traitorous crime unpunish’d rest?
9Even yet they cease not, carried on with rage,
10In their rebellious routs, to threaten still
11A new bloodshed unto the prince’s kin,
12To slay them all, and to uproot the race
13Both of the King and Queen, so are they mov’d
14With Porrex’ death wherein they falsely charge
15The guiltless King without desert at all,
16And traitorously have murder’d him therefore,
17And eke the Queen.

gwenard

And eke the Queen. Shall subjects dare with force
18To work revenge upon their prince’s fact?
19Admit the worst that may – as sure in this
20The deed was foul, the Queen to slay her son –
21Shall yet the subjects seek to take the sword,
22Arise against his lord, and slay his King?
23O wretched state, where those rebellious hearts
24Are not rent out even from their living breasts,
25And with the body thrown unto the fowls
26As carrion food, for terror of the rest!

fergus

27There can no punishment be thought too great
28For this so grievous crime; let speed therefore
29Be used therein, for it behoveth so.

eubulus

30Ye all, my lords, I see, consent in one,
31And I as one consent with ye in all;
32I hold it more than need with sharpest law
33To punish this tumultuous bloody rage.
34For nothing more may shake the common state
35Than sufferance of uproars without redress;
36Whereby how some kingdoms of mighty power
37After great conquests made, and flourishing
38In fame and wealth, have been to ruin brought:
39I pray to Jove that we may rather wail
40Such hap in them than witness in ourselves.
41Eke fully with the Duke my mind agrees
42That no cause serves, whereby the subject may
43Call to account the doings of his prince;
44Much less in blood by sword to work revenge
45No more than may the hand cut off the head:
46In act nor speech, no, not in secret thought
47The subject may rebel against his lord,
48Or judge of him that sits in Caesar’s seat,
49With grudging mind to damn those he mislikes.
50Though kings forget to govern as they ought,
51Yet subjects must obey as they are bound.
52But now, my lords, before ye farther wade
53Or spend your speech what sharp revenge shall fall
54By justice’ plague on these rebellious wights,
55Methinks ye rather should first search the way
56By which in time the rage of this uproar
57Might be repress’d, and these great tumults ceas’d.
58Even yet the life of Britain land doth hang
59In traitors’ balance of unequal weight:
60Think not, my lords, the death of Gorboduc,
61Not yet Videna’s blood, will cease their rage.
62Even our own lives, our wives and children dear,
63Our country, dearest of all, in danger stands,
64Now to be spoil’d, now, now made desolate,
65And by ourselves a conquest to ensue.
66For give once sway unto the people’s lusts
67To rush forth on, and stay them not in time,
68And as the stream that rolleth down the hill,
69So will they headlong run with raging thoughts
70From blood to blood, from mischief unto moe,
71To ruin of the realm, themselves and all;
72So giddy are the common people’s minds,
73So glad of change, more wavering than the sea.
74Ye see, my lords, what strength these rebels have,
75What hugy number is assembled still;
76For though the traitorous fact for which they rose
77Be wrought and done, yet lodge they still in field,
78So that how far their furies yet will stretch
79Great cause we have to dread. That we may seek
80By present battle to repress their power,
81Speed must we use to levy force therefore;
82For either they forthwith will mischief work,
83Or their rebellious roars forthwith will cease:
84These violent things may have no lasting long.
85Let us therefore use this for present help:
86Persuade by gentle speech, and offer grace
87With gift of pardon, save unto the chief;
88And that upon condition that forthwith
89They yield the captains of their enterprise
90To bear such guerdon of their traitorous fact
91As may be both due vengeance to themselves
92And wholesome terror to posterity.
93This shall, I think, scatter the greatest part
94That now are holden with desire of home,
95Wearied in field with cold of winter’s nights,
96And some, no doubt, stricken with dread of law.
97When this is once proclaimed, it shall make
98The captains to mistrust the multitude,
99Whose safety bids them to betray their heads,
100And so much more because the rascal routs
101In things of great and perilous attempts
102Are never trusty to the noble race.
103And while we treat and stand on terms of grace,
104We shall both stay their fury’s rage the while,
105And eke gain time, whose only help sufficeth
106Withouten war to vanquish rebel’s power.
107In the meanwhile, make you in readiness
108Such band of horsemen as ye may prepare;
109Horsemen, you know, are not the commons’ strength,
110But are the force and store of noble men,
111Whereby the unchosen and unarmed sort
112Of skilless rebels, whom none other power
113But number makes to be of dreadful force,
114With sudden brunt may quickly be oppress’d.
115And if this gentle mean of proffer’d grace
116With stubborn hearts cannot so far avail
117As to assuage their desperate courages,
118Then do I wish such slaughter to be made
119And present age and eke posterity
120May be adread with horror of revenge
121That justly then shall on these rebels fall.
122This is, my lords, the sum of mine advice.

clotyn

123Neither this case admits debate at large,
124And thought it did, this speech that hath been said
125Hath well abridg’d the tale I would have told.
126Fully with Eubulus do I consent
127In all that he hath said, and if the same
128To you, my lords, may seem for best advice,
129I wish that it should straight be put in ure.

mandud

130My lords, then let us presently depart
131And follow this that liketh us so well.

[Exeunt all but Fergus.]

fergus

132If ever time to gain a kingdom here
133Were offer’d man, now it is offer’d me:
134The realm is reft both of their King and Queen;
135The offspring of the Prince is slain and dead;
136No issue now remains, the heir unknown;
137The people are in arms and mutinies;
138The nobles they are busi’d how to cease
139The great rebellious tumults and uproars;
140And Britain land, now desert, left alone
141Amid these broils uncertain where to rest,
142Offers herself unto that noble heart
143That will or dare pursue to bear her crown.
144Shall I that am the Duke of Albany,
145Descended from that line of noble blood
146Which hath so long flourish’d in worthy fame
147Of valiant hearts, such as in noble breasts
148Of right should rest above the baser sort,
149Refuse to venture life to win a crown?
150Whom shall I find enemies that will withstand
151My fact herein, if I attempt by arms
152To seek the same now in these times of broil?
153These duke’s power can hardly well appease
154The people that already are in arms,
155But if perhaps my force be once in field,
156Is not my strength in power above the best
157Of all these lords now left in Britain land?
158And though they should match me with power of men,
159Yet doubtful is the chance of battles joined:
160If victors of the field we may depart,
161Ours is the sceptre then of great Britain;
162If slain amid the plain this body lie,
163Mine enemies yet shall not deny me this,
164But that I died giving the noble charge
165To hazard life for conquest of a crown.
166Forthwith therefore will I in post depart
167To Albany, and raise in armour there
168All power I can. And here my secret friends
169By secret practice shall solicit still
170To seek to win to me the people’s hearts.

[Exit.]

ACT V SCENE 2

[Enter Eubulus.]

eubulus

171O Jove, how are these people’s hearts abus’d!
172What blind fury thus headlong carries them!
173That thought so many books, so many rolls
174Of ancient time, record what grievous plagues
175Light on these rebels aye, and though so oft
176Their ears have heard their aged fathers tell
177What just reward these traitors still receive,
178Yea, though themselves have seen deep death and blood,
179By strangling cord and slaughter of the sword
180To such assign’d, yet can they not beware,
181Yet cannot stay their lewd rebellious hands,
182But suffering, lo, foul treason to distain
183Their wretched minds, forget their loyal heart,
184Reject all truth and rise against their prince.
185A ruthful case, that those whom duty’s bond,
186Whom grafted law by nature, truth and faith
187Bound to preserve their country and their King,
188Born to defend their commonwealth and prince,
189Even they should give consent thus to subvert
190Thee, Britain land, and from thy womb should spring,
191O native soil, those that will needs destroy
192And ruin thee and eke themselves in fine.
193For lo, when once the dukes had offer’d grace
194Of pardon sweet, the multitude, misled
195By traitorous fraud of their ungracious heads,
196One sort, that saw the dangerous success
197Of stubborn standing in rebellious war,
198And knew the difference of prince’s power
199From headless number of tumultuous routs,
200Whom common country’s care and private fear
201Taught to repent the error of their rage,
202Laid hands upon the captains of their band,
203And brought them bound unto the mighty dukes.
204Another sort, not trusting yet so well
205The truth of pardon, or mistrusting more
206Their own offence than that they could conceive
207Such hope of pardon for so foul misdeed,
208Or of that they their captains could not yield
209(Who, fearing to be yielded, fled before),
210Stale home by silence of the secret night.
211The third unhappy and enraged sort
212Of desperate hearts, who, stain’d in princes’ blood,
213From traitorous furor could not be withdrawn
214By love, by law, by grace, ne yet by fear,
215By proffer’d life, ne yet by threaten’d death,
216With minds hopeless of life, dreadless of death,
217Careless of country, and aweless of God,
218Stood bent to fight, as Furies did them move,
219With violent death to close their traitorous life.
220These all by power of horsemen were oppress’d
221And with revenging sword slain in the field,
222Or with the strangling cord hang’d on the trees
223Where yet their carrion carcasses do preach
224The fruits that rebels reap of their uproars
225And of the murder of their sacred prince.

[Looking offstage] But lo, where do approach the noble dukes

226By whom these tumults have been thus appeas’d.

[Enter Clotyn, Mandud, Gwenard and Arostus.]

clotyn

227I think the world will now at lenght beware
228And fear to put on arms against their prince.

mandud

229If not, those traitorous hearts that dare rebel
230Let them behold the wide and hugy fields
231With blood and bodies spread of rebels slain,
232The lofty trees cloth’d with the corpses dead
233That strangled with the cord do hang thereon.

arostus

234A just reward, such as all times before
235Have ever lotted to those wretched folks.

gwenard

236But what means he that cometh here so fast?

[Enter Nuntius.]

237nuntius My lords, as duty and my truth doth move
238And of my country work a care in me,
239That, if the spending of my breath avail’d
240To do the service that my heart desires,
241I would not shun to embrace a present death:
242So have I now in that wherein I thought
243My travail might perform some good effect,
244Ventur’d my life to bring these tidings here.
245Fergus, the mighty Duke of Albany,
246Is now in arms and lodgeth in the field
247With twenty thousand men. Hither he bends
248His speedy march, and mind to invade the crown.
249Daily he gathereth strength, and spreads abroad
250That to this realm no certain heir remains;
251That Britain land is left without a guide;
252That he the sceptre seeks for nothing else
253But to preserve the people and the land,
254Which now remain as ship without a stern.
255Lo, this is that which I have here to say.

clotyn

256Is this his faith? And shall he falsely thus
257Abuse the vantage of unhappy times?
258O wretched land, if his outrageous pride,
259His cruel and untemper’d wilfulness,
260His deep dissembling show of false pretence,
261Should once attain the crown of Britain land!
262Let us, my lords, with timely force resist
263The new attempt of this our common foe,
264As me would quench the flames of common fire.

mandud

265Though we remain without a certain prince
266To wield the realm or guide the wand’ring rule,
267Yet now the common mother of us all,
268Our native land, our country, that contains
269Our wives, children, kindred, ourselves and all
270That ever is or may be dear to man,
271Cries unto us to help ourselves and her.
272Let us advance our powers to repress
273This growing foe of all our liberties.

gwenard

274Yea, let us so, my lords, with hasty speed:
275And ye, O gods, send us the welcome death
276To shed our blood in field, and leave us not
277In loathsome life to linger out our days,
278To see the hugy heaps of these unhaps
279That now roll down upon the wretched land
280Where empty place of princely governance,
281No certain stay now left of doubtless heir.
282Thus leave this guideless realm an open prey
283To endless storms and waste of civil war.

arostus

284That ye, my lords, do so agree in one
285To save tour country from the violent reign
286And wrongfully usurped tyranny
287Of him that threatens conquest of you all,
288To save your realm, and in this realm yourselves,
289From foreign thraldom of so proud a prince,
290Much do I praise; and I beseach the gods
291With happy honour to requite it you.
292But, O my lords, sith now the heaven’s wrath
293Hath reft this land the issue of their prince;
294Sith of the body of our late sovereign lord
295Remains no moe, since the young kings be slain;
296And of the title of descended crown
297Uncertainly the diverse minds do think
298Even of the learned sort, and more uncertainly
299Will partial fancy and affection deem,
300But most uncertainly will climbing pride
301And hope of reign withdraw to sundry parts
302The doubtful right and hopeful lust to reign.
303When once this noble service is achiev’d
304For Britain land, the mother of ye all;
305When once ye have with armed force repress’d
306The proud attempts of this Albanian prince
307That threatens thraldom to your native land;
308When ye shall vanquishers return from field
309And find the princely state an open prey
310To greedy lust and to usurping power;
311Then, them my lords, if ever kindly care
312Of ancient honour of your ancestors,
313Of present wealth and nobless of your stocks,
314Yea, of the lives and safety yet to come
315Of your dear wives, your children, and yourselves,
316Might move your noble hearts with gentle ruth;
317Then, then, have pity on the torn estate.
318Then help to salve the well-near hopeless sore,
319Which ye shall do, if ye yourselves withhold
320Then slaying knife from your own mother’s throat.
321Her shall you save, and you, and yours in her,
322If ye shall all with one assent forbear
323Once to lay hand or take unto yourselves
324The crown, by colour of pretended right,
325Or by what other means soever it be,
326Till first by common counsel of you all
327In parliament the regal diadem
328Be set in certain place of governance,
329In which your parliament, and in your choice,
330Prefer the right, my lords, without respect
331Of strength or friends, or whatsoever cause
332That may set forward any other’s part:
333For right will last, and wrong cannot endure.
334Right mean I his or hers, upon whose name
335The people rest by mean of native line,
336Or by the virtue of some former law
337Already made their title to advance.
338Such one, my lords, let be your chosen king;
339Such one so born within your native land;
340Such one prefer, and in no wise admit
341The heavy yoke of foreign governance:
342Let foreign titles yield to public wealth.
343And with that heart wherewith ye now prepare
344Thus to withstand the proud invading foe,
345With that same heart, my lords, keep out also
346Unnatural thraldom of [a] stranger’s reign,
347Ne suffer you against the rules of king
348Your motherland to serve a foreign prince.

eubulus

349Lo, here the end of Brutus’ royal line,
350And, lo, the entry to the woeful wrack
351And utter ruin of this noble realm;
352The royal King and eke his sons are slain;
353No ruler rests within the regal seat;
354The heir, to whom the sceptre longs, unknown;
355That to each force of foreign prince’s power,
356Whom vantage of our wretched state may move
357By sudden arms to gain so rich a realm
358And so the proud and greedy mind at home,
359Whom blinded lust to reign leads to aspire,
360Lo, Britain realm is left an open prey,
361A present spoil by conquest to ensue.
362Who seeth not now how many rising minds
363Do feed their thoughts with hope to reach a realm?
364And who will not by force attempt to win
365So great a gain, that hope persuades to have?
366A simple colour shall for title serve:
367Who wins the royal crown will want no right,
368Nor such as shall display by long descent
369A lineal race to prove him lawful king.
370In the meanwhile these civil arms shall rage
371And thus a thousand mischiefs shall unfold,
372And far and near spread thee, O Britain land:
373All right and law shall cease, and he that had
374Nothing today, tomorrow shall enjoy
375Great heaps of gold, and he that flow’d in wealth,
376Lo, he shall be bereft of life and all,
377And happiest he that then possesseth least.
378The wives shall suffer rape, the maids deflower’d,
379And children fatherless shall weep and wail;
380With fire and sword thy native folk shall perish,
381One kinsman shall bereave another’s life;
382The father shall unwitting slay the son,
383The son shall slay the sire and know it not,
384Women and maids the cruel soldier’s sword
385Shall pierce to death, and silly children, lo,
386That playing in the streets and fields are found
387By violent hand shall close their latter day.
388Whom shall the fierce and bloody soldier
389Reserve to life? Whom shall he spare from death?
390Even thou, O wretched mother, half alive,
391Thou shalt behold thy dear and only child
392Slain with the sword while he yet sucks thy breast!
393Lo, guiltless blood shall thus each where be shed:
394Thus shall the wasted soil yield forth no fruit,
395But dearth and famine shall possess the land.
396The towns shall be consum’d and burnt with fire,
397The peopled cities shall wax desolate,
398And thou, O Britain, whilom in renown,
399Whilom in wealth and fame, shalt thus be torn,
400Dismember’d thus, and thus be rent in twain,
401Thus wasted and defac’d, spoil’d and destroy’d!
402These be the fruits your civil wars will bring;
403Hereto it comes when kings will not consent
404To grave advice, but follow wilful will;
405This is the end, when in young princes’ hearts
406Flattery prevails and sage rede hath no place;
407These are the plagues, when murder is the mean
408To make new heirs unto the royal crown.
409Thus wreak the gods, when that the mother’s wrath
410Nought but the blood of her own child may suage;
411These mischiefs spring when rebels will arise,
412To work revenge and judge their prince’s fact;
413This, this ensues, when noble men do fail
414In loyal truth and subjects will be kings.
415And this doth grow when, lo, unto the prince,
416Whom death or sudden hap of life bereaves,
417No certain heir remains, such certain heir,
418As not all only is the rightful heir
419But to the realm is so made known to be,
420And truth thereby vested in subjects’ hearts,
421To owe faith there where right is known to rest.
422Alas, in parliament what hope can be,
423When is of parliament no hope at all?
424Which, though it be assembled by consent,
425Yet is not likely with consent to end,
426While each one for himself, or for his friend,
427Against his foe, shall travail what he may;
428While now the state, left open to the man
429That shall with greatest force invade the same,
430Shall fill ambitious minds with gaping hope:
431When will they once with yielding hearts agree?
432Or in the while, how shall the realm be us’d?
433No, no! Then parliament should have been holden,
434And certain heirs appointed to the crown
435To stay the title of establish’d right
436And in the people plant obedience
437While yet the prince did live, whose name and power
438By lawful summons and authority
439Might make a parliament to be of force
440And might have set the state in quiet stay.
441But now, O happy man, whom speedy death
442Deprives of life, ne is enforc’d to see
443These hugy mischiefs and these miseries,
444These civil wars, these murders and these wrongs.
445Of justice yet must Jove in fine restore
446This noble crown unto the lawful heir,
447For right will always live and rise at length,
448Bur wrong can never take deep root to lat.

[Exeunt.]
