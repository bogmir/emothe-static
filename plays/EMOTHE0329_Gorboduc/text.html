Thomas Sackville

Gorboduc

First the music of violins began to play, during which came in upon the stage six wild men clothed in leaves. Of whom the first bare in his neck a faggot of small sticks, which they all both severally and together assayed with all their strengths to break, but it could not be broken by them. At the length one of them plucked out one of the sticks and brake it. And the rest, plucking out all the other sticks one after another, did easily break them, the same being severed, which being conjoined they had before attempted in vain. After they had this done, they departed the stage, and the music ceased. Hereby was signified that a state knit in unity doth continue strong against all force, but being divided is easily destroyed. As befell upon Duke Gorboduc, dividing his land to his two sons which he before held in monarchy, and upon the dissension of the brethren to whom it was divided.

ACT I SCENE I

Enter Videna and Ferrex.

videna

449The silent night, that brings the quiet pause
450From painful travails of the weary day,
451Prolongs my carefull thoughts, and makes me blame
452The slow Aurore, that so for love or shame
453Doth long delay to show her blushing face:
454And now the day renews my griefull plaint.

ferrex

455My gracious lady and my mother dear,
456Pardon my grief for your so grieved mind,
457To ask what cause tormenteth so your heart.

videna

458So great a wrong, and so unjust despite,
459Without all cause, against all course of kind!

ferrex

460Such causeless wrong and so unjust despite,
461May have redress, or at the least, revenge.

videna

462Neither, my son: such is the froward will,
463The person such, such my mishap and thine.

ferrex

464Mine know I none, but grief for your distress.

videna

465Yes; mine for thine, my son. A father? No.
466In kind a father, not in kindliness.

ferrex

467My father? Why? I know nothing at all,
468Wherein I have misdone unto his grace.

videna

469Therefore the more unkind to thee and me.
470For I have ever borne and bear to thee,
471He, griev’d thereat, is not content alone
472To spoil me of thy sight, my chiefest joy,
473But thee of thy birthright and heritage
474Causeless, unkindly, and in wrongful wise,
475Against all law and right, he will bereave.
476Half of his kingdom he will give away.

ferrex

477To whom?

videna

To whom? Even to Porrex, his younger son,
478Whose growing pride I do so sore suspect,
479That, being rais’d to equal rule with thee,
480Methinks I see his envious heart to swell,
481Fill’d with disdain and with ambitious hope.
482The end the gods do know, whose altars I
483Full oft have made in vain of cattle slain
484To send the sacred smoke to Heaven’s throne,
485For thee, my son, if things do so succeed,
486As now my jealous mind misdeemeth sore.

ferrex

487Madam, leave care and carefull plaint for me:
488Just hath my father been to every wight.
489His first unjustice he will not extend
490To me, I trust, that give no cause thereof:
491My brother’s pride shall hurt himself, not me.

videna

492So grant the gods! But yet thy father so
493Hath firmly fixed his unmoved mind
494That plaints and prayers can no whit avail,
495For those have I assay’d, but even this day
496He will endeavour to procure assent
497Of all his council to his fond device.

ferrex

498Their ancestors from race to race have borne
499True faith to my forefathers and their seed:
500I trust they eke will bear the like to me.

videna

501There resteth all. But if they fail thereof,
502And if the end bring forth an ill success,
503On them and theirs the mischief shall befall,
504And so I pray the gods requite it them,
505And so they will, for so is wont to be!
506When lords, and trusted rulers under kings,
507To please the present fancy of the prince
508With wrong transpose the course of governance,
509Murders, mischief, or civil sword at length,
510Or mutual treason, or a just revenge,
511When right succeeding line returns again,
512By Jove’s just judgement and deserved wrath,
513Brings them to cruel and reproachful death,
514And roots their names and kindreds from the earth.

ferrex

515Mother, content you; you shall see the end.

videna

516The end? Thy end I fear: Jove end me first!

Exeunt.

ACT I SCENE 2

[Gorboduc’s court]

[Enter Gorboduc, Arostus, Philander, Eubulus, Chorus, Attendants etc.]

gorboduc

517My lords, whose grave advice and faithful aid
518Have long upheld my honour and my realm,
519And brought me to this age from tender years,
520Guiding so great estate with great renown,
521Now more importeth me than erst to use
522Your faith and wisdom, whereby yet I reign:
523That when my death my life and rule shall cease,
524The kingdom yet may with unbroken course
525Have certain prince, by whose undoubted right
526Your wealth and peace may stand in quiet stay;
527And eke that they whom nature hath prepar’d
528In time to take my place in princely seat –
529While in their father’s time their pliant youth
530Yields to the frame of skilful governance–
531May so be taught and train’d in noble arts,
532As what their fathers which have reigned before
533Have with great fame derived down to them,
534With honour they may leave unto their seed
535And not be thought for their unworthy life,
536And for their lawless swerving out of kind,
537Worthy to lose what law and kind them gave;
538But that they may preserve the common peace,
539The cause that first began and still maintains
540The lineal course of kings’ inheritance.
541For me, for mine, for you, and for the state,
542Whereof both I and you have charge and care,
543Thus do I mean to use your wonted faith
544To me and mine, and to your native land.
545My lords, be plain without all wry respect
546Or poisonous craft to speak in pleasing wise,
547Lest as the blame of ill-succeeding thing
548Shall light on you, so light the harms also.

arostus

549Your good acceptance so, most noble King,
550Of such our faithfulness as heretofore
551We have employed in duties to your grace
552And to this realm, whose worthy head you are,
553Well proves that neither you mistrust at all,
554Nor we shall need in boasting wise to show
555Our truth to you, nor yet out wakeful care
556For you, four yours, and for our native land.
557Wherefore, O King, I speak as one for all –
558Sith all as one do bear you equal faith –
559Doubt not to use our counsels and our aids,
560Whose honours, goods and lives are whole avow’d
561To serve, to aid, and to defend your grace.

gorboduc

562My lords, I thank you all. This is the case:
563Ye know the gods, who have the sovereig care
564For kings, for kingdoms, and for commonweals,
565Gave me two sons in my more lusty age,
566Who now in my decaying years are grown
567Well towards riper state of strength,
568To take in hand some greater princely charge.
569As yet they live and spend their hopeful days
570With me and with their mother here in court.
571Their age now asketh other place and trade,
572And mine also doth ask another change,
573Theirs to more travail, mine to greater ease.
574When fatal death shall end my mortal life,
575My purpose is to leave unto them twain
576The realm divided into two sundry parts:
577The one Ferrex, mine elder son, shall have;
578The other shall the younger, Porrex, rule.
579That both my purpose may more firmly stand,
580And eke that they may better rule their charge,
581I mean forthwith to place them in the same,
582That in my life they may both learn to rule
583And I may joy to see their ruling well.
584This is in sum what I would have ye weigh:
585First, whether ye allow my whole device
586And think it good for me, for them, for you,
587And for our country, mother of us all;
588And if ye like it, and allow it well,
589Then for their guiding and their governance,
590Show forth such means of circumstance
591As ye think meet to be both known and kept.
592Lo, this is all; now tell me your advice.

arostus

593And this is much, and asketh great advice.
594But for my part, my sovereign lord and King,
595This do I think; your Majesty doth know
596How under you in justice and in peace
597Great wealth and honour long we have enjoy’d,
598So as we cannot seem with greedy minds
599To wish for change of prince or governance.
600But if we like your purpose and device,
601Our liking must be deemed to proceed
602Of rightful reason and of heedful care,
603Not for ourselves, but for the common state,
604Sith our own state doth need no better change.
605I think in all as erst your grace hath said:
606First, when you shall unload your aged mind
607Of heavy care and troubles manifold
608And lay the same upon my lords your sons,
609Whose growing years may bear the burden long
610(And long I pray the gods to grant it so)
611And in your life while you shall so behold
612Their rule, their virtues, and their noble deeds,
613Such as their kind behighteth to us all,
614Great be the profits that shall grow thereof.
615Your age in quiet shall the longer last;
616Your lasting age shall be their longer stay,
617For cares of kings, that rule as you have rul’d
618For public wealth and not for private joy,
619Do waste man’s life and hasten crooked age,
620With furrow’d face and with enfeebl’d limbs,
621To draw on creeping death a swifter pace:
622They two, yet young, shall bear the parted reign
623With greater ease than one, now old, alone,
624Can wield the whole, for whom much harder is
625With lessen’d strength the double weight to bear.
626Your eye, your counsel, and the grace regard
627Of father, yea, of such a father’s name –
628Now at beginning of their whole success,
629Shall bridle so their force of youthful heats,
630And so restrain the rage of insolence
631Which most assails the young and noble minds,
632And so shall guide and train in temper’d stay
633Their yet green-bending wits with reverent awe,
634As now inur’d with virtues at the first,
635Custom, O King, shall bring delightfulness.
636By use of virtue, vice shall grow in hate;
637But if you so dispose it that the day
638Which ends your life shall first begin their reign,
639Great is the peril what will be the end.
640When such beginning of such liberties –
641Void of such stays as in your life do lie –
642Shall leave them free to random of their will,
643An open prey to traitorous flattery,
644The greastest pestilence of noble youth.
645Which peril shall be past, if in your life
646Their temper’d youth with aged father’s awe
647Be brought in ure of skilful stayedness,
648And in your life their lives disposed so
649Shall length your noble life in joyfulness.
650Thus think I that your grace hath wisely thought,
651And that your tender care of common weal
652Hath bred this thought, so to divide your land,
653And plant your sons to bear the present rule,
654While you yet live to see their ruling well,
655That you may longer live by joy therein.
656What further means behoveful are and meet
657At greater leisure may your grace devise,
658When all have said, and when we agreed
659If this be best to part the realm in twain
660And place your sons in present government,
661Whereof, as I have plainly said my mind,
662So would I hear the rest of all my lords.

philander

663In part I think as hath been said before;
664In part again my mind is otherwise.
665As for dividing of this realm in twain,
666And lotting out the same in equal parts
667To either of my lords, your grace’s sons,
668That think I best for this your realm’s behove,
669For profit and advancement of your sons,
670And for your comfort and your honour eke.
671But so to place them while your life do last;
672To yield to them your royal governance;
673To be above them only in the name
674Of father, not in kingly state also;
675I think not good for you, for them, nor us.
676This kingdom, since the bloody civil field
677Where Morgan slain did yield his conquered part
678Unto his cousin’s sword in Camberland,
679Containeth all that whilom did suffice
680Three noble sons of your forefather Brute:
681So your two sons it may suffice also,
682The moe the stronger, if they gree in one.
683The smaller compass that the realm doth hold,
684The easier is the sway thereof to wield,
685The nearer justice to the wronged poor,
686The smaller charge, and yet enough for one.
687And when the region is divided so,
688That brethren be the lords of either part,
689Such strength doth nature knit between them both,
690In sundry bodies by conjoined love,
691That not as two, but one of doubled force,
692Each is to other as a sure defence.
693The nobleness and glory of the one
694Doth sharp the courage of the other’s mind
695With virtuous envy to content for praise.
696And such an equalness hath nature made
697Between the brethren of one father’s seed,
698As an unkindly wrong it seems to be
699To throw the brother-subject under feet
700Of him whose peer he is by course of kind.
701And Nature, that did make this equalness,
702Oft so repineth at so great a wrong,
703That oft she raiseth up a grudging grief
704In younger brethren at the elder’s state,
705Whereby both towns and kingdoms have been raz’d
706And famous stocks of royal blood destroy’d.
707The brother, that should be the brother’s aid
708And have a wakeful care for his defence,
709Gapes for his death, and blames the lingering years
710That draw not forth his end with faster course;
711And, oft impatient of so long delays,
712With hateful slaughter he prevents the fates,
713And heaps a just reward for brother’s blood,
714With endless vengeance on his stock for aye.
715Such mischiefs here are wisely met withal,
716If equal state may nourish equal love,
717Where none hath cause to grudge at other’s good:
718But now the head to stoop beneath them both,
719Ne kind, ne reason, ne good order bears.
720And oft it hath been seen, where Nature’s course
721Hath been perverted in disolder’d wise,
722When fathers cease to know that they should rule,
723The children cease to know they should obey,
724And often overkindly tenderness
725Is mother of unkindly stubbornness.
726I speak not this in envy or reproach
727As if I grudg’d the glory of your sons,
728Whose honour I beseech the gods increase,
729Nor yet as if I thought there did remain
730So filthy cankers in their noble breasts,
731Whom I esteem –which is their greatest praise–
732Undoubted children of so good a King.
733Only I mean to show by certain rules
734Which kind hath graft within the mind of man
735That Nature hath her order and her course,
736Which, being broken, doth corrupt the state
737Of minds and things, even in the best of all.
738My lord, your sons may learn to rule of you;
739Your own example in your noble court
740Is fittest guider of their youthful years.
741If you desire to see some present joy
742By sight of their well ruling in your life,
743See them obey, so shall you see them rule.
744Whoso obeyeth not with humbleness
745Will rule with outrage and with insolence.
746Long may they rule, I do beseech the gods;
747Long may they learn, ere they begin to rule!
748If kind and fates would suffer, I would wish
749Them aged princes and immortal kings.
750Wherefore, most noble King, I well assent
751Between your sons that you divide your realm,
752And as in kind, so match them in degree;
753But while the gods prolong your royal life,
754Prolong your reign; for thereto live you here,
755And therefore have the gods so long forborne
756To join you to themselves that still you might
757Be prince and father of our commonweal.
758They, when they see your children ripe to rule,
759Will make them room, and will remove you hence,
760That yours, in right ensuing of your life,
761May rightly honour your immortal name.

eubulus

762Your wonted true regard of faithful hearts
763Makes me, O King, the bolder to presume,
764To speak what I conceive within my breast,
765Although the same do not agree at all
766With that which other here my lords have said,
767Nor which yourself have seemed best to like.
768Pardon I crave, and that my words be deem’d
769To flow from hearty zeal unto your grace,
770And to the safety of your commonweal.
771To part your realm unto my lords, your sons,
772I think not good for you, ne yet for them,
773But worst of all for this our native land.
774Within one land, one single rules is best:
775Divided reigns do make divides hearts,
776But peace preserves the country and the prince.
777Such is in man the greedy mind to reign,
778So great is his desire to climb aloft,
779In worldly stage the stateliest parts to bear,
780That faith and justice and all kindly love
781Do yield unto desire of sovereignty,
782Where equal state doth raise an equal hope
783To win the thing that either would attain.
784Your grace remembereth how in passed years
785The mighty Brute, first prince of all this land,
786Possess’d the same and rul’d it well in one;
787He, thinking that the compass did suffice
788For his three sons three kingdoms eke to make,
789Cut it in three, as you would now in twain.
790But how much British blood hath since been spilt
791To join again the sunder’d unity!
792What princes slain before their timely hour,
793What waste of towns and people in the land,
794What treasons heap’d on murders and on spoils
795Whose just revenge even yet is scarcely ceas’d,
796Ruthful remembrance is yet raw in mind –
797The gods forbid the like to change again!
798And you, O King, give not the cause thereof:
799Mu lord Ferrex, your elder son, perhaps
800(Whom kind and custom gives a rightful hope
801To be your heir and to succeed your reign)
802Shall think that he doth suffer greater wrong
803Than he perchance will bear, if power serve;
804Porrex, the younger, so uprais’d in state,
805Perhaps in courage will be rais’d also.
806If flattery, then, which fails not to assail
807The tender minds of yet unskilful youth,
808In one shall kindle and increase disdain
809And envy in the other’s heart inflame,
810This fire shall waste their love, their lives, their land,
811And ruthful ruin shall destroy them both.
812I wish not this, O King, so to befall,
813But fear the thing that I do most abhor.
814Give no beginning to so dreadful end:
815Keep them in order and obedience,
816And let them both by now obeying you
817Learn such behaviour as beseems their state:
818The elder, mildness in his governance,
819The younger, a yielding contentedness.
820And keep them near unto your presence still,
821That they, restrained by the awe of you,
822May live in compass of well-temper’d stay
823And pass the perils of their youthful years.
824Your aged life draws on to feebler time,
825Wherein you shall less able be to bear
826The travails that in youth you have sustain’d,
827Both in your person’s and your realm’s defence;
828If planting now your sons in further parts,
829You send them further from your present reach,
830Less shall you know how they themselves demean.
831Traitorous corrupters of their pliant youth
832Shall have unspied a much more free access;
833And if ambition and inflam’d disdain
834Shall arm the one, the other, or them both,
835To civil war, or to usurping pride,
836Late shall you rue, that you ne reck’d before.
837Good is, I grant, of all to hope the best,
838But not to live still dreadless of the worst;
839So trust the one, that the other be foreseen.
840Arm not unskilfulness with princely power,
841But you that long have wisely rul’d the reins
842Of royalty within your noble realm,
843So hold them, while the gods for our avails
844Shall stretch the thread of your prolonged days:
845Too soon he clamb into the flaming car,
846Whose want of skill did set the earth on fire.
847Time and example of your noble grace
848Shall teach your sons both to obey and rule;
849When time hath taught them, time shall make the place,
850The place that now is full: and so, I pray,
851Long it remain, to confort of us all.

gorboduc

852I take your faithful hearts in thankful part,
853But sith I see no cause to draw my mind
854To fear the nature of my loving sons,
855Or to misdeem that envy or disdain
856Can there work hate where Nature planteth love,
857In one self purpose do I still abide.
858My love extendeth equally to both;
859My land sufficeth for them both also.
860Humber shall part the marches of their realms,
861The southern part the elder shall possess,
862The northern shall Porrex, the younger, rule;
863In quiet I will pass my aged days,
864Free from the travail and the painful cares
865That hasten age upon the worthiest kings.
866But lest the fraud (that ye do seem to fear)
867Of flattering tongues corrupt their tender youth
868And writhe them to the ways of youthful lust,
869To climbing pride, or to revenging hate,
870Or to neglecting of their carefull charge,
871Lewdly to live in wanton recklessness,
872Or to oppressing of the rightful cause,
873Or not to wreak the wrongs done to the poor,
874To tread down truth, or favour false deceit,
875I mean to join to either of my sons
876Someone of those whose long approved faith
877And wisdom tried may well assure my heart
878That mining fraud shall find no way to creep
879Into their fenced ears with grave advice.
880This is the end, and so I pray you all
881To bear my sons the love and loyalty
882That I have found within your faithful breasts.

arostus

883You, nor your sons, our sovereing lord, shall want
884Our faith and service while our lives do last.

[Exeunt all but the Chorus.]

chorus

885When settled stay doth hold the royal throne
886In steadfast place, by known and doubtless right,
887And chiefly when descent on one alone
888Makes single and unparted reign to light,
889Each change of course unjoints the whole estate,
890And yields it thrall to ruin by debate.
891The strength that, knit by fast accord in one
892Against all foreign power of mighty foes,
893Could of itself defend itself alone,
894Disjoined once, the former force doth lose:
895The sticks that sunder’d brake so soon in twain,
896In faggot bound attempted were in vain.
897Oft tender mind that leads the partial eye
898Of erring parents in their children’s love
899Destroys the wrongly loved child thereby:
900This doth the proud son of Apollo prove,
901Who, rashly set in chariot of his sire,
902Inflam’d the parched earth with heaven’s fire.
903And this great King, that doth divide his land,
904And change the course of his descending crown,
905And yields the rein into his children’s hand,
906From blissful state of joy and great renown,
907A mirror shall become to princes all,
908To learn to shun the cause of such a fall.

First the music of cornets began to play, during which came in upon the stage a king accompanied with a number of his nobility and gentlemen. And after he had placed himself in a chair of estate prepared for him, there came and kneeled before him a grave and aged gentleman and offered up a cup unto him of wine in a glass, which the king refused. After him comes a brave and lusty young gentleman and presents the king with a cup of gold filled with poison, which the king accepted, and, drinking the same, immediately fell down dead upon the stage, and so was carried thence away by his lords and gentlemen, and then the music ceased. Hereby was signified that as glass by nature holdeth no poison, but is clear and may easily be seen through, ne boweth by any art, so a faithful counsellor holdeth no treason, but is plain and open, ne yieldeth to any undiscreet affection, but giveth wholesome counsel, which the ill-advised prince refuseth. The delighful gold filled with poison betokeneth flattery, which under fair seeming of pleasant words beareth deadly poison, which destroyeth the prince that receiveth it, as befell in the two brethren Ferrex and Porrex, who, refusing the wholesome advice of grave counsellors, credited these young parasites and brought to themselves death and destruction thereby.

ACT II SCENE I

[Ferrex’s court]

Enter Ferrex, Hermon and Dordan.]

ferrex

909I marvel much what reason led the King
910My father, thus without all my desert,
911To reave me half the kingdom, which by course
912Of law and nature should remain to me.

hermon

913If you with stubborn and untamed pride
914Had stood against him in rebelling wise,
915Or if with grudging mind you had envied
916So slow a sliding of his aged years,
917Or sought before your time to haste the course
918Of fatal death upon his royal head,
919Or stain’d your stock with murder of your kin,
920Some face of reason might perhaps have seem’d
921To yield some likely cause to spoil ye thus.

ferrex

922The wreakful gods pour on my cursed head
923Eternal plagues and never-dying woes;
924The hellish prince adjudge my damned ghost
925To Tantale’s thirst, or proud Ixion’s wheel,
926Or cruel gripe to gnaw my growing heart,
927To during torments and unquenched flames,
928If ever I conceiv’d so foul a thought,
929To wish his end of life, or yet of reign!

dordan

930Ne yet your father, O most noble Prince,
931Did ever think so foul a thing of you:
932For he, with more than father’s tender love,
933While yet the fates do lend him life to rule
934(Who long might live to see your ruling well),
935To you, my lord, and to his other son,
936Lo, he resigns his realm and royalty,
937Which never would so wise a prince have done
938If he had once misdeem’d that in your heart
939There ever lodged so unkind a thought.
940But tender love, my lord, and settled trust
941Of your good nature and your noble mind
942Made him to place you thus in royal throne,
943And now to give you half his realm to guide –
944Yea, and that half which in abounding store
945Of things that serve to make a wealthy realm,
946In stately cities, and in fruitful soil,
947In temperate breathing of the milder Heaven,
948In things of needful use, which friendly sea
949Transports by traffic from the foreign parts,
950In flowing wealth, in honour and in force,
951Doth pass the double value of the part
952That Porrex hath allotted to his reign.
953Such is your case, such is your father’s love.

ferrex

954Ah love, my friends? Love wrongs not whom he loves.

dordan

955Ne yet he wrongeth you, that giveth you
956So large a reign, ere that the course of time
957Bring you to kingdom by descended right,
958Which time perhaps might end your time before.

ferrex

959Is this no wrong, say you, to reave from me
960My native right of half so great a realm,
961And thus to match his younger son with me
962In equal power, and in as great degree?
963Yea, and what son? The son whose swelling pride
964Would never yield one point of reverence,
965When I the elder and apparent heir
966Stood in the likelihood to possess the whole;
967Yea, and that son which from his childish age
968Envieth mine honour and doth hate my life.
969What will he now do, when his pride, his rage,
970The mindful malice of his grudging heart,
971Is arm’d with force, with wealth, and kingly state?

hermon

972Was this not wrong, yea, ill-advised wrong,
973To give so mad a man so sharp a sword,
974To so great peril of so great mishap,
975Wide open thus to set so large a way?

dordan

976Alas, my lord, what griefull thing is this,
977That of your brother you can think so ill?
978I never saw him utter likely sign
979Whereby a man might see or once misdeem
980Such hate of you, ne such unyielding pride.
981Ill is their counsel, shameful be their end,
982That, raising such mistrustful fear in you,
983Sowing the seed of such unkindly hate,
984Travail by treason to destroy you both.
985Wise is your brother, and of noble hope,
986Worthy to wield a large and mighty realm;

First the drums and flutes began to sound, during which there came forth upon the stage a company of harquebusiers and of armed men, all in order of battle. These, after their pieces discharged and that the armed men had three times marched about the stage, departed, and then the drums and flutes did cease. Hereby was signified tumults, rebellions, arms and civil wars to follow, as fell in the realm of Great Britain, which by the space of fifty years and more continued in civil war between the nobility after the death King Gorboduc, and of his issues, for want of certain limitation, in the succession of the crown, till the time of Dunwallo Molmutius, who reduced the land to monarchy.

3The mother she hath dy’d her cruel hands

9Even yet they cease not, carried on with rage,
10In their rebellious routs, to threaten still
11A new bloodshed unto the prince’s kin,
12To slay them all, and to uproot the race
13Both of the King and Queen, so are they mov’d
14With Porrex’ death wherein they falsely charge
15The guiltless King without desert at all,

17And eke the Queen.

gwenard

18To work revenge upon their prince’s fact?
19Admit the worst that may – as sure in this
20The deed was foul, the Queen to slay her son –
21Shall yet the subjects seek to take the sword,
22Arise against his lord, and slay his King?
23O wretched state, where those rebellious hearts
24Are not rent out even from their living breasts,
25And with the body thrown unto the fowls
26As carrion food, for terror of the rest!

fergus

27There can no punishment be thought too great
28For this so grievous crime; let speed therefore
29Be used therein, for it behoveth so.

eubulus

30Ye all, my lords, I see, consent in one,
31And I as one consent with ye in all;
32I hold it more than need with sharpest law
33To punish this tumultuous bloody rage.
34For nothing more may shake the common state
35Than sufferance of uproars without redress;
36Whereby how some kingdoms of mighty power
37After great conquests made, and flourishing
38In fame and wealth, have been to ruin brought:
39I pray to Jove that we may rather wail
40Such hap in them than witness in ourselves.
41Eke fully with the Duke my mind agrees
42That no cause serves, whereby the subject may
43Call to account the doings of his prince;
44Much less in blood by sword to work revenge
45No more than may the hand cut off the head:
46In act nor speech, no, not in secret thought
47The subject may rebel against his lord,
48Or judge of him that sits in Caesar’s seat,
49With grudging mind to damn those he mislikes.
50Though kings forget to govern as they ought,
51Yet subjects must obey as they are bound.
52But now, my lords, before ye farther wade
53Or spend your speech what sharp revenge shall fall
54By justice’ plague on these rebellious wights,
55Methinks ye rather should first search the way
56By which in time the rage of this uproar
57Might be repress’d, and these great tumults ceas’d.
58Even yet the life of Britain land doth hang
59In traitors’ balance of unequal weight:
60Think not, my lords, the death of Gorboduc,
61Not yet Videna’s blood, will cease their rage.
62Even our own lives, our wives and children dear,
63Our country, dearest of all, in danger stands,
64Now to be spoil’d, now, now made desolate,
65And by ourselves a conquest to ensue.
66For give once sway unto the people’s lusts
67To rush forth on, and stay them not in time,
68And as the stream that rolleth down the hill,
69So will they headlong run with raging thoughts
70From blood to blood, from mischief unto moe,
71To ruin of the realm, themselves and all;
72So giddy are the common people’s minds,
73So glad of change, more wavering than the sea.

75What hugy number is assembled still;
76For though the traitorous fact for which they rose
77Be wrought and done, yet lodge they still in field,
78So that how far their furies yet will stretch
79Great cause we have to dread. That we may seek
80By present battle to repress their power,
81Speed must we use to levy force therefore;
82For either they forthwith will mischief work,
83Or their rebellious roars forthwith will cease:
84These violent things may have no lasting long.

86Persuade by gentle speech, and offer grace
87With gift of pardon, save unto the chief;
88And that upon condition that forthwith
89They yield the captains of their enterprise
90To bear such guerdon of their traitorous fact
91As may be both due vengeance to themselves
92And wholesome terror to posterity.

94That now are holden with desire of home,
95Wearied in field with cold of winter’s nights,
96And some, no doubt, stricken with dread of law.

98The captains to mistrust the multitude,
99Whose safety bids them to betray their heads,
100And so much more because the rascal routs
101In things of great and perilous attempts
102Are never trusty to the noble race.

104We shall both stay their fury’s rage the while,
105And eke gain time, whose only help sufficeth
106Withouten war to vanquish rebel’s power.

108Such band of horsemen as ye may prepare;
109Horsemen, you know, are not the commons’ strength,
110But are the force and store of noble men,
111Whereby the unchosen and unarmed sort
112Of skilless rebels, whom none other power
113But number makes to be of dreadful force,
114With sudden brunt may quickly be oppress’d.
115And if this gentle mean of proffer’d grace
116With stubborn hearts cannot so far avail
117As to assuage their desperate courages,
118Then do I wish such slaughter to be made
119And present age and eke posterity
120May be adread with horror of revenge
121That justly then shall on these rebels fall.

172What blind fury thus headlong carries them!
173That thought so many books, so many rolls
174Of ancient time, record what grievous plagues
175Light on these rebels aye, and though so oft
176Their ears have heard their aged fathers tell
177What just reward these traitors still receive,
178Yea, though themselves have seen deep death and blood,
179By strangling cord and slaughter of the sword
180To such assign’d, yet can they not beware,
181Yet cannot stay their lewd rebellious hands,
182But suffering, lo, foul treason to distain
183Their wretched minds, forget their loyal heart,
184Reject all truth and rise against their prince.

186Whom grafted law by nature, truth and faith
187Bound to preserve their country and their King,
188Born to defend their commonwealth and prince,
189Even they should give consent thus to subvert
190Thee, Britain land, and from thy womb should spring,
191O native soil, those that will needs destroy
192And ruin thee and eke themselves in fine.
193For lo, when once the dukes had offer’d grace
194Of pardon sweet, the multitude, misled
195By traitorous fraud of their ungracious heads,
196One sort, that saw the dangerous success
197Of stubborn standing in rebellious war,
198And knew the difference of prince’s power
199From headless number of tumultuous routs,
200Whom common country’s care and private fear
201Taught to repent the error of their rage,
202Laid hands upon the captains of their band,
203And brought them bound unto the mighty dukes.
204Another sort, not trusting yet so well
205The truth of pardon, or mistrusting more
206Their own offence than that they could conceive
207Such hope of pardon for so foul misdeed,
208Or of that they their captains could not yield
209(Who, fearing to be yielded, fled before),
210Stale home by silence of the secret night.
211The third unhappy and enraged sort
212Of desperate hearts, who, stain’d in princes’ blood,
213From traitorous furor could not be withdrawn
214By love, by law, by grace, ne yet by fear,
215By proffer’d life, ne yet by threaten’d death,
216With minds hopeless of life, dreadless of death,
217Careless of country, and aweless of God,

219With violent death to close their traitorous life.

223Where yet their carrion carcasses do preach

248His speedy march, and mind to invade the crown.
249Daily he gathereth strength, and spreads abroad
250That to this realm no certain heir remains;
251That Britain land is left without a guide;
252That he the sceptre seeks for nothing else
253But to preserve the people and the land,
254Which now remain as ship without a stern.
255Lo, this is that which I have here to say.

clotyn

256Is this his faith? And shall he falsely thus
257Abuse the vantage of unhappy times?
258O wretched land, if his outrageous pride,
259His cruel and untemper’d wilfulness,
260His deep dissembling show of false pretence,
261Should once attain the crown of Britain land!
262Let us, my lords, with timely force resist
263The new attempt of this our common foe,
264As me would quench the flames of common fire.

mandud

265Though we remain without a certain prince
266To wield the realm or guide the wand’ring rule,
267Yet now the common mother of us all,
268Our native land, our country, that contains
269Our wives, children, kindred, ourselves and all
270That ever is or may be dear to man,
271Cries unto us to help ourselves and her.
272Let us advance our powers to repress
273This growing foe of all our liberties.

gwenard

274Yea, let us so, my lords, with hasty speed:
275And ye, O gods, send us the welcome death
276To shed our blood in field, and leave us not
277In loathsome life to linger out our days,
278To see the hugy heaps of these unhaps
279That now roll down upon the wretched land
280Where empty place of princely governance,
281No certain stay now left of doubtless heir.
282Thus leave this guideless realm an open prey

arostus

284That ye, my lords, do so agree in one
285To save tour country from the violent reign
286And wrongfully usurped tyranny
287Of him that threatens conquest of you all,
288To save your realm, and in this realm yourselves,
289From foreign thraldom of so proud a prince,
290Much do I praise; and I beseach the gods
291With happy honour to requite it you.
292But, O my lords, sith now the heaven’s wrath
293Hath reft this land the issue of their prince;
294Sith of the body of our late sovereign lord
295Remains no moe, since the young kings be slain;
296And of the title of descended crown
297Uncertainly the diverse minds do think
298Even of the learned sort, and more uncertainly
299Will partial fancy and affection deem,
300But most uncertainly will climbing pride
301And hope of reign withdraw to sundry parts
302The doubtful right and hopeful lust to reign.
303When once this noble service is achiev’d
304For Britain land, the mother of ye all;
305When once ye have with armed force repress’d
306The proud attempts of this Albanian prince
307That threatens thraldom to your native land;
308When ye shall vanquishers return from field
309And find the princely state an open prey
310To greedy lust and to usurping power;
311Then, them my lords, if ever kindly care
312Of ancient honour of your ancestors,
313Of present wealth and nobless of your stocks,
314Yea, of the lives and safety yet to come
315Of your dear wives, your children, and yourselves,
316Might move your noble hearts with gentle ruth;
317Then, then, have pity on the torn estate.
318Then help to salve the well-near hopeless sore,
319Which ye shall do, if ye yourselves withhold
320Then slaying knife from your own mother’s throat.
321Her shall you save, and you, and yours in her,
322If ye shall all with one assent forbear
323Once to lay hand or take unto yourselves
324The crown, by colour of pretended right,
325Or by what other means soever it be,
326Till first by common counsel of you all
327In parliament the regal diadem
328Be set in certain place of governance,
329In which your parliament, and in your choice,
330Prefer the right, my lords, without respect
331Of strength or friends, or whatsoever cause
332That may set forward any other’s part:
333For right will last, and wrong cannot endure.
334Right mean I his or hers, upon whose name
335The people rest by mean of native line,
336Or by the virtue of some former law
337Already made their title to advance.
338Such one, my lords, let be your chosen king;
339Such one so born within your native land;
340Such one prefer, and in no wise admit
341The heavy yoke of foreign governance:
342Let foreign titles yield to public wealth.
343And with that heart wherewith ye now prepare
344Thus to withstand the proud invading foe,
345With that same heart, my lords, keep out also
346Unnatural thraldom of [a] stranger’s reign,
347Ne suffer you against the rules of king
348Your motherland to serve a foreign prince.

eubulus

349Lo, here the end of Brutus’ royal line,
350And, lo, the entry to the woeful wrack
351And utter ruin of this noble realm;
352The royal King and eke his sons are slain;
353No ruler rests within the regal seat;
354The heir, to whom the sceptre longs, unknown;
355That to each force of foreign prince’s power,
356Whom vantage of our wretched state may move
357By sudden arms to gain so rich a realm
358And so the proud and greedy mind at home,
359Whom blinded lust to reign leads to aspire,
360Lo, Britain realm is left an open prey,
361A present spoil by conquest to ensue.
362Who seeth not now how many rising minds
363Do feed their thoughts with hope to reach a realm?
364And who will not by force attempt to win
365So great a gain, that hope persuades to have?
366A simple colour shall for title serve:
367Who wins the royal crown will want no right,
368Nor such as shall display by long descent
369A lineal race to prove him lawful king.
370In the meanwhile these civil arms shall rage
371And thus a thousand mischiefs shall unfold,
372And far and near spread thee, O Britain land:
373All right and law shall cease, and he that had
374Nothing today, tomorrow shall enjoy
375Great heaps of gold, and he that flow’d in wealth,
376Lo, he shall be bereft of life and all,
377And happiest he that then possesseth least.
378The wives shall suffer rape, the maids deflower’d,
379And children fatherless shall weep and wail;
380With fire and sword thy native folk shall perish,
381One kinsman shall bereave another’s life;
382The father shall unwitting slay the son,
383The son shall slay the sire and know it not,
384Women and maids the cruel soldier’s sword
385Shall pierce to death, and silly children, lo,
386That playing in the streets and fields are found
387By violent hand shall close their latter day.
388Whom shall the fierce and bloody soldier
389Reserve to life? Whom shall he spare from death?
390Even thou, O wretched mother, half alive,
391Thou shalt behold thy dear and only child
392Slain with the sword while he yet sucks thy breast!
393Lo, guiltless blood shall thus each where be shed:
394Thus shall the wasted soil yield forth no fruit,
395But dearth and famine shall possess the land.
396The towns shall be consum’d and burnt with fire,
397The peopled cities shall wax desolate,
398And thou, O Britain, whilom in renown,
399Whilom in wealth and fame, shalt thus be torn,
400Dismember’d thus, and thus be rent in twain,
401Thus wasted and defac’d, spoil’d and destroy’d!
402These be the fruits your civil wars will bring;
403Hereto it comes when kings will not consent
404To grave advice, but follow wilful will;
405This is the end, when in young princes’ hearts
406Flattery prevails and sage rede hath no place;
407These are the plagues, when murder is the mean
408To make new heirs unto the royal crown.
409Thus wreak the gods, when that the mother’s wrath
410Nought but the blood of her own child may suage;
411These mischiefs spring when rebels will arise,
412To work revenge and judge their prince’s fact;
413This, this ensues, when noble men do fail
414In loyal truth and subjects will be kings.
415And this doth grow when, lo, unto the prince,
416Whom death or sudden hap of life bereaves,
417No certain heir remains, such certain heir,
418As not all only is the rightful heir
419But to the realm is so made known to be,
420And truth thereby vested in subjects’ hearts,
421To owe faith there where right is known to rest.
422Alas, in parliament what hope can be,
423When is of parliament no hope at all?
424Which, though it be assembled by consent,
425Yet is not likely with consent to end,
426While each one for himself, or for his friend,
427Against his foe, shall travail what he may;
428While now the state, left open to the man
429That shall with greatest force invade the same,
430Shall fill ambitious minds with gaping hope:
431When will they once with yielding hearts agree?
432Or in the while, how shall the realm be us’d?
433No, no! Then parliament should have been holden,
434And certain heirs appointed to the crown
435To stay the title of establish’d right
436And in the people plant obedience
437While yet the prince did live, whose name and power
438By lawful summons and authority
439Might make a parliament to be of force
440And might have set the state in quiet stay.
441But now, O happy man, whom speedy death
442Deprives of life, ne is enforc’d to see
443These hugy mischiefs and these miseries,
444These civil wars, these murders and these wrongs.
445Of justice yet must Jove in fine restore
446This noble crown unto the lawful heir,
447For right will always live and rise at length,
