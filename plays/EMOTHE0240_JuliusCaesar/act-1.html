Act I

[1.1]

Enter Flavius, Murellus, and certain commoners over the stage.

FLAVIUS

1Hence! Home, you idle creatures! Get you home!
2Is this a holiday? What, know you not,
3Being mechanical, you ought not walk
4Upon a laboring day, without the sign
5Of your profession? Speak, what trade art thou?

CARPENTER

Why, sir, a carpenter.

MURELLUS

6Where is thy leather apron and thy rule?
7What dost thou with thy best apparel on?
8You, sir, what trade are you?

COBBLER

Truly, sir, in respect of a fine workman, I am but as you would say, a cobbler.

MURELLUS

But what trade art thou? Answer me directly.

COBBLER

A trade, sir, that I hope I may use, with a safe conscience, which is indeed sir, a mender of bad soles.

FLAVIUS

What trade, thou knave? Thou naughty knave, what trade?

COBBLER

Nay, I beseech you, sir, be not out with me. Yet if you be out, sir, I can mend you.

MURELLUS

What mean'st thou by that? Mend me, thou saucy fellow?

COBBLER

Why, sir, cobble you.

FLAVIUS

Thou art a cobbler, art thou?

COBBLER

Truly sir, all that I live by is with the awl. I meddle with no tradesman's matters, nor women's matters; but withal I am indeed, sir, a surgeon to old shoes: when they are in great danger, I recover them. As proper men as ever trod upon neat's leather, have gone upon my handiwork.

FLAVIUS

9But wherefore art not in thy shop today?
10Why dost thou lead these men about the streets?

COBBLER

Truly sir, to wear out their shoes, to get myself into more work. But indeed, sir, we make holiday to see Caesar, and to rejoice in his triumph.

MURELLUS

11Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home?
12What tributaries follow him to Rome,
13To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels?
14You blocks! You stones! You worse than senseless things!
15O you hard hearts! You cruel men of Rome!
16Knew you not Pompey many a time and oft?
17Have you climbed up to walls and battlements,
18To towers and windows, yea, to chimney tops,
19Your infants in your arms, and there have sat
20The livelong day, with patient expectation,
21To see great Pompey pass the streets of Rome?
22And when you saw his chariot but appear,
23Have you not made an universal shout,
24That Tiber trembled underneath her banks
25To hear the replication of your sounds,
26Made in her concave shores?
27And do you now put on your best attire?
28And do you now cull out a holiday?
29And do you now strew flowers in his way
30That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood?
31Begone!
32Run to your houses! Fall upon your knees!
33Pray to the gods to intermit the plague
34That needs must light on this ingratitude!

FLAVIUS

35Go, go, good countrymen, and for this fault
36Assemble all the poor men of your sort;
37Draw them to Tiber banks, and weep your tears
38Into the channel, till the lowest stream
39Do kiss the most exalted shores of all.

Exeunt all the Commoners.

40See whe'er their basest mettle be not moved:
41They vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness.
42Go you down that way towards the Capitol;
43This way will I. Disrobe the images,
44If you do find them decked with ceremonies.

MURELLUS

45May we do so?
46You know it is the Feast of Lupercal.

FLAVIUS

47It is no matter. Let no images
48Be hung with Caesar's trophies. I'll about,
49And drive away the vulgar from the streets;
50So do you too, where you perceive them thick.
51These growing feathers, plucked from Caesar's wing,
52Will make him fly an ordinary pitch,
53Who else would soar above the view of men,
54And keep us all in servile fearfulness.

Exeunt

[1.2]

Enter Caesar, Antony for the course, Calpurnia, Portia, Decius, Cicero, Brutus, Cassius, Casca, a Soothsayer; after them Murellus and Flavius.

CAESAR

55Calpurnia.

CASCA

Calpurnia. Peace ho! Caesar speaks.

CAESAR

Calpurnia. Peace ho! Caesar speaks. Calpurnia!

CALPURNIA

56Here, my Lord.

CAESAR

57Stand you directly in Antonio's way
58When he doth run his course. Antonio!

ANTONY

59Caesar, my Lord?

CAESAR

60Forget not in your speed, Antonio,
61To touch Calpurnia, for our elders say,
62The barren touchèd in this holy chase,
63Shake off their sterile curse.

ANTONY

Shake off their sterile curse. I shall remember.
64When Caesar says, "Do this," it is performed.

CAESAR

65Set on, and leave no ceremony out.

[Sennet]

SOOTHSAYER

66Caesar!

CAESAR

67Ha? Who calls?

CASCA

68Bid every noise be still! Peace yet again!

CAESAR

69Who is it in the press that calls on me?
70I hear a tongue shriller than all the music
71Cry "Caesar." Speak! Caesar is turned to hear.

SOOTHSAYER

72Beware the ides of March.

CAESAR

Beware the ides of March. What man is that?

BRUTUS

73A soothsayer bids you beware the ides of March.

CAESAR

74Set him before me. Let me see his face.

CASSIUS

75Fellow, come from the throng! Look upon Caesar.

CAESAR

76What say'st thou to me now? Speak once again.

SOOTHSAYER

77Beware the ides of March.

CAESAR

78He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass.

Sennet.

Exeunt [all but Brutus and Cassius].

CASSIUS

79Will you go see the order of the course?

BRUTUS

80Not I.

CASSIUS

81I pray you, do.

BRUTUS

82I am not gamesome. I do lack some part
83Of that quick spirit that is in Antony.
84Let me not hinder, Cassius, your desires.
85I'll leave you.

CASSIUS

86Brutus, I do observe you now of late.
87I have not from your eyes that gentleness
88And show of love as I was wont to have.
89You bear too stubborn and too strange a hand
90Over your friend that loves you.

BRUTUS

Over your friend that loves you. Cassius,
91Be not deceived. If I have veiled my look,
92I turn the trouble of my countenance
93Merely upon myself. Vexed I am
94Of late with passions of some difference,
95Conceptions only proper to myself,
96Which give some soil, perhaps, to my behaviors;
97But let not therefore my good friends be grieved,
98Among which number, Cassius, be you one,
99Nor construe any further my neglect
100Than that poor Brutus with himself at war,
101Forgets the shows of love to other men.

CASSIUS

102Then Brutus, I have much mistook your passion,
103By means whereof, this breast of mine hath buried
104Thoughts of great value, worthy cogitations.
105Tell me, good Brutus, can you see your face?

BRUTUS

106No, Cassius, for the eye sees not itself
107But by reflection, by some other things.

CASSIUS

108'Tis just.
109And it is very much lamented, Brutus,
110That you have no such mirrors as will turn
111Your hidden worthiness into your eye,
112That you might see your shadow. I have heard,
113Where many of the best respect in Rome,
114Except immortal Caesar, speaking of Brutus
115And groaning underneath this age's yoke,
116Have wished that noble Brutus had his eyes.

BRUTUS

117Into what dangers, would you lead me, Cassius,
118That you would have me seek into myself
119For that which is not in me?

CASSIUS

120Therefore, good Brutus, be prepared to hear;
121And since you know you cannot see yourself
122So well as by reflection, I, your glass,
123Will modestly discover to yourself
124That of yourself which you yet know not of.
125And be not jealous on me, gentle Brutus.
126Were I a common laughter, or did use
127To stale with ordinary oaths my love
128To every new protester; if you know
129That I do fawn on men and hug them hard
130And after scandal them; or if you know
131That I profess myself in banqueting
132To all the rout, then hold me dangerous.

Flourish, and shout.

BRUTUS

133What means this shouting? I do fear the people
134Choose Caesar for their king.

CASSIUS

Choose Caesar for their king. Ay, do you fear it?
135Then must I think you would not have it so.

BRUTUS

136I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well.
137But wherefore do you hold me here so long?
138What is it that you would impart to me?
139If it be ought toward the general good,
140Set honor in one eye and death i'th'other,
141And I will look on both indifferently.
142For let the gods so speed me, as I love
143The name of honor more than I fear death.

CASSIUS

144I know that virtue to be in you, Brutus,
145As well as I do know your outward favor.
146Well, honor is the subject of my story.
147I cannot tell what you and other men
148Think of this life, but for my single self,
149I had as lief not be, as live to be
150In awe of such a thing as I myself.
151I was born free as Caesar, so were you;
152We both have fed as well; and we can both
153Endure the winter's cold, as well as he.
154For once, upon a raw and gusty day,
155The troubled Tiber, chafing with her shores,
156Caesar said to me, "Dar'st thou, Cassius, now
157Leap in with me into this angry flood
158And swim to yonder point?" Upon the word,
159Accoutred as I was, I plungèd in,
160And bade him follow. So indeed he did.
161The torrent roared, and we did buffet it
162With lusty sinews, throwing it aside,
163And stemming it with hearts of controversy.
164But ere we could arrive the point proposed,
165Caesar cried, "Help me, Cassius, or I sink."
166I, as Aeneas, our great ancestor,
167Did from the flames of Troy upon his shoulder
168The old Anchises bear, so, from the waves of Tiber
169Did I the tirèd Caesar. And this man
170Is now become a god, and Cassius is
171A wretched creature and must bend his body
172If Caesar carelessly but nod on him.
173He had a fever when he was in Spain,
174And when the fit was on him, I did mark
175How he did shake. 'Tis true, this god did shake.
176His coward lips did from their color fly,
177And that same eye, whose bend doth awe the world,
178Did lose his luster. I did hear him groan.
179Ay, and that tongue of his, that bade the Romans
180Mark him and write his speeches in their books,
181"Alas," it cried, "Give me some drink, Titinius,"
182As a sick girl. Ye gods, it doth amaze me
183A man of such a feeble temper should
184So get the start of the majestic world
185And bear the palm alone.

Shout. Flourish.

BRUTUS

186Another general shout?
187I do believe that these applauses are
188For some new honors that are heaped on Caesar.

CASSIUS

189Why man, he doth bestride the narrow world
190Like a colossus, and we petty men
191Walk under his huge legs, and peep about
192To find ourselves dishonorable graves.
193Men at some time are masters of their fates.
194The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars,
195But in ourselves that we are underlings.
196"Brutus" and "Caesar." What should be in that "Caesar"?
197Why should that name be sounded more than yours?
198Write them together: yours is as fair a name.
199Sound them: it doth become the mouth as well.
200Weigh them: it is as heavy. Conjure with 'em:
201"Brutus" will start a spirit as soon as "Caesar."
202Now in the names of all the gods at once,
203Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed
204That he is grown so great? Age, thou art shamed!
205Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!
206When went there by an age since the great flood,
207But it was famed with more than with one man?
208When could they say, till now, that talked of Rome,
209That her wide walks encompassed but one man?
210Now is it Rome indeed, and room enough
211When there is in it but one only man.
212Oh, you and I have heard our fathers say
213There was a Brutus once that would have brooked
214Th'eternal devil to keep his state in Rome
215As easily as a king.

BRUTUS

216That you do love me, I am nothing jealous.
217What you would work me to, I have some aim.
218How I have thought of this and of these times
219I shall recount hereafter. For this present,
220I would not, so with love I might entreat you,
221Be any further moved. What you have said,
222I will consider; what you have to say,
223I will with patience hear, and find a time
224Both meet to hear and answer such high things.
225Till then, my noble friend, chew upon this:
226Brutus had rather be a villager
227Than to repute himself a son of Rome
228Under these hard conditions as this time
229Is like to lay upon us.

CASSIUS

230I am glad that my weak words
231Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus.

Enter Caesar and his train.

BRUTUS

232The games are done, and Caesar is returning.

CASSIUS

233As they pass by, pluck Casca by the sleeve,
234And he will, after his sour fashion, tell you
235What hath proceeded worthy note today.

BRUTUS

236I will do so. But look you, Cassius,
237The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow,
238And all the rest look like a chidden train:
239Calpurnia's cheek is pale, and Cicero
240Looks with such ferret and such fiery eyes
241As we have seen him in the Capitol,
242Being crossed in conference by some senators.

CASSIUS

243Casca will tell us what the matter is.

CAESAR

244Antonio!

ANTONY

245Caesar?

CAESAR

246Let me have men about me that are fat,
247Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep o'nights.
248Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look.
249He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous.

ANTONY

250Fear him not Caesar, he's not dangerous,
251He is a noble Roman, and well given.

CAESAR

252Would he were fatter! But I fear him not.
253Yet if my name were liable to fear,
254I do not know the man I should avoid
255So soon as that spare Cassius. He reads much,
256He is a great observer, and he looks
257Quite through the deeds of men. He loves no plays,
258As thou dost, Antony; he hears no music;
259Seldom he smiles, and smiles in such a sort
260As if he mocked himself and scorned his spirit
261That could be moved to smile at anything.
262Such men as he be never at heart's ease
263Whiles they behold a greater than themselves,
264And therefore are they very dangerous.
265I rather tell thee what is to be feared
266Than what I fear, for always I am Caesar.
267Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf,
268And tell me truly, what thou think'st of him.

Sennet.

Exeunt Caesar and [all] his train [but Casca].

CASCA

You pulled me by the cloak. Would you speak with me?

BRUTUS

269Ay, Casca. Tell us what hath chanced today,
270That Caesar looks so sad.

CASCA

Why, you were with him, were you not?

BRUTUS

271I should not then ask Casca what had chanced.

CASCA

Why, there was a crown offered him; and being offered him, he put it by with the back of his hand, thus, and then the people fell a-shouting.

BRUTUS

What was the second noise for?

CASCA

Why, for that too.

CASSIUS

272They shouted thrice. What was the last cry for?

CASCA

Why, for that too.

BRUTUS

Was the crown offered him thrice?

CASCA

Ay, marry, was't, and he put it by thrice, every time gentler than other; and at every putting by, mine honest neighbors shouted.

CASSIUS

273Who offered him the crown?

CASCA

Why, Antony.

BRUTUS

274Tell us the manner of it, gentle Casca.

CASCA

I can as well be hanged as tell the manner of it. It was mere foolery. I did not mark it. I saw Mark Antony offered him a crown; yet 'twas not a crown, neither; 'twas one of these coronets. And as I told you, he put it by once; but for all that, to my thinking, he would fain have had it. Then he offered it to him again; then he put it by again. But to my thinking, he was very loath to lay his fingers off it. And then he offered it the third time; he put it the third time by. And still as he refused it, the rabblement hooted, and clapped their chapped hands, and threw up their sweaty nightcaps, and uttered such a deal of stinking breath, because Caesar refused the crown, that it had almost choked Caesar, for he swooned, and fell down at it. And for mine own part, I durst not laugh, for fear of opening my lips and receiving the bad air.

CASSIUS

275But soft, I pray you: what, did Caesar swoon?

CASCA

He fell down in the marketplace and foamed at mouth, and was speechless.

BRUTUS

276'Tis very like. He hath the falling sickness.

CASSIUS

277No, Caesar hath it not, but you and I,
278And honest Casca: we have the falling sickness.

CASCA

I know not what you mean by that, but I am sure Caesar fell down. If the tag-rag people did not clap him, and hiss him, according as he pleased, and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theater, I am no true man.

BRUTUS

279What said he, when he came unto himself?

CASCA

Marry, before he fell down, when he perceived the common herd was glad he refused the crown, he plucked me ope his doublet, and offered them his throat to cut. And I had been a man of any occupation, if I would not have taken him at a word, I would I might go to hell among the rogues, and so he fell. When he came to himself again, he said, if he had done or said anything amiss, he desired their worships to think it was his infirmity. Three or four wenches where I stood, cried, "Alas, good soul," and forgave him with all their hearts. But there's no heed to be taken of them. If Caesar had stabbed their mothers, they would have done no less.

BRUTUS

280And after that, he came thus sad away.

CASCA

Ay.

CASSIUS

Did Cicero say anything?

CASCA

Ay, he spoke Greek.

CASSIUS

To what effect?

CASCA

Nay, and I tell you that, I'll ne'er look you i'th'face again. But those that understood him, smiled at one another and shook their heads. But for mine own part, it was Greek to me. I could tell you more news too: Murellus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence. Fare you well. There was more foolery yet, if I could remember it.

CASSIUS

Will you sup with me tonight, Casca?

CASCA

No, I am promised forth.

CASSIUS

Will you dine with me tomorrow?

CASCA

Ay, if I be alive, and your mind hold, and your dinner worth the eating.

CASSIUS

Good, I will expect you.

CASCA

Do so. Farewell both.

Exit.

BRUTUS

281What a blunt fellow is this grown to be?
282He was quick mettle when he went to school.

CASSIUS

283So is he now, in execution
284Of any bold, or noble enterprise,
285However he puts on this tardy form.
286This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit,
287Which gives men stomach to digest his words
288With better appetite.

BRUTUS

With better appetite. And so it is.
289For this time I will leave you:
290Tomorrow, if you please to speak with me,
291I will come home to you, or if you will,
292Come home to me, and I will wait for you.

CASSIUS

293I will do so. Till then, think of the world.

Exit Brutus.

294Well, Brutus, thou art noble, yet I see
295Thy honorable mettle may be wrought
296From that it is disposed. Therefore it is meet
297That noble minds keep ever with their likes,
298For who so firm that cannot be seduced?
299Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus.
300If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius,
301He should not humor me. I will this night
302In several hands in at his windows throw,
303As if they came from several citizens,
304Writings, all tending to the great opinion
305That Rome holds of his name, wherein obscurely
306Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at.
307And after this, let Caesar seat him sure,
308For we will shake him, or worse days endure.

Exit.

[1.3]

Thunder and lightning. Enter Casca and Cicero.

CICERO

309Good even, Casca. Brought you Caesar home?
310Why are you breathless, and why stare you so?

CASCA

311Are not you moved, when all the sway of earth
312Shakes like a thing unfirm? O Cicero,
313I have seen tempests when the scolding winds
314Have rived the knotty oaks, and I have seen
315Th'ambitious ocean swell, and rage, and foam,
316To be exalted with the threatening clouds,
317But never till tonight, never till now,
318Did I go through a tempest dropping fire.
319Either there is a civil strife in heaven,
320Or else the world, too saucy with the gods,
321Incenses them to send destruction.

CICERO

322Why, saw you any thing more wonderful?

CASCA

323A common slave, you know him well by sight,
324Held up his left hand, which did flame and burn
325Like twenty torches joined; and yet his hand,
326Not sensible of fire, remained unscorched.
327Besides—I ha'not since put up my sword—
328Against the Capitol I met a lion,
329Who glazed upon me and went surly by,
330Without annoying me. And there were drawn
331Upon a heap a hundred ghastly women,
332Transformèd with their fear, who swore they saw
333Men all in fire walk up and down the streets.
334And yesterday, the bird of night did sit
335Even at noonday upon the marketplace,
336Hooting and shrieking. When these prodigies
337Do so conjointly meet, let not men say,
338"These are their reasons, they are natural,"
339For I believe they are portentous things
340Unto the climate that they point upon.

CICERO

341Indeed, it is a strange disposèd time.
342But men may construe things after their fashion
343Clean from the purpose of the things themselves.
344Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?

CASCA

345He doth, for he did bid Antonio
346Send word to you he would be there tomorrow.

CICERO

347Good night then, Casca. This disturbèd sky
348Is not to walk in.

CASCA

Is not to walk in. Farewell Cicero.

Exit Cicero.

Enter Cassius.

CASSIUS

349Who's there?

CASCA

Who's there? A Roman.

CASSIUS

Who's there? A Roman. Casca, by your voice.

CASCA

350Your ear is good. Cassius, what night is this?

CASSIUS

351A very pleasing night to honest men.

CASCA

352Who ever knew the heavens menace so?

CASSIUS

353Those that have known the earth so full of faults.
354For my part, I have walked about the streets,
355Submitting me unto the perilous night,
356And thus unbracèd, Casca, as you see,
357Have bared my bosom to the thunder-stone,
358And when the cross blue lightning seemed to open
359The breast of heaven, I did present myself
360Even in the aim and very flash of it.

CASCA

361But wherefore did you so much tempt the heavens?
362It is the part of men to fear and tremble,
363When the most mighty gods by tokens send
364Such dreadful heralds to astonish us.

CASSIUS

365You are dull, Casca, And those sparks of life
366That should be in a Roman you do want,
367Or else you use not. You look pale, and gaze,
368And put on fear, and cast yourself in wonder,
369To see the strange impatience of the heavens.
370But if you would consider the true cause
371Why all these fires, why all these gliding ghosts,
372Why birds and beasts from quality and kind,
373Why old men, fools, and children calculate,
374Why all these things change from their ordinance,
375Their natures and preformèd faculties
376To monstrous quality—why, you shall find
377That heaven hath infused them with these spirits
378To make them instruments of fear and warning
379Unto some monstrous state.
380Now could I, Casca, name to thee a man,
381Most like this dreadful night,
382That thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars,
383As doth the lion in the Capitol,
384A man no mightier than thyself or me
385In personal action, yet prodigious grown
386And fearful, as these strange eruptions are.

CASCA

387'Tis Caesar that you mean. Is it not, Cassius?

CASSIUS

388Let it be who it is, for Romans now
389Have thews and limbs like to their ancestors.
390But woe the while, our fathers' minds are dead,
391And we are governed with our mothers' spirits.
392Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish.

CASCA

393Indeed, they say, the senators tomorrow
394Mean to establish Caesar as a king,
395And he shall wear his crown by sea and land
396In every place, save here in Italy.

CASSIUS

397I know where I will wear this dagger then:
398Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius.
399Therein, ye gods, you make the weak most strong,
400Therein, ye gods, you tyrants do defeat.
401Nor stony tower, nor walls of beaten brass,
402Nor airless dungeon, nor strong links of iron
403Can be retentive to the strength of spirit,
404But life, being weary of these worldly bars,
405Never lacks power to dismiss itself.
406If I know this, know all the world besides,
407That part of tyranny that I do bear
408Thunder still. Thunder still. I can shake off at pleasure.

CASCA

Thunder still. Thunder still. I can shake off at pleasure. So can I:

Thunder still.

409So every bondman in his own hand bears
410The power to cancel his captivity.

CASSIUS

411And why should Caesar be a tyrant then?
412Poor man, I know he would not be a wolf,
413But that he sees the Romans are but sheep;
414He were no lion, were not Romans hinds.
415Those that with haste will make a mighty fire
416Begin it with weak straws. What trash is Rome,
417What rubbish and what offal, when it serves
418For the base matter to illuminate
419So vile a thing as Caesar! But, O grief,
420Where hast thou led me? I perhaps speak this
421Before a willing bondman; then I know
422My answer must be made. But I am armed,
423And dangers are to me indifferent.

CASCA

424You speak to Casca, and to such a man
425That is no fleering tell-tale. Hold, my hand.
426Be factious for redress of all these griefs,
427And I will set this foot of mine as far
428As who goes farthest.

CASSIUS

As who goes farthest. There's a bargain made.
429Now know you, Casca, I have moved already
430Some certain of the noblest-minded Romans
431To undergo with me an enterprise
432Of honorable dangerous consequence,
433And I do know by this, they stay for me
434In Pompey's porch. For now, this fearful night,
435There is no stir or walking in the streets,
436And the complexion of the element
437In favor's like the work we have in hand:
438Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible.

Enter Cinna.

CASCA

439Stand close awhile, for here comes one in haste.

CASSIUS

440'Tis Cinna; I do know him by his gait;
441He is a friend. Cinna, where haste you so?

CINNA

442To find out you. Who's that, Metellus Cimber?

CASSIUS

443No, it is Casca, one incorporate
444To our attempts. Am I not stayed for, Cinna?

CINNA

445I am glad on't. What a fearful night is this?
446There's two or three of us have seen strange sights.

CASSIUS

447Am I not stayed for? Tell me.

CINNA

448Yes, you are. O Cassius, if you could
449But win the noble Brutus to our party—

CASSIUS

450Be you content. Good Cinna, take this paper,
451And look you lay it in the praetor's chair,
452Where Brutus may but find it. And throw this
453In at his window. Set this up with wax
454Upon old Brutus' statue. All this done,
455Repair to Pompey's porch, where you shall find us.
456Is Decius Brutus and Trebonius there?

CINNA

457All but Metellus Cimber, and he's gone
458To seek you at your house. Well, I will hie,
459And so bestow these papers as you bade me.

CASSIUS

460That done, repair to Pompey's theater.

Exit Cinna.

461Come, Casca, you and I will yet, ere day,
462See Brutus at his house. Three parts of him
463Is ours already, and the man entire
464Upon the next encounter yields him ours.

CASCA

465Oh, he sits high in all the people's hearts,
466And that which would appear offense in us,
467His countenance, like richest alchemy,
468Will change to virtue and to worthiness.

CASSIUS

469Him and his worth and our great need of him
470You have right well conceited. Let us go,
471For it is after midnight, and ere day,
472We will awake him and be sure of him.

Exeunt.
