Act III

[3.1]

Flourish.

Enter Caesar, Brutus, Cassius, Casca, Decius, Metellus, Trebonius, Cinna, Antony, Lepidus, Artemidorus, Publius, [Popilius Lena,] and the Soothsayer.

CAESAR

991The ides of March are come.

SOOTHSAYER

992Ay, Caesar, but not gone.

ARTEMIDORUS

993Hail, Caesar! Read this schedule.

DECIUS

994Trebonius doth desire you to o'erread,
995At your best leisure, this his humble suit.

ARTEMIDORUS

996O Caesar, read mine first, for mine's a suit
997That touches Caesar nearer. Read it, great Caesar.

CAESAR

998What touches us ourself shall be last served.

ARTEMIDORUS

999Delay not Caesar, read it instantly!

CAESAR

1000What, is the fellow mad?

PUBLIUS

What, is the fellow mad? Sirrah, give place.

CASSIUS

1001What, urge you your petitions in the street?
1002Come to the Capitol.

[Caesar and his train move away.]

POPILIUS

1003I wish your enterprise today may thrive.

CASSIUS

1004What enterprise, Popilius?

POPILIUS

What enterprise, Popilius? Fare you well.

[moves toward Caesar]

BRUTUS

1005What said Popilius Lena?

CASSIUS

1006He wished today our enterprise might thrive:
1007I fear our purpose is discoverèd.

BRUTUS

1008Look how he makes to Caesar: mark him.

CASSIUS

1009Casca, be sudden, for we fear prevention.
1010Brutus, what shall be done? If this be known,
1011Cassius or Caesar never shall turn back,
1012For I will slay myself.

BRUTUS

For I will slay myself. Cassius, be constant.
1013Popilius Lena speaks not of our purposes,
1014For look he smiles, and Caesar doth not change.

CASSIUS

1015Trebonius knows his time, for look you, Brutus,
1016He draws Mark Antony out of the way.

[Exeunt Antony and Trebonius.]

DECIUS

1017Where is Metellus Cimber? Let him go,
1018And presently prefer his suit to Caesar.

BRUTUS

1019He is addressed. Press near, and second him.

CINNA

1020Casca, you are the first that rears your hand.

CAESAR

1021Are we all ready? What is now amiss,
1022That Caesar and his senate must redress?

METELLUS

1023Most high, most mighty, and most puissant Caesar!
1024Metellus Cimber throws before thy seat
1025An humble heart.

CAESAR

An humble heart. I must prevent thee, Cimber.
1026These couchings and these lowly courtesies
1027Might fire the blood of ordinary men,
1028And turn preordinance and first decree
1029Into the lane of children. Be not fond
1030To think that Caesar bears such rebel blood
1031That will be thawed from the true quality
1032With that which melteth fools—I mean sweet words,
1033Low-crookèd curtsies, and base spaniel fawning.
1034Thy brother by decree is banishèd.
1035If thou doest bend, and pray, and fawn for him,
1036I spurn thee like a cur out of my way!
1037Know, Caesar doth not wrong, nor without cause
1038Will he be satisfied.

METELLUS

1039Is there no voice more worthy than my own
1040To sound more sweetly in great Caesar's ear
1041For the repealing of my banished brother?

BRUTUS

1042I kiss thy hand, but not in flattery, Caesar,
1043Desiring thee, that Publius Cimber may
1044Have an immediate freedom of repeal.

CAESAR

1045What, Brutus?

CASSIUS

What, Brutus? Pardon, Caesar! Caesar, pardon!
1046As low as to thy foot doth Cassius fall
1047To beg enfranchisement for Publius Cimber.

CAESAR

1048I could be well moved, if I were as you.
1049If I could pray to move, prayers would move me.
1050But I am constant as the northern star,
1051Of whose true fixed and resting quality,
1052There is no fellow in the firmament.
1053The skies are painted with unnumbered sparks;
1054They are all fire, and every one doth shine;
1055But there's but one in all doth hold his place.
1056So in the world: 'tis furnished well with men,
1057And men are flesh and blood, and apprehensive;
1058Yet in the number I do know but one
1059That unassailable holds on his rank
1060Unshaked of motion, and that I am he,
1061Let me a little show it, even in this:
1062That I was constant Cimber should be banished,
1063And constant do remain to keep him so.

CINNA

1064O Caesar—

CAESAR

O Caesar— Hence! Wilt thou lift up Olympus?

DECIUS

1065Great Caesar—

CAESAR

Great Caesar— Doth not Brutus bootless kneel?

CASCA

1066Speak, hands, for me!

They stab Caesar.

CAESAR

1067Et tu Brutè? —Then fall, Caesar!

Dies

CINNA

1068Liberty! Freedom! Tyranny is dead!
1069Run hence! Proclaim! Cry it about the streets!

CASSIUS

1070Some to the common pulpits and cry out!
1071Liberty, freedom, and enfranchisement!

BRUTUS

1072People and senators, be not affrighted.
1073Fly not! Stand still! Ambition's debt is paid.

CASCA

1074Go to the pulpit, Brutus.

DECIUS

Go to the pulpit, Brutus. And Cassius too.

BRUTUS

1075Where's Publius?

CINNA

1076Here, quite confounded with this mutiny.

METELLUS

1077Stand fast together, lest some friend of Caesar's
1078Should chance—

BRUTUS

1079Talk not of standing. Publius, good cheer.
1080There is no harm intended to your person,
1081Nor to no Roman else. So tell them, Publius.

CASSIUS

1082And leave us, Publius, lest that the people
1083Rushing on us, should do your age some mischief.

BRUTUS

1084Do so, and let no man abide this deed
1085But we the doers.

Enter Trebonius.

CASSIUS

1086Where is Antony?

TREBONIUS

Where is Antony? Fled to his house amazed.
1087Men, wives, and children, stare, cry out, and run
1088As it were doomsday.

BRUTUS

As it were doomsday. Fates, we will know your pleasures.
1089That we shall die, we know. 'Tis but the time
1090And drawing days out that men stand upon.

CASCA

1091Why, he that cuts off twenty years of life
1092Cuts off so many years of fearing death.

BRUTUS

1093Grant that, and then is death a benefit.
1094So are we Caesar's friends, that have abridged
1095His time of fearing death. Stoop, Romans, stoop,
1096And let us bathe our hands in Caesars blood
1097Up to the elbows, and besmear our swords.
1098Then walk we forth, even to the marketplace,
1099And waving our red weapons o'er our heads,
1100Let's all cry, "Peace, freedom, and liberty!"

CASSIUS

1101Stoop then, and wash. How many ages hence
1102Shall this our lofty scene be acted o'er,
1103In states unborn and accents yet unknown!

BRUTUS

1104How many times shall Caesar bleed in sport,
1105That now on Pompey's basis lies along,
1106No worthier than the dust!

CASSIUS

No worthier than the dust! So oft as that shall be,
1107So often shall the knot of us be called
1108The men that gave their country liberty.

DECIUS

1109What, shall we forth?

CASSIUS

What, shall we forth? Ay, every man away.
1110Brutus shall lead, and we will grace his heels
1111With the most boldest and best hearts of Rome.

Enter a servant.

BRUTUS

1112Soft, who comes here? A friend of Antony's.

SERVANT

1113Thus, Brutus, did my master bid me kneel.
1114Thus did Mark Antony bid me fall down,
1115And being prostrate, thus he bade me say:
1116"Brutus is noble, wise, valiant, and honest;
1117Caesar was mighty, bold, royal, and loving.
1118Say I love Brutus, and I honor him;
1119Say I feared Caesar, honored him, and loved him.
1120If Brutus will vouchsafe that Antony
1121May safely come to him, and be resolved
1122How Caesar hath deserved to lie in death,
1123Mark Antony shall not love Caesar dead
1124So well as Brutus living, but will follow
1125The fortunes and affairs of noble Brutus
1126Thorough the hazards of this untrod state,
1127With all true faith."
1128So says my master Antony.

BRUTUS

1129Thy master is a wise and valiant Roman;
1130I never thought him worse.
1131Tell him, so please him come unto this place,
1132He shall be satisfied, and by my honor
1133Depart untouched.

SERVANT

Depart untouched. I'll fetch him presently.

Exit servant.

BRUTUS

1134I know that we shall have him well to friend.

CASSIUS

1135I wish we may, but yet have I a mind
1136That fears him much, and my misgiving still
1137Falls shrewdly to the purpose.

Enter Antony.

BRUTUS

1138But here comes Antony: Welcome, Mark Antony.

ANTONY

1139O mighty Caesar! Dost thou lie so low?
1140Are all thy conquests, glories, triumphs, spoils,
1141Shrunk to this little measure? Fare thee well.
1142I know not, gentlemen, what you intend,
1143Who else must be let blood, who else is rank;
1144If I myself, there is no hour so fit
1145As Caesar's death's hour, nor no instrument
1146Of half that worth as those your swords, made rich
1147With the most noble blood of all this world.
1148I do beseech ye, if you bear me hard,
1149Now, whilst your purpled hands do reek and smoke,
1150Fulfill your pleasure. Live a thousand years,
1151I shall not find myself so apt to die;
1152No place will please me so, no mean of death,
1153As here by Caesar, and by you cut off,
1154The choice and master spirits of this age.

BRUTUS

1155O Antony! Beg not your death of us.
1156Though now we must appear bloody and cruel,
1157As by our hands and this our present act
1158You see we do. Yet see you but our hands
1159And this, the bleeding business they have done.
1160Our hearts you see not; they are pitiful,
1161And pity to the general wrong of Rome—
1162As fire drives out fire, so pity, pity—
1163Hath done this deed on Caesar. For your part,
1164To you our swords have leaden points, Mark Antony.
1165Our arms in strength of malice, and our hearts
1166Of brothers' temper, do receive you in,
1167With all kind love, good thoughts, and reverence.

CASSIUS

1168Your voice shall be as strong as any man's,
1169In the disposing of new dignities.

BRUTUS

1170Only be patient, till we have appeased
1171The multitude, beside themselves with fear,
1172And then we will deliver you the cause
1173Why I, that did love Caesar when I struck him,
1174Have thus proceeded.

ANTONY

Have thus proceeded. I doubt not of your wisdom.
1175Let each man render me his bloody hand.
1176First, Marcus Brutus. will I shake with you;
1177Next, Caius Cassius, do I take your hand;
1178Now, Decius Brutus yours; now yours, Metellus;
1179Yours, Cinna; and my valiant Casca, yours;
1180Though last, not least in love, yours, good Trebonius.
1181Gentlemen all. Alas, what shall I say?
1182My credit now stands on such slippery ground,
1183That one of two bad ways you must conceit me:
1184Either a coward, or a flatterer.
1185That I did love thee, Caesar, oh, 'tis true!
1186If then thy spirit look upon us now,
1187Shall it not grieve thee dearer than thy death
1188To see thy Antony making his peace,
1189Shaking the bloody fingers of thy foes—
1190Most noble — in the presence of thy corpse?
1191Had I as many eyes as thou hast wounds,
1192Weeping as fast as they stream forth thy blood,
1193It would become me better, than to close
1194In terms of friendship with thine enemies.
1195Pardon me Julius! Here was't thou bayed, brave hart,
1196Here did'st thou fall, and here thy hunters stand
1197Signed in thy spoil and crimsoned in thy Lethe.
1198O world! Thou wast the forest to this hart,
1199And this indeed, O world, the heart of thee!
1200How like a deer, strucken by many princes,
1201Dost thou here lie!

CASSIUS

1202Mark Antony—

ANTONY

Mark Antony— Pardon me, Caius Cassius.
1203The enemies of Caesar shall say this;
1204Then in a friend it is cold modesty.

CASSIUS

1205I blame you not for praising Caesar so,
1206But what compact mean you to have with us?
1207Will you be pricked in number of our friends,
1208Or shall we on, and not depend on you?

ANTONY

1209Therefore I took your hands, but was indeed
1210Swayed from the point by looking down on Caesar.
1211Friends am I with you all, and love you all,
1212Upon this hope, that you shall give me reasons,
1213Why and wherein Caesar was dangerous.

BRUTUS

1214Or else were this a savage spectacle.
1215Our reasons are so full of good regard
1216That were you, Antony, the son of Caesar,
1217You should be satisfied.

ANTONY

You should be satisfied. That's all I seek,
1218And am moreover suitor that I may
1219Produce his body to the marketplace,
1220And in the pulpit, as becomes a friend,
1221Speak in the order of his funeral.

BRUTUS

1222You shall, Mark Antony.

CASSIUS

You shall, Mark Antony. Brutus, a word with you:
1223You know not what you do! Do not consent
1224That Antony speak in his funeral.
1225Know you how much the people may be moved
1226By that which he will utter?

BRUTUS

By that which he will utter? By your pardon:
1227I will myself into the pulpit first,
1228And show the reason of our Caesar's death.
1229What Antony shall speak, I will protest
1230He speaks by leave and by permission,
1231And that we are contented Caesar shall
1232Have all true rites and lawful ceremonies.
1233It shall advantage more than do us wrong.

CASSIUS

1234I know not what may fall. I like it not.

BRUTUS

1235Mark Antony, here, take you Caesar's body.
1236You shall not in your funeral speech blame us,
1237But speak all good you can devise of Caesar,
1238And say you do't by our permission.
1239Else shall you not have any hand at all
1240About his funeral. And you shall speak
1241In the same pulpit whereto I am going,
1242After my speech is ended.

ANTONY

After my speech is ended. Be it so.
1243I do desire no more.

BRUTUS

1244Prepare the body, then, and follow us.

[Exeunt all but] Antony.

ANTONY

1245O pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth,
1246That I am meek and gentle with these butchers!
1247Thou art the ruins of the noblest man
1248That ever livèd in the tide of times.
1249Woe to the hand that shed this costly blood!
1250Over thy wounds now do I prophesy,
1251Which like dumb mouths do ope their ruby lips,
1252To beg the voice and utterance of my tongue:
1253A curse shall light upon the limbs of men,
1254Domestic fury and fierce civil strife
1255Shall cumber all the parts of Italy;
1256Blood and destruction shall be so in use,
1257And dreadful objects so familiar,
1258That mothers shall but smile when they behold
1259Their infants quartered with the hands of war,
1260All pity choked with custom of fell deeds;
1261And Caesar's spirit ranging for revenge,
1262With Atè by his side come hot from hell,
1263Shall in these confines, with a monarch's voice,
1264Cry havoc and let slip the dogs of war,
1265That this foul deed shall smell above the earth
1266With carrion men, groaning for burial.

Enter Octavius' servant.

1267You serve Octavius Caesar, do you not?

SERVANT

1268I do, Mark Antony.

ANTONY

1269Caesar did write for him to come to Rome.

SERVANT

1270He did receive his letters and is coming,
1271And bid me say to you by word of mouth—
1272O Caesar!

ANTONY

1273Thy heart is big. Get thee apart and weep.
1274Passion I see is catching, for mine eyes,
1275Seeing those beads of sorrow stand in thine,
1276Begin to water. Is thy master coming?

SERVANT

1277He lies tonight within seven leagues of Rome.

ANTONY

1278Post back with speed, And tell him what hath chanced.
1279Here is a mourning Rome, a dangerous Rome,
1280No Rome of safety for Octavius yet.
1281Hie hence, and tell him so. Yet stay awhile;
1282Thou shalt not back till I have borne this corpse
1283Into the marketplace. There shall I try
1284In my oration how the people take
1285The cruel issue of these bloody men,
1286According to the which, thou shalt discourse
1287To young Octavius of the state of things.
1288Lend me your hand.

Exeunt

[3.2]

Enter Brutus and goes into the pulpit, and Cassius with the plebians.

PLEBIANS

1289We will be satisfied! Let us be satisfied!

BRUTUS

1290Then follow me, and give me audience, friends.
1291Cassius, go you into the other street,
1292And part the numbers.
1293Those that will hear me speak, let 'em stay here;
1294Those that will follow Cassius, go with him;
1295And public reasons shall be rendered
1296Of Caesar's death.

1 PLEBIAN

Of Caesar's death. I will hear Brutus speak.

2 PLEBIAN

1297I will hear Cassius and compare their reasons,
1298When severally we hear them renderèd.

[Exit Cassius, with some of the Plebians.]

3 PLEBIAN

1299The noble Brutus is ascended. Silence!

BRUTUS

1300Be patient till the last.

Romans, countrymen, and lovers! Hear me for my cause, and be silent, that you may hear. Believe me for mine honor, and have respect to mine honor that you may believe. Censure me in your wisdom, and awake your senses that you may the better judge. If there be any in this assembly, any dear friend of Caesar's, to him I say, that Brutus' love to Caesar was no less than his. If then that friend demand why Brutus rose against Caesar, this is my answer: not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more. Had you rather Caesar were living and die all slaves, than that Caesar were dead to live all free men? As Caesar loved me, I weep for him; as he was fortunate, I rejoice at it; as he was valiant, I honor him; but as he was ambitious, I slew him. There is tears for his love; joy, for his fortune; honor, for his valor; and death, for his ambition. Who is here so base that would be a bondman? If any, speak, for him have I offended. Who is here so rude that would not be a Roman? If any, speak, for him have I offended. Who is here so vile that will not love his country? If any, speak, for him have I offended. I pause for a reply.

ALL

None, Brutus, none.

BRUTUS

Then none have I offended. I have done no more to Caesar than you shall do to Brutus. The question of his death is enrolled in the Capitol; his glory not extenuated, wherein he was worthy; nor his offenses enforced, for which he suffered death. Enter Mark Antony, with Caesar's body. Here comes his body, mourned by Mark Antony, who, though he had no hand in his death, shall receive the benefit of his dying, a place in the commonwealth, as which of you shall not? With this, I depart, that as I slew my best lover for the good of Rome, I have the same dagger for myself, when it shall please my country to need my death.

ALL

Live Brutus! Live! Live!

1 PLEBIAN

1301Bring him with triumph home unto his house.

2 PLEBIAN

1302Give him a statue with his ancestors.

3 PLEBIAN

1303Let him be Caesar.

4 PLEBIAN

Let him be Caesar. Caesar's better parts,
1304Shall be crowned in Brutus.

1 PLEBIAN

1305We'll bring him to his house with shouts and clamors.

BRUTUS

1306My countrymen!

2 PLEBIAN

1307Peace! Silence! Brutus speaks.

1 PLEBIAN

1308Peace, ho!

BRUTUS

1309Good countrymen, let me depart alone,
1310And for my sake, stay here with Antony.
1311Do grace to Caesar's corpse, and grace his speech
1312Tending to Caesar's glories, which Mark Antony
1313By our permission is allowed to make.
1314I do entreat you, not a man depart,
1315Save I alone, till Antony have spoke.

Exit

1 PLEBIAN

1316Stay, ho! And let us hear Mark Antony.

3 PLEBIAN

1317Let him go up into the public chair.
1318We'll hear him. Noble Antony, go up.

ANTONY

1319For Brutus' sake, I am beholding to you.

4 PLEBIAN

1320What does he say of Brutus?

3 PLEBIAN

1321He says, for Brutus' sake
1322He finds himself beholding to us all.

4 PLEBIAN

1323'Twere best he speak no harm of Brutus here!

1 PLEBIAN

1324This Caesar was a tyrant.

3 PLEBIAN

This Caesar was a tyrant. Nay, that's certain:
1325We are blest that Rome is rid of him.

2 PLEBIAN

1326Peace! Let us hear what Antony can say.

ANTONY

1327You gentle Romans—

ALL

You gentle Romans— Peace, ho! Let us hear him.

ANTONY

1328Friends! Romans! Countrymen! Lend me your ears.
1329I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him.
1330The evil that men do lives after them;
1331The good is oft interrèd with their bones:
1332So let it be with Caesar. The noble Brutus
1333Hath told you Caesar was ambitious.
1334If it were so, it was a grievous fault,
1335And grievously hath Caesar answered it.
1336Here, under leave of Brutus and the rest—
1337For Brutus is an honorable man,
1338So are they all, all honorable men—
1339Come I to speak in Caesar's funeral.
1340He was my friend: faithful and just to me.
1341But Brutus says he was ambitious,
1342And Brutus is an honorable man.
1343He hath brought many captives home to Rome,
1344Whose ransoms did the general coffers fill.
1345Did this in Caesar seem ambitious?
1346When that the poor have cried, Caesar hath wept;
1347Ambition should be made of sterner stuff.
1348Yet Brutus says he was ambitious,
1349And Brutus is an honorable man.
1350You all did see that on the Lupercal
1351I thrice presented him a kingly crown,
1352Which he did thrice refuse. Was this ambition?
1353Yet Brutus says he was ambitious,
1354And sure he is an honorable man.
1355I speak not to disprove what Brutus spoke,
1356But here I am, to speak what I do know.
1357You all did love him once, not without cause.
1358What cause withholds you, then, to mourn for him?
1359O judgment! Thou art fled to brutish beasts,
1360And men have lost their reason! Bear with me;
1361My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar,
1362And I must pause till it come back to me.

1 PLEBIAN

1363Methinks there is much reason in his sayings.

2 PLEBIAN

1364If thou consider rightly of the matter,
1365Caesar has had great wrong.

3 PLEBIAN

Caesar has had great wrong. Has he, masters?
1366I fear there will a worse come in his place.

4 PLEBIAN

1367Marked ye his words? He would not take the crown;
1368Therefore 'tis certain he was not ambitious.

1 PLEBIAN

1369If it be found so, some will dear abide it.

2 PLEBIAN

1370Poor soul, his eyes are red as fire with weeping.

3 PLEBIAN

1371There's not a nobler man in Rome than Antony.

4 PLEBIAN

1372Now mark him, he begins again to speak.

ANTONY

1373But yesterday, the word of Caesar might
1374Have stood against the world. Now lies he there,
1375And none so poor to do him reverence.
1376O masters! If I were disposed to stir
1377Your hearts and minds to mutiny and rage,
1378I should do Brutus wrong, and Cassius wrong,
1379Who you all know are honorable men.
1380I will not do them wrong; I rather choose
1381To wrong the dead, to wrong myself and you,
1382Than I will wrong such honorable men.
1383But here's a parchment with the seal of Caesar;
1384I found it in his closet. 'Tis his will.
1385Let but the commons hear this testament,
1386Which pardon me, I do not mean to read,
1387And they would go and kiss dead Caesar's wounds,
1388And dip their napkins in his sacred blood,
1389Yea, beg a hair of him for memory,
1390And dying, mention it within their wills,
1391Bequeathing it as a rich legacy
1392Unto their issue.

4 PLEBIAN

1393We'll hear the will! Read it, Mark Antony!

ALL

1394The will! The will! We will hear Caesar's will!

ANTONY

1395Have patience, gentle friends. I must not read it.
1396It is not meet you know how Caesar loved you.
1397You are not wood, you are not stones, but men;
1398And being men, hearing the will of Caesar,
1399It will inflame you; it will make you mad.
1400'Tis good you know not that you are his heirs,
1401For if you should, oh, what would come of it?

4 PLEBIAN

1402Read the will! We'll hear it, Antony!
1403You shall read us the will! Caesar's will!

ANTONY

1404Will you be patient? Will you stay awhile?
1405I have o'ershot myself to tell you of it,
1406I fear I wrong the honorable men
1407Whose daggers have stabbed Caesar. I do fear it.

4 PLEBIAN

1408They were traitors! "Honorable men"?

ALL

1409The will! The testament!

2 PLEBIAN

1410They were villains, murderers! The will! Read the will!

ANTONY

1411You will compel me then to read the will?
1412Then make a ring about the corpse of Caesar,
1413And let me show you him that made the will.
1414Shall I descend? And will you give me leave?

ALL

1415Come down!

2 PLEBIAN

Come down! Descend!

4 PLEBIAN

1416A ring!
A ring! Stand round!

1 PLEBIAN

1417Stand from the hearse! Stand from the body!

2 PLEBIAN

1418Room for Antony! Most noble Antony!

ANTONY

1419Nay, press not so upon me! Stand far'er off.

ALL

1420Stand back! Room! Bear back!

ANTONY

1421If you have tears, prepare to shed them now.
1422You all do know this mantle; I remember
1423The first time ever Caesar put it on.
1424'Twas on a summer's evening in his tent,
1425That day he overcame the Nervii.
1426Look, in this place ran Cassius' dagger through.
1427See what a rent the envious Casca made.
1428Through this, the well-belovèd Brutus stabbed,
1429And as he plucked his cursèd steel away,
1430Mark how the blood of Caesar followed it,
1431As rushing out of doors to be resolved
1432If Brutus so unkindly knocked or no;
1433For Brutus, as you know, was Caesar's angel.
1434Judge, O you gods, how dearly Caesar loved him.
1435This was the most unkindest cut of all,
1436For when the noble Caesar saw him stab,
1437Ingratitude, more strong than traitors' arms,
1438Quite vanquished him; then burst his mighty heart,
1439And in his mantle muffling up his face,
1440Even at the base of Pompey's statue,
1441Which all the while ran blood, great Caesar fell.
1442Oh, what a fall was there, my countrymen!
1443Then I, and you, and all of us fell down,
1444Whilst bloody treason flourished over us.
1445Oh, now you weep, and I perceive you feel
1446The dint of pity. These are gracious drops.
1447Kind souls, what weep you when you but behold
1448Our Caesar's vesture wounded? Look you here!
1449Here is himself, marred as you see with traitors.

1 PLEBIAN

1450Oh, piteous spectacle!

2 PLEBIAN

Oh, piteous spectacle! O noble Caesar!

3 PLEBIAN

1451Oh, woeful day!

4 PLEBIAN

Oh, woeful day! Oh, traitors! Villains!

1 PLEBIAN

1452Oh, most bloody sight!

2 PLEBIAN

Oh, most bloody sight! We will be revenged!

ALL

1453Revenge! About! Seek! Burn! Fire! Kill! Slay!
1454Let not a traitor live!

ANTONY

Let not a traitor live! Stay, countrymen!

1 PLEBIAN

1455Peace there! Hear the noble Antony!

2 PLEBIAN

1456We'll hear him! We'll follow him! We'll die with him.

ANTONY

1457Good friends! Sweet friends! Let me not stir you up
1458To such a sudden flood of mutiny.
1459They that have done this deed are honorable.
1460What private griefs they have, alas, I know not,
1461That made them do it. They are wise and honorable,
1462And will no doubt with reasons answer you.
1463I come not, friends, to steal away your hearts.
1464I am no orator, as Brutus is,
1465But, as you know me all, a plain blunt man
1466That love my friend, and that they know full well
1467That gave me public leave to speak of him:
1468For I have neither wit, nor words, nor worth,
1469Action, nor utterance, nor the power of speech
1470To stir men's blood. I only speak right on.
1471I tell you that which you yourselves do know;
1472Show you sweet Caesar's wounds, poor poor dumb mouths,
1473And bid them speak for me. But were I Brutus,
1474And Brutus Antony, there were an Antony
1475Would ruffle up your spirits and put a tongue
1476In every wound of Caesar that should move
1477The stones of Rome to rise and mutiny.

ALL

1478We'll mutiny!

1 PLEBIAN

We'll mutiny! We'll burn the house of Brutus!

3 PLEBIAN

1479Away then! Come, seek the conspirators!

ANTONY

1480Yet hear me countrymen! Yet hear me speak!

ALL

1481Peace, ho! Hear Antony! Most noble Antony!

ANTONY

1482Why, friends, you go to do you know not what.
1483Wherein hath Caesar thus deserved your loves?
1484Alas you know not. I must tell you then:
1485You have forgot the will I told you of.

ALL

1486Most true! The will! Let's stay and hear the will!

ANTONY

1487Here is the will, and under Caesar's seal.
1488To every Roman citizen he gives,
1489To every several man, seventy-five drachmas.

2 PLEBIAN

1490Most noble Caesar! We'll revenge his death!

3 PLEBIAN

1491O royal Caesar!

ANTONY

O royal Caesar! Hear me with patience.

ALL

O royal Caesar! Hear me with patience. Peace ho!

ANTONY

1492Moreover, he hath left you all his walks,
1493His private arbors, and new-planted orchards
1494On this side Tiber. He hath left them you
1495And to your heirs forever—common pleasures
1496To walk abroad and recreate yourselves.
1497Here was a Caesar! When comes such another?

1 PLEBIAN

1498Never, never! Come! Away! Away!
1499We'll burn his body in the holy place,
1500And with the brands fire the traitors' houses!
1501Take up the body!

2 PLEBIAN

1502Go! Fetch fire!

3 PLEBIAN

1503Pluck down benches!

4 PLEBIAN

1504Pluck down forms, windows, anything!

Exit Plebians [with the body].

ANTONY

1505Now let it work! Mischief, thou art a-foot:
1506Take thou what course thou wilt.

Enter servant.

SERVANT

1507Sir, Octavius is already come to Rome.

ANTONY

1508Where is he?

SERVANT

1509He and Lepidus are at Caesar's house.

ANTONY

1510And thither will I straight to visit him.
1511He comes upon a wish. Fortune is merry,
1512And in this mood will give us anything.

SERVANT

1513I heard him say Brutus and Cassius
1514Are rid like madmen through the gates of Rome.

ANTONY

1515Belike they had some notice of the people
1516How I had moved them. Bring me to Octavius.

Exeunt

[3.3]

Enter Cinna the Poet, and after him the Plebians.

CINNA

1517I dreamt tonight, that I did feast with Caesar,
1518And things unluckily charge my fantasy.
1519I have no will to wander forth of doors,
1520Yet something leads me forth.

1 PLEBIAN

What is your name?

2 PLEBIAN

Whither are you going?

3 PLEBIAN

Where do you dwell?

4 PLEBIAN

Are you a married man or a bachelor?

2 PLEBIAN

Answer every man directly.

1 PLEBIAN

Ay, and briefly.

4 PLEBIAN

Ay, and wisely.

3 PLEBIAN

Ay, and truly, you were best.

CINNA

What is my name? Whither am I going? Where do I dwell? Am I a married man or a bachelor? Then to answer every man, directly and briefly, wisely and truly: wisely I say, I am a bachelor.

2 PLEBIAN

That's as much as to say, they are fools that marry. You'll bear me a bang for that, I fear. Proceed directly!

CINNA

Directly I am going to Caesar's funeral.

1 PLEBIAN

As a friend or an enemy?

CINNA

As a friend.

2 PLEBIAN

That matter is answered directly.

4 PLEBIAN

For your dwelling—briefly.

CINNA

Briefly, I dwell by the Capitol.

3 PLEBIAN

Your name sir, truly.

CINNA

Truly, my name is Cinna.

1 PLEBIAN

Tear him to pieces! He's a conspirator!

CINNA

I am Cinna the poet! I am Cinna the poet!

4 PLEBIAN

Tear him for his bad verses! Tear him for his bad verses!

CINNA

I am not Cinna the conspirator.

4 PLEBIAN

It is no matter; his name's Cinna. Pluck but his name out of his heart, and turn him going.

3 PLEBIAN

Tear him! Tear him! Come! Brands ho! Firebrands to Brutus! To Cassius! Burn all! Some to Decius' house, and some to Casca's! Some to Ligarius'! Away, go!

Exeunt all the plebians.
