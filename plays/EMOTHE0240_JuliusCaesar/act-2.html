Act II

[2.1]

Enter Brutus in his orchard.

BRUTUS

473What, Lucius, ho!
474I cannot, by the progress of the stars,
475Give guess how near to day— Lucius, I say!
476I would it were my fault to sleep so soundly.
477When, Lucius, when? Awake, I say! What, Lucius!

Enter Lucius.

LUCIUS

478Called you, my lord?

BRUTUS

479Get me a taper in my study, Lucius.
480When it is lighted, come and call me here.

LUCIUS

481I will, my Lord.

Exit.

BRUTUS

482It must be by his death, and for my part,
483I know no personal cause to spurn at him,
484But for the general. He would be crowned.
485How that might change his nature, there's the question.
486It is the bright day that brings forth the adder,
487And that craves wary walking. Crown him that,
488And then I grant we put a sting in him
489That at his will he may do danger with.
490Th'abuse of greatness is when it disjoins
491Remorse from power, and to speak truth of Caesar,
492I have not known when his affections swayed
493More than his reason. But 'tis a common proof
494That lowliness is young ambition's ladder,
495Whereto the climber upward turns his face,
496But when he once attains the upmost round,
497He then unto the ladder turns his back,
498Looks in the clouds, scorning the base degrees
499By which he did ascend. So Caesar may.
500Then lest he may, prevent. And since the quarrel
501Will bear no color for the thing he is,
502Fashion it thus: that what he is, augmented,
503Would run to these and these extremities;
504And therefore think him as a serpent's egg,
505Which hatched would, as his kind, grow mischievous,
506And kill him in the shell.

Enter Lucius.

LUCIUS

507The taper burneth in your closet, sir.
508Searching the window for a flint, I found
509This paper, thus sealed up, and I am sure
510It did not lie there when I went to bed.

Gives him the letter.

BRUTUS

511Get you to bed again; it is not day.
512Is not tomorrow, boy, the first of March?

LUCIUS

513I know not, sir.

BRUTUS

514Look in the calender, and bring me word.

LUCIUS

515I will, sir.

Exit.

BRUTUS

516The exhalations, whizzing in the air,
517Give so much light that I may read by them.

Opens the letter and reads.

518"Brutus, thou sleep'st. Awake, and see thyself.
519Shall Rome, etc. Speak, strike, redress!"
520"Brutus, thou sleep'st. Awake."
521Such instigations have been often dropped
522Where I have took them up.
523"Shall Rome, etc." Thus must I piece it out:
524Shall Rome stand under one man's awe? What Rome?
525My ancestors did from the streets of Rome
526The Tarquin drive, when he was called a king.
527"Speak, strike, redress!" Am I entreated
528To speak and strike? O Rome, I make thee promise,
529If the redress will follow, thou receivest
530Thy full petition at the hand of Brutus.

Enter Lucius.

LUCIUS

531Sir, March is wasted fifteen days.

Knock within.

BRUTUS

532'Tis good. Go to the gate, somebody knocks:

[Exit Lucius.]

533Since Cassius first did whet me against Caesar,
534I have not slept.
535Between the acting of a dreadful thing
536And the first motion, all the interim is
537Like a phantasma or a hideous dream:
538The genius and the mortal instruments
539Are then in council, and the state of man,
540Like to a little kingdom, suffers then
541The nature of an insurrection.

Enter Lucius.

LUCIUS

542Sir, 'tis your brother Cassius at the door,
543Who doth desire to see you.

BRUTUS

Who doth desire to see you. Is he alone?

LUCIUS

544No, sir. There are more with him.

BRUTUS

No, sir. There are more with him. Do you know them?

LUCIUS

545No, sir. Their hats are plucked about their ears,
546And half their faces buried in their cloaks,
547That by no means I may discover them
548By any mark of favor.

BRUTUS

By any mark of favor. Let 'em enter.

[Exit Lucius.]

549They are the faction. O conspiracy,
550Sham'st thou to show thy dangerous brow by night,
551When evils are most free? O then, by day
552Where wilt thou find a cavern dark enough
553To mask thy monstrous visage? Seek none, conspiracy!
554Hide it in smiles and affability!
555For if thou put thy native semblance on,
556Not Erebus itself were dim enough
557To hide thee from prevention.

Enter the Conspirators, Cassius, Casca, Decius, Cinna, Metellus, and Trebonius.

CASSIUS

558I think we are too bold upon your rest.
559Good morrow, Brutus. Do we trouble you?

BRUTUS

560I have been up this hour, awake all night.
561Know I these men that come along with you?

CASSIUS

562Yes, every man of them, and no man here
563But honors you, and every one doth wish
564You had but that opinion of yourself
565Which every noble Roman bears of you.
566This is Trebonius.

BRUTUS

This is Trebonius. He is welcome hither.

CASSIUS

567This, Decius Brutus.

BRUTUS

This, Decius Brutus. He is welcome too.

CASSIUS

568This, Casca; this, Cinna; and this, Metellus Cimber.

BRUTUS

569They are all welcome.
570What watchful cares do interpose themselves
571Betwixt your eyes and night?

CASSIUS

572Shall I entreat a word?

They whisper.

DECIUS

573Here lies the east. Doth not the day break here?

CASCA

574No.

CINNA

575O pardon, sir, it doth; and yon gray lines
576That fret the clouds are messengers of day.

CASCA

577You shall confess that you are both deceived.
578Here, as I point my sword, the sun arises,
579Which is a great way growing on the south,
580Weighing the youthful season of the year.
581Some two months hence, up higher toward the north
582He first presents his fire, and the high east
583Stands as the Capitol, directly here.

BRUTUS

584Give me your hands all over, one by one.

CASSIUS

585And let us swear our resolution.

BRUTUS

586No, not an oath. If not the face of men,
587The sufferance of our souls, the time's abuse—
588If these be motives weak, break off betimes,
589And every man hence, to his idle bed.
590So let high-sighted tyranny range on,
591Till each man drop by lottery. But if these,
592As I am sure they do, bear fire enough
593To kindle cowards and to steel with valor
594The melting spirits of women, then, countrymen,
595What need we any spur but our own cause
596To prick us to redress? What other bond
597Than secret Romans that have spoke the word
598And will not palter? And what other oath,
599Than honesty to honesty engaged,
600That this shall be, or we will fall for it.
601Swear priests and cowards, and men cautelous,
602Old feeble carrions and such suffering souls
603That welcome wrongs. Unto bad causes swear
604Such creatures as men doubt, but do not stain
605The even virtue of our enterprise,
606Nor th'insuppressive mettle of our spirits,
607To think that or our cause or our performance
608Did need an oath, when every drop of blood
609That every Roman bears—and nobly bears—
610Is guilty of a several bastardy,
611If he do break the smallest particle
612Of any promise that hath passed from him.

CASSIUS

613But what of Cicero? Shall we sound him?
614I think he will stand very strong with us.

CASCA

615Let us not leave him out.

CINNA

Let us not leave him out. No, by no means.

METELLUS

616O let us have him, for his silver hairs
617Will purchase us a good opinion
618And buy men's voices to commend our deeds.
619It shall be said his judgment ruled our hands.
620Our youths and wildness shall no whit appear,
621But all be buried in his gravity.

BRUTUS

622Oh, name him not; let us not break with him,
623For he will never follow anything
624That other men begin.

CASSIUS

625Then leave him out.

CASCA

Then leave him out. Indeed, he is not fit.

DECIUS

626Shall no man else be touched but only Caesar?

CASSIUS

627Decius well urged. I think it is not meet
628Mark Antony, so well beloved of Caesar,
629Should outlive Caesar. We shall find of him
630A shrewd contriver. And you know, his means,
631If he improve them, may well stretch so far
632As to annoy us all: which to prevent,
633Let Antony and Caesar fall together.

BRUTUS

634Our course will seem too bloody, Caius Cassius,
635To cut the head off and then hack the limbs,
636Like wrath in death, and envy afterwards;
637For Antony is but a limb of Caesar.
638Let's be sacrificers, but not butchers, Caius.
639We all stand up against the spirit of Caesar,
640And in the spirit of men there is no blood.
641Oh, that we then could come by Caesar's spirit,
642And not dismember Caesar! But, alas,
643Caesar must bleed for it. And, gentle friends,
644Let's kill him boldly but not wrathfully;
645Let's carve him as a dish fit for the gods,
646Not hew him as a carcass fit for hounds.
647And let our hearts, as subtle masters do,
648Stir up their servants to an act of rage
649And after seem to chide 'em. This shall make
650Our purpose necessary and not envious;
651Which so appearing to the common eyes,
652We shall be called purgers, not murderers.
653And for Mark Antony, think not of him,
654For he can do no more than Caesar's arm,
655When Caesar's head is off.

CASSIUS

When Caesar's head is off. Yet I fear him,
656For in the engrafted love he bears to Caesar—

BRUTUS

657Alas, good Cassius, do not think of him.
658If he love Caesar, all that he can do
659Is to himself: take thought, and die for Caesar.
660And that were much he should, for he is given
661To sports, to wildness, and much company.

TREBONIUS

662There is no fear in him. Let him not die,
663For he will live, and laugh at this hereafter.

Clock strikes.

BRUTUS

664Peace! Count the clock.

CASSIUS

Peace! Count the clock. The clock hath stricken three.

TREBONIUS

665'Tis time to part.

CASSIUS

'Tis time to part. But it is doubtful yet
666Whether Caesar will come forth today or no.
667For he is superstitious grown of late,
668Quite from the main opinion he held once
669Of fantasy, of dreams, and ceremonies.
670It may be these apparent prodigies,
671The unaccustomed terror of this night,
672And the persuasion of his augurers
673May hold him from the Capitol today.

DECIUS

674Never fear that. If he be so resolved
675I can o'ersway him. For he loves to hear
676That unicorns may be betrayed with trees,
677And bears with glasses; elephants, with holes;
678Lions, with toils; and men, with flatterers.
679But when I tell him he hates flatterers,
680He says he does, being then most flatterèd.
681Let me work.
682For I can give his humor the true bent,
683And I will bring him to the Capitol.

CASSIUS

684Nay, we will all of us be there to fetch him.

BRUTUS

685By the eighth hour. Is that the uttermost?

CINNA

686Be that the uttermost, and fail not then.

METELLUS

687Caius Ligarius doth bear Caesar hard,
688Who rated him for speaking well of Pompey.
689I wonder none of you have thought of him.

BRUTUS

690Now, good Metellus, go along by him.
691He loves me well, and I have given him reasons.
692Send him but hither, and I'll fashion him.

CASSIUS

693The morning comes upon's. We'll leave you, Brutus.
694And friends, disperse yourselves, but all remember
695What you have said, and show yourselves true Romans.

BRUTUS

696Good gentlemen, look fresh and merrily.
697Let not our looks put on our purposes,
698But bear it as our Roman actors do,
699With untired spirits and formal constancy.
700And so good morrow to you every one.

Exeunt [all but] Brutus.

701Boy! Lucius! Fast asleep? It is no matter.
702Enjoy the honey-heavy dew of slumber.
703Thou hast no figures nor no fantasies,
704Which busy care draws in the brains of men.
705Therefore thou sleep'st so sound.

Enter Portia. Enter Portia.

PORTIA

Therefore thou sleep'st so sound. Brutus, my Lord.

BRUTUS

706Portia! What mean you? Wherefore rise you now?
707It is not for your health thus to commit
708Your weak condition to the raw cold morning.

PORTIA

709Nor for yours neither. You've ungently, Brutus,
710Stole from my bed, and yesternight at supper
711You suddenly arose and walked about,
712Musing and sighing, with your arms across,
713And when I asked you what the matter was,
714You stared upon me, with ungentle looks.
715I urged you further; then you scratched your head
716And too impatiently stamped with your foot.
717Yet I insisted; yet you answered not,
718But with an angry wafture of your hand
719Gave sign for me to leave you. So I did,
720Fearing to strengthen that impatience
721Which seemed too much enkindled, and withal
722Hoping it was but an effect of humor,
723Which sometime hath his hour with every man.
724It will not let you eat, nor talk, nor sleep;
725And could it work so much upon your shape
726As it hath much prevailed on your condition,
727I should not know you Brutus. Dear my Lord,
728Make me acquainted with your cause of grief.

BRUTUS

729I am not well in health, and that is all.

PORTIA

730Brutus is wise, and were he not in health,
731He would embrace the means to come by it.

BRUTUS

732Why so I do. Good Portia, go to bed.

PORTIA

733Is Brutus sick? And is it physical
734To walk unbracèd and suck up the humors
735Of the dank morning? What, is Brutus sick,
736And will he steal out of his wholesome bed
737To dare the vile contagion of the night,
738And tempt the rheumy and unpurgèd air
739To add unto his sickness? No, my Brutus.
740You have some sick offense within your mind,
741Which by the right and virtue of my place
742I ought to know of. And upon my knees,
743I charm you, by my once-commended beauty,
744By all your vows of love, and that great vow
745Which did incorporate and make us one,
746That you unfold to me, your self, your half,
747Why you are heavy, and what men tonight
748Have had resort to you. For here have been
749Some six or seven who did hide their faces
750Even from darkness.

BRUTUS

Even from darkness. Kneel not, gentle Portia.

PORTIA

751I should not need, if you were gentle Brutus.
752Within the bond of marriage, tell me, Brutus,
753Is it excepted, I should know no secrets
754That appertain to you? Am I your self,
755But as it were in sort, or limitation,
756To keep with you at meals, comfort your bed,
757And talk to you sometimes? Dwell I but in the suburbs
758Of your good pleasure? If it be no more,
759Portia is Brutus' harlot, not his wife.

BRUTUS

760You are my true and honorable wife,
761As dear to me as are the ruddy drops
762That visit my sad heart.

PORTIA

763If this were true, then should I know this secret.
764I grant I am a woman, but withal
765A woman that Lord Brutus took to wife.
766I grant I am a woman, but withal
767A woman well reputed, Cato's daughter.
768Think you I am no stronger than my sex
769Being so fathered, and so husbanded?
770Tell me your counsels; I will not disclose 'em.
771I have made strong proof of my constancy,
772Giving myself a voluntary wound
773Here, in the thigh. Can I bear that with patience
774And not my husband's secrets?

BRUTUS

And not my husband's secrets? O ye gods!
775Render me worthy of this noble wife.

Knock.

776Hark, hark, one knocks! Portia go in awhile,
777And by and by thy bosom shall partake
778The secrets of my heart.
779All my engagements I will construe to thee,
780All the charactery of my sad brows.
781Leave me with haste. Ligarius. Ligarius.
782Lucius, who's that knocks.

Exit Portia. Enter Lucius and Ligarius.

LUCIUS

783Here is a sick man that would speak with you.

BRUTUS

784Caius Ligarius, that Metellus spake of.
785Boy, stand aside. Caius Ligarius, how?

LIGARIUS

786Vouchsafe good morrow from a feeble tongue.

BRUTUS

787O what a time have you chose out, brave Caius,
788To wear a kerchief. Would you were not sick!.

LIGARIUS

789I am not sick, if Brutus have in hand
790Any exploit worthy the name of honor.

BRUTUS

791Such an exploit have I in hand Ligarius,
792Had you a healthful ear to hear of it.

LIGARIUS

793By all the gods that Romans bow before,
794I here discard my sickness. Soul of Rome,
795Brave son, derived from honorable loins,
796Thou like an exorcist hast conjured up
797My mortified spirit. Now bid me run,
798And I will strive with things impossible,
799Yea, get the better of them. What's to do?

BRUTUS

800A piece of work that will make sick men whole.

LIGARIUS

801But are not some whole, that we must make sick?

BRUTUS

802That must we also. What it is my Caius,
803I shall unfold to thee as we are going
804To whom it must be done.

LIGARIUS

To whom it must be done. Set on your foot,
805And with a heart new-fired, I follow you
806To do I know not what, but it sufficeth
807That Brutus leads me on.

Thunder

BRUTUS

808Follow me, then.

Exeunt

[2.2]

Thunder and lightning. Enter Julius Caesar in his nightgown.

CAESAR

809Nor heaven nor earth have been at peace tonight.
810Thrice hath Calpurnia in her sleep cried out,
811"Help, ho, they murder Caesar!" Who's within?

Enter a servant.

SERVANT

812My Lord.

CAESAR

813Go bid the priests do present sacrifice,
814And bring me their opinions of success.

SERVANT

815I will, my lord.

Exit

Enter Calpurnia.

CALPURNIA

816What mean you, Caesar? Think you to walk forth?
817You shall not stir out of your house today.

CAESAR

818Caesar shall forth. The things that threatened me
819Ne'er looked but on my back. When they shall see
820The face of Caesar, they are vanishèd.

CALPURNIA

821Caesar, I never stood on ceremonies,
822Yet now they fright me. There is one within,
823Besides the things that we have heard and seen,
824Recounts most horrid sights seen by the watch.
825A lionness hath whelpèd in the streets,
826And graves have yawned and yielded up their dead;
827Fierce fiery warriors fight upon the clouds
828In ranks, and squadrons, and right form of war,
829Which drizzeled blood upon the Capitol.
830The noise of battle hurtled in the air:
831Horses do neigh, and dying men did groan,
832And ghosts did shriek and squeal about the streets.
833O Caesar, these things are beyond all use,
834And I do fear them.

CAESAR

And I do fear them. What can be avoided
835Whose end is purposed by the mighty gods?
836Yet Caesar shall go forth, for these predictions
837Are to the world in general as to Caesar.

CALPURNIA

838When beggars die, there are no comets seen;
839The heavens themselves blaze forth the death of princes

CAESAR

840Cowards die many times before their deaths;
841The valiant never taste of death but once.
842Of all the wonders that I yet have heard,
843It seems to me most strange that men should fear,
844Seeing that death, a necessary end,
845Will come when it will come.

Enter a servant.

CAESAR

Will come when it will come. What say the augurers?

SERVANT

846They would not have you to stir forth today.
847Plucking the entrails of an offering forth,
848They could not find a heart within the beast.

CAESAR

849The gods do this in shame of cowardice.
850Caesar should be a beast without a heart,
851If he should stay at home today for fear.
852No Caesar shall not. Danger knows full well
853That Caesar is more dangerous than he.
854We are two lions littered in one day,
855And I the elder and more terrible;
856And Caesar shall go forth.

CALPURNIA

And Caesar shall go forth. Alas, my Lord,
857Your wisdom is consumed in confidence.
858Do not go forth today. Call it my fear
859That keeps you in the house, and not your own.
860We'll send Mark Antony to the Senate House,
861And he shall say you are not well today.
862Let me upon my knee prevail in this.

CAESAR

863Mark Antony shall say I am not well,
864And for thy humor, I will stay at home.

Enter Decius.

865Here's Decius Brutus. He shall tell them so.

DECIUS

866Caesar, all hail! Good morrow, worthy Caesar,
867I come to fetch you to the Senate House.

CAESAR

868And you are come in very happy time
869To bear my greeting to the senators,
870And tell them that I will not come today—
871Cannot, is false; and that I dare not, falser:
872I will not come today. Tell them so, Decius.

CALPURNIA

873Say he is sick.

CAESAR

Say he is sick. Shall Caesar send a lie?
874Have I in conquest stretched mine arm so far
875To be afeard to tell gray-beards the truth?
876Decius, go tell them Caesar will not come.

DECIUS

877Most mighty Caesar, let me know some cause,
878Lest I be laughed at when I tell them so.

CAESAR

879The cause is in my will; I will not come.
880That is enough to satisfy the Senate.
881But for your private satisfaction,
882Because I love you, I will let you know.
883Calpurnia here, my wife, stays me at home.
884She dreamt tonight she saw my statue,
885Which like a fountain with an hundred spouts
886Did run pure blood, and many lusty Romans
887Came smiling and did bathe their hands in it.
888And these does she apply for warnings, and portents,
889And evils imminent; and on her knee
890Hath begged that I will stay at home today.

DECIUS

891This dream is all amiss interpreted:
892It was a vision fair and fortunate.
893Your statue spouting blood in many pipes,
894In which so many smiling Romans bathed,
895Signifies that from you great Rome shall suck
896Reviving blood, and that great men shall press
897For tinctures, stains, relics, and cognizance.
898This by Calpurnia's dream is signified.

CAESAR

899And this way have you well expounded it.

DECIUS

900I have, when you have heard what I can say,
901And know it now: the Senate have concluded
902To give this day a crown to mighty Caesar.
903If you shall send them word you will not come,
904Their minds may change. Besides, it were a mock
905Apt to be rendered for someone to say,
906"Break up the Senate till another time,
907When Caesar's wife shall meet with better dreams."
908If Caesar hide himself, shall they not whisper
909"Lo, Caesar is afraid"?
910Pardon me, Caesar, for my dear dear love
911To your proceeding bids me tell you this,
912And reason to my love is liable.

CAESAR

913How foolish do your fears seem now, Calpurnia?
914I am ashamèd I did yield to them.
915Give me my robe, for I will go.

Enter Brutus, Ligarius, Metellus, Casca, Trebonius, Cinna, and Publius.

916And look where Publius is come to fetch me.

PUBLIUS

917Good morrow, Caesar.

CAESAR

Good morrow, Caesar. Welcome, Publius.
918What, Brutus, are you stirred so early too?
919Good morrow, Casca. Caius Ligarius,
920Caesar was ne'er so much your enemy
921As that same ague which hath made you lean.
922What is't o'clock?

BRUTUS

What is't o'clock? Caesar, 'tis strucken eight.

CAESAR

923I thank you for your pains and courtesy.

Enter Antony.

924See, Antony, that revels long o' nights,
925Is notwithstanding up. Good morrow, Antony.

ANTONY

926So to most noble Caesar.

CAESAR

So to most noble Caesar. Bid them prepare within.
927I am to blame to be thus waited for.
928Now, Cinna. Now, Metellus. What, Trebonius,
929I have an hour's talk in store for you.
930Remember that you call on me today.
931Be near me, that I may remember you.

TREBONIUS

932Caesar I will,
933and so near will I be,aparte
934That your best friends shall wish I had been further.aparte

CAESAR

935Good friends, go in, and taste some wine with me.
936And we, like friends, will straight way go together.

BRUTUS

937That every like is not the same, O Caesar,aparte
938The heart of Brutus yearns to think upon.aparte

Exeunt

[2.3]

Enter Artemidorus [reading a paper].

ARTEMIDORUS

"Caesar, beware of Brutus; take heed of Cassius; come not near Casca; have an eye to Cinna; trust not Trebonius; mark well Metellus Cimber. Decius Brutus loves thee not. Thou hast wronged Caius Ligarius. There is but one mind in all these men, and it is bent against Caesar. If thou be'est not immortal, look about you. Security gives way to conspiracy. The mighty gods defend thee. Thy lover, Artemidorus."

939Here will I stand, till Caesar pass along,
940And as a suitor will I give him this.
941My heart laments that virtue cannot live
942Out of the teeth of emulation.
943If thou read this, O Caesar, thou mayst live;
944If not, the Fates with traitors do contrive.

Exit.

[2.4]

Enter Portia and Lucius.

PORTIA

945I prithee, boy, run to the Senate House.
946Stay not to answer me, but get thee gone.
947Why dost thou stay?

LUCIUS

Why dost thou stay? To know my errand, madam.

PORTIA

948I would have had thee there and here again
949Ere I can tell thee what thou shouldst do there.
950O constancy, be strong upon my side!
951Set a huge mountain 'tween my heart and tongue.
952I have a man's mind, but a woman's might.
953How hard it is for women to keep counsel.
954Art thou here yet?

LUCIUS

Art thou here yet? Madam, what should I do?
955Run to the Capitol, and nothing else?
956And so return to you, and nothing else?

PORTIA

957Yes, bring me word, boy, if thy lord look well,
958For he went sickly forth, and take good note
959What Caesar doth, what suitors press to him.
960Hark, boy, what noise is that?

LUCIUS

961I hear none, madam.

PORTIA

I hear none, madam. Prithee, listen well:
962I heard a bustling rumor, like a fray,
963And the wind brings it from the Capitol.

LUCIUS

964Sooth, madam, I hear nothing.

Enter the Soothsayer.

PORTIA

965Come hither, fellow. Which way hast thou been?

SOOTHSAYER

966At mine own house, good lady.

PORTIA

967What is't o'clock?

SOOTHSAYER

What is't o'clock? About the ninth hour, lady.

PORTIA

968Is Caesar yet gone to the Capitol?

SOOTHSAYER

969Madam, not yet. I go to take my stand
970To see him pass on to the Capitol.

PORTIA

971Thou hast some suit to Caesar, hast thou not?

SOOTHSAYER

972That I have, lady, if it will please Caesar
973To be so good to Caesar as to hear me:
974I shall beseech him to befriend himself.

PORTIA

975Why, know'st thou any harm's intended towards him?

SOOTHSAYER

976None that I know will be; much that I fear may chance.
977Good morrow to you. Here the street is narrow.
978The throng that follows Caesar at the heels
979Of senators, of praetors, common suitors,
980Will crowd a feeble man almost to death.
981I'll get me to a place more void, and there
982Speak to great Caesar as he comes along.

Exit

PORTIA

983I must go in. Ay me! How weak a thing
984The heart of woman is! O Brutus,
985The Heavens speed thee in thine enterprise.
986Sure the boy heard me. Brutus hath a suit
987That Caesar will not grant. O, I grow faint!
988Run Lucius, and commend me to my lord!
989Say I am merry. Come to me again,
990And bring me word what he doth say to thee.

Exeunt
