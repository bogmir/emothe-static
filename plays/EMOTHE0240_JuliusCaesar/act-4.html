Act IV

[4.1]

Enter Antony, Octavius, and Lepidus.

ANTONY

1521These many then shall die; their names are pricked.

OCTAVIUS

1522Your brother too must die. Consent you, Lepidus?

LEPIDUS

1523I do consent.

OCTAVIUS

I do consent. Prick him down, Antony.

LEPIDUS

1524Upon condition Publius shall not live,
1525Who is your sister's son, Mark Antony.

ANTONY

1526He shall not live. Look, with a spot I damn him.
1527But Lepidus, go you to Caesar's house,
1528Fetch the will hither, and we shall determine
1529How to cut off some charge in legacies.

LEPIDUS

1530What, shall I find you here?

OCTAVIUS

1531Or here, or at the Capitol.

Exit Lepidus

ANTONY

1532This is a slight, unmeritable man,
1533Meet to be sent on errands. Is it fit,
1534The three-fold world divided, he should stand
1535One of the three to share it?

OCTAVIUS

One of the three to share it? So you thought him,
1536And took his voice who should be pricked to die
1537In our black sentence and proscription.

ANTONY

1538Octavius, I have seen more days than you,
1539And though we lay these honors on this man
1540To ease ourselves of divers sland'rous loads,
1541He shall but bear them, as the ass bears gold,
1542To groan and sweat under the business,
1543Either led or driven, as we point the way;
1544And having brought our treasure where we will,
1545Then take we down his load and turn him off,
1546Like to the empty ass, to shake his ears
1547And graze in commons.

OCTAVIUS

And graze in commons. You may do your will,
1548But he's a tried and valiant soldier.

ANTONY

1549So is my horse, Octavius, and for that
1550I do appoint him store of provender.
1551It is a creature that I teach to fight,
1552To wind, to stop, to run directly on,
1553His corporal motion governed by my spirit.
1554And in some taste is Lepidus but so:
1555He must be taught, and trained, and bid go forth—
1556A barren-spirited fellow, one that feeds
1557On objects, arts, and imitations,
1558Which out of use, and staled by other men,
1559Begin his fashion. Do not talk of him
1560But as a property. And now, Octavius,
1561Listen great things. Brutus and Cassius
1562Are levying powers; we must straight make head.
1563Therefore let our alliance be combined,
1564Our best friends made, our means stretched,
1565And let us presently go sit in council
1566How covert matters may be best disclosed,
1567And open perils surest answerèd.

OCTAVIUS

1568Let us do so, for we are at the stake
1569And bayed about with many enemies,
1570And some that smile have in their hearts, I fear,
1571Millions of mischiefs.

Exeunt

[4.2]

Drum. Enter Brutus, Lucilius, and the army. Titinius and Pindarus meet them.

BRUTUS

1572Stand, ho!

LUCILIUS

1573Give the word, ho, and stand!

BRUTUS

1574What now, Lucilius, is Cassius near?

LUCILIUS

1575He is at hand, and Pindarus is come
1576To do you salutation from his master.

BRUTUS

1577He greets me well. Your master, Pindarus,
1578In his own change or by ill officers,
1579Hath given me some worthy cause to wish
1580Things done, undone; but if he be at hand
1581I shall be satisfied.

PINDARUS

I shall be satisfied. I do not doubt
1582But that my noble master will appear
1583Such as he is, full of regard and honor.

BRUTUS

1584He is not doubted. A word, Lucilius,
1585How he received you. Let me be resolved.

LUCILIUS

1586With courtesy and with respect enough,
1587But not with such familiar instances,
1588Nor with such free and friendly conference
1589As he hath used of old.

BRUTUS

As he hath used of old. Thou hast described
1590A hot friend cooling. Ever note, Lucilius,
1591When love begins to sicken and decay,
1592It useth an enforcèd ceremony.
1593There are no tricks in plain and simple faith,
1594But hollow men, like horses hot at hand,
1595Make gallant show and promise of their mettle;

Low march within.

1596But when they should endure the bloody spur,
1597They fall their crests, and like deceitful jades
1598Sink in the trial. Comes his army on?

LUCILIUS

1599They mean this night in Sardis to be quartered.
1600The greater part, the horse in general,
1601Enter Cassius and his powers. Are come with Cassius. his powers.

BRUTUS

Enter Cassius and his powers. Are come with Cassius. his powers. Hark! he is arrived.
1602March gently on to meet him.

CASSIUS

1603Stand, ho!

BRUTUS

1604Stand, ho! Speak the word along!

1 SOLDIER

1605Stand!

2 SOLDIER

1606Stand!

3 SOLDIER

1607Stand!

CASSIUS

1608Most noble brother, you have done me wrong.

BRUTUS

1609Judge me, you gods! Wrong I mine enemies?
1610And if not so, how should I wrong a brother?

CASSIUS

1611Brutus, this sober form of yours hides wrongs,
1612And when you do them—

BRUTUS

And when you do them— Cassius, be content.
1613Speak your griefs softly. I do know you well.
1614Before the eyes of both our armies here,
1615Which should perceive nothing but love from us,
1616Let us not wrangle. Bid them move away;
1617Then in my tent, Cassius, enlarge your griefs,
1618And I will give you audience.

CASSIUS

And I will give you audience. Pindarus,
1619Bid our commanders lead their charges off
1620A little from this ground.

BRUTUS

1621Lucilius, do you the like, and let no man
1622Come to our tent, till we have done our conference.
1623Let Lucius and Titinius guard our door.

Exeunt [all but] Brutus and Cassius.

[4.3]

Enter BRUTUS and CASSIUS

CASSIUS

1624That you have wronged me doth appear in this:
1625You have condemned and noted Lucius Pella
1626For taking bribes here of the Sardians,
1627Wherein my letters, praying on his side,
1628Because I knew the man, was slighted off

BRUTUS

1629You wronged yourself to write in such a case.

CASSIUS

1630In such a time as this, it is not meet
1631That every nice offense should bear his comment.

BRUTUS

1632Let me tell you, Cassius, you yourself
1633Are much condemned to have an itching palm,
1634To sell and mart your offices for gold
1635To undeservers.

CASSIUS

To undeservers. I, an itching palm?
1636You know that you are Brutus that speaks this,
1637Or by the gods, this speech were else your last!

BRUTUS

1638The name of Cassius honors this corruption,
1639And chastisement doth therefore hide his head.

CASSIUS

1640Chastisement!

BRUTUS

1641Remember March, the ides of March remember.
1642Did not great Julius bleed for justice' sake?
1643What villain touched his body that did stab
1644And not for justice? What, shall one of us
1645That struck the foremost man of all this world
1646But for supporting robbers, shall we now
1647Contaminate our fingers with base bribes,
1648And sell the mighty space of our large honors
1649For so much trash as may be graspèd thus?
1650I had rather be a dog and bay the moon
1651Than such a Roman.

CASSIUS

Than such a Roman. Brutus, bait not me!
1652I'll not endure it. You forget yourself
1653To hedge me in. I am a soldier, I,
1654Older in practice, abler than yourself
1655To make conditions.

BRUTUS

1656Go to. You are not Cassius.

CASSIUS

1657I am.

BRUTUS

1658I say, you are not.

CASSIUS

1659Urge me no more! I shall forget myself.
1660Have mind upon your health. Tempt me no farther.

BRUTUS

1661Away, slight man.

CASSIUS

1662Is't possible?

BRUTUS

Is't possible? Hear me, for I will speak.
1663Must I give way and room to your rash choler?
1664Shall I be frighted when a madman stares?

CASSIUS

1665O ye gods! Ye gods! Must I endure all this?

BRUTUS

1666All this? Ay, more. Fret till your proud heart break.
1667Go show your slaves how choleric you are,
1668And make your bondmen tremble. Must I budge?
1669Must I observe you? Must I stand and crouch
1670Under your testy humor? By the gods,
1671You shall digest the venom of your spleen,
1672Though it do split you. For from this day forth,
1673I'll use you for my mirth, yea for my laughter,
1674When you are waspish.

CASSIUS

When you are waspish. Is it come to this?

BRUTUS

1675You say you are a better soldier.
1676Let it appear so; make your vaunting true,
1677And it shall please me well. For mine own part,
1678I shall be glad to learn of noble men.

CASSIUS

1679You wrong me every way. You wrong me, Brutus.
1680I said an elder soldier, not a better.
1681Did I say "better"?

BRUTUS

Did I say "better"? If you did, I care not.

CASSIUS

1682When Caesar lived, he durst not thus have moved me.

BRUTUS

1683Peace, peace! You durst not so have tempted him.

CASSIUS

1684I durst not?

BRUTUS

1685No.

CASSIUS

1686What? durst not tempt him?

BRUTUS

What? durst not tempt him? For your life you durst not.

CASSIUS

1687Do not presume too much upon my love;
1688I may do that I shall be sorry for.

BRUTUS

1689You have done that you should be sorry for.
1690There is no terror, Cassius, in your threats,
1691For I am armed so strong in honesty
1692That they pass by me as the idle wind,
1693Which I respect not. I did send to you
1694For certain sums of gold, which you denied me,
1695For I can raise no money by vile means.
1696By heaven, I had rather coin my heart
1697And drop my blood for drachmas than to wring
1698From the hard hands of peasants their vile trash
1699By any indirection. I did send
1700To you for gold to pay my legions,
1701Which you denied me. Was that done like Cassius?
1702Should I have answered Caius Cassius so?
1703When Marcus Brutus grows so covetous,
1704To lock such rascal counters from his friends,
1705Be ready, gods: with all your thunderbolts
1706Dash him to pieces.

CASSIUS

Dash him to pieces. I denied you not.

BRUTUS

Dash him to pieces. I denied you not. You did.

CASSIUS

1707That brought my answer back. Brutus hath rived my hart.
1708A friend should bear his friend's infirmities,
1709But Brutus makes mine greater than they are.

BRUTUS

1710I do not, till you practice them on me.

CASSIUS

1711You love me not.

BRUTUS

You love me not. I do not like your faults.

CASSIUS

1712A friendly eye could never see such faults.

BRUTUS

1713A flatterer's would not, though they do appear
1714As huge as high Olympus.

CASSIUS

1715Come, Antony, and young Octavius, come,
1716Revenge yourselves alone on Cassius,
1717For Cassius is aweary of the world,
1718Hated by one he loves, braved by his brother,
1719Checked like a bondman, all his faults observed,
1720Set in a notebook, learned, and conned by rote
1721To cast into my teeth. Oh, I could weep
1722My spirit from mine eyes. There is my dagger,
1723And here my naked breast; within, a heart
1724Dearer than Pluto's mine, richer than gold.
1725If that thou be'est a Roman, take it forth.
1726I that denied the gold will give my heart.
1727Strike as thou did'st at Caesar, for I know,
1728When thou did'st hate him worst, thou loved'st him better
1729Than ever thou loved'st Cassius.

BRUTUS

Than ever thou loved'st Cassius. Sheath your dagger.
1730Be angry when you will, it shall have scope;
1731Do what you will, dishonor shall be humor.
1732O Cassius, you are yokèd with a lamb
1733That carries anger as the flint bears fire,
1734Who much enforcèd, shows a hasty spark,
1735And straight is cold again.

CASSIUS

And straight is cold again. Hath Cassius lived
1736To be but mirth and laughter to his Brutus,
1737When grief and blood ill-tempered vexeth him?

BRUTUS

1738When I spoke that, I was ill-tempered too.

CASSIUS

1739Do you confess so much? Give me your hand.

BRUTUS

1740And my heart too.

CASSIUS

And my heart too. O Brutus!

BRUTUS

And my heart too. O Brutus! What's the matter?

CASSIUS

1741Have not you love enough to bear with me,
1742When that rash humor which my mother gave me
1743Makes me forgetful?

BRUTUS

Makes me forgetful? Yes, Cassius, and from henceforth,
1744When you are over-earnest with your Brutus,
1745He'll think your mother chides, and leave you so.

Enter a Poet, [Lucilius, and Titinius].

POET

1746Let me go in to see the generals!
1747There is some grudge between 'em. 'Tis not meet
1748They be alone.

LUCILIUS

They be alone. You shall not come to them!

POET

1749Nothing but death shall stay me.

CASSIUS

1750How now? What's the matter?

POET

1751For shame, you generals! What do you mean?
1752Love, and be friends, as two such men should be,
1753For I have seen more years, I'm sure, than ye.

CASSIUS

1754Ha, ha! How vilely doth this cynic rhyme!

BRUTUS

1755Get you hence, sirrah! Saucy fellow, hence!

CASSIUS

1756Bear with him, Brutus; 'tis his fashion.

BRUTUS

1757I'll know his humor, when he knows his time.
1758What should the wars do with these jigging fools?
1759Companion, hence.

CASSIUS

Companion, hence. Away, away be gone.

Exit Poet

BRUTUS

1760Lucilius and Titinius, bid the commanders
1761Prepare to lodge their companies tonight.

CASSIUS

1762And come yourselves, and bring Messala with you
1763Immediately to us.

[Exeunt Lucilius and Titinius] [Exeunt Lucilius and Titinius]

BRUTUS

Immediately to us. Lucius, a bowl of wine.

CASSIUS

1764I did not think you could have been so angry.

BRUTUS

1765O Cassius, I am sick of many griefs.

CASSIUS

1766Of your philosophy you make no use,
1767If you give place to accidental evils.

BRUTUS

1768No man bears sorrow better. Portia is dead.

CASSIUS

1769Ha? Portia?

BRUTUS

1770She is dead.

CASSIUS

1771How scaped I killing, when I crossed you so?
1772O insupportable and touching loss!
1773Upon what sickness?

BRUTUS

Upon what sickness? Impatient of my absence,
1774And grief that young Octavius with Mark Antony
1775Have made themselves so strong—for with her death
1776That tidings came—with this she fell distract,
1777And, her attendants absent, swallowed fire.

CASSIUS

1778And died so?

BRUTUS

And died so? Even so.

CASSIUS

And died so? Even so. O ye immortal gods!

Enter Boy with wine and tapers.

BRUTUS

1779Speak no more of her. Give me a bowl of wine.
1780In this I bury all unkindness, Cassius.

Drinks

CASSIUS

1781My heart is thirsty for that noble pledge.
1782Fill, Lucius, till the wine o'erswell the cup.
1783I cannot drink too much of Brutus' love.

[Drinks. Exit Lucius.]

Enter Titinius and Messala.

BRUTUS

1784Come in, Titinius. Welcome, good Messala.
1785Now sit we close about this taper here,
1786And call in question our necessities.

CASSIUS

1787Portia, art thou gone?

BRUTUS

Portia, art thou gone? No more I pray you.
1788Messala, I have here received letters
1789That young Octavius and Mark Antony
1790Come down upon us with a mighty power,
1791Bending their expedition toward Philippi.

MESSALA

1792Myself have letters of the self-same tenor.

BRUTUS

1793With what addition?

MESSALA

1794That by proscription and bills of outlawry
1795Octavius, Antony, and Lepidus
1796Have put to death an hundred senators.

BRUTUS

1797Therein our letters do not well agree:
1798Mine speak of seventy senators that died
1799By their proscriptions, Cicero being one.

CASSIUS

1800Cicero one?

MESSALA

Cicero one? Cicero is dead,
1801And by that order of proscription.
1802Had you your letters from your wife, my Lord?

BRUTUS

1803No, Messala.

MESSALA

1804Nor nothing in your letters writ of her?

BRUTUS

1805Nothing, Messala.

MESSALA

Nothing, Messala. That methinks is strange.

BRUTUS

1806Why ask you? Hear you ought of her in yours?

MESSALA

1807No, my lord.

BRUTUS

1808Now as you are a Roman, tell me true.

MESSALA

1809Then like a Roman, bear the truth I tell.
1810For certain she is dead, and by strange manner.

BRUTUS

1811Why farewell Portia. We must die, Messala.
1812With meditating that she must die once,
1813I have the patience to endure it now.

MESSALA

1814Even so great men great losses should endure.

CASSIUS

1815I have as much of this in art as you,
1816But yet my nature could not bear it so.

BRUTUS

1817Well, to our work alive. What do you think
1818Of marching to Philippi presently?

CASSIUS

1819I do not think it good.

BRUTUS

I do not think it good. Your reason?

CASSIUS

I do not think it good. Your reason? This it is:
1820'Tis better that the enemy seek us,
1821So shall he waste his means, weary his soldiers,
1822Doing himself offense, whilst we, lying still,
1823Are full of rest, defense, and nimbleness.

BRUTUS

1824Good reasons must of force give place to better.
1825The people 'twixt Philippi and this ground
1826Do stand but in a forced affection,
1827For they have grudged us contribution.
1828The enemy, marching along by them,
1829By them shall make a fuller number up,
1830Come on refreshed, new-added, and encouraged,
1831From which advantage shall we cut him off,
1832If at Philippi we do face him there,
1833These people at our back.

CASSIUS

These people at our back. Hear me, good brother—

BRUTUS

1834Under your pardon. You must note, beside,
1835That we have tried the utmost of our friends,
1836Our legions are brim full, our cause is ripe.
1837The enemy increaseth every day;
1838We, at the height, are ready to decline.
1839There is a tide in the affairs of men,
1840Which taken at the flood leads on to fortune;
1841Omitted, all the voyage of their life
1842Is bound in shallows and in miseries.
1843On such a full sea are we now afloat,
1844And we must take the current when it serves,
1845Or lose our ventures.

CASSIUS

Or lose our ventures. Then with your will go on.
1846We'll along ourselves and meet them at Philippi.

BRUTUS

1847The deep of night is crept upon our talk,
1848And nature must obey necessity,
1849Which we will niggard with a little rest.
1850There is no more to say.

CASSIUS

There is no more to say. No more, good night,
1851Early tomorrow will we rise, and hence.

BRUTUS

1852Lucius!
Lucius! [Enter Lucius.] My gown.
Lucius! [Enter Lucius.] My gown. [Exit Lucius.] Farewell, good Messala.
1853Good night Titinius. Noble, noble Cassius,
1854Good night, and good repose.

CASSIUS

1855O my dear brother!
1856This was an ill beginning of the night.
1857Never come such division 'tween our souls;
1858Let it not, Brutus.

BRUTUS

Let it not, Brutus. Every thing is well.

CASSIUS

1859Good night, my lord.

BRUTUS

Good night, my lord. Good night, good brother.

TITINIUS, MESSALA

1860Good night, Lord Brutus.

BRUTUS

Good night, Lord Brutus. Farewell, everyone.

Exeunt [Cassius, Titinius, Messala].

1861Give me the gown. Where is thy instrument?

LUCIUS

1862Here in the tent.

BRUTUS

Here in the tent. What? Thou speak'st drowsily!
1863Poor knave, I blame thee not; thou art o'er-watched.
1864Call Claudio and some other of my men.
1865I'll have them sleep on cushions in my tent.

LUCIUS

1866Varrus and Claudio!

Enter Varrus and Claudio.

VARRUS

Varrus and Claudio! Calls my Lord?

BRUTUS

1867I pray you, sirs, lie in my tent and sleep.
1868It may be I shall raise you by and by
1869On business to my brother Cassius.

VARRUS

1870So please you, we will stand and watch your pleasure.

BRUTUS

1871I will not have it so. Lie down, good sirs.
1872It may be I shall otherwise bethink me.
1873Look, Lucius, here's the book I sought for so:
1874I put it in the pocket of my gown.

LUCIUS

1875I was sure your lordship did not give it me.

BRUTUS

1876Bear with me, good boy. I am much forgetful.
1877Canst thou hold up thy heavy eyes awhile,
1878And touch thy instrument a strain or two?

LUCIUS

1879Ay, my lord, an't please you.

BRUTUS

Ay, my lord, an't please you. It does, my boy:
1880I trouble thee too much, but thou art willing.

LUCIUS

1881It is my duty, sir.

BRUTUS

1882I should not urge thy duty past thy might;
1883I know young bloods look for a time of rest.

LUCIUS

1884I have slept, my lord, already.

BRUTUS

1885It was well done, and thou shalt sleep again.
1886I will not hold thee long. If I do live,
1887I will be good to thee.

Music and a song.

1888This is a sleepy tune. O murd'rous slumber,
1889Layest thou thy leaden mace upon my boy
1890That plays the music? Gentle knave, good night.
1891I will not do thee so much wrong to wake thee.
1892If thou dost nod, thou break'st thy instrument.
1893I'll take it from thee, and, good boy, good night.
1894Let me see, let me see. Is not the leaf turned down
1895Where I left reading? Here it is I think.

Enter the Ghost of Caesar.

1896How ill this taper burns. Ha! Who comes here?
1897I think it is the weakness of mine eyes
1898That shapes this monstrous apparition.
1899It comes upon me! Art thou anything?
1900Art thou some god, some angel, or some devil
1901That mak'st my blood cold and my hair to stare?
1902Speak to me what thou art!

GHOST

1903Thy evil spirit, Brutus.

BRUTUS

Thy evil spirit, Brutus. Why com'st thou?

GHOST

1904To tell thee thou shalt see me at Philippi.

BRUTUS

1905Well; then I shall see thee again?

GHOST

1906Ay, at Philippi.

BRUTUS

1907Why, I will see thee at Philippi, then.
1908Now I have taken heart, thou vanishest.

[Exit Ghost.]

1909Ill spirit, I would hold more talk with thee.
1910Boy! Lucius! Varrus! Claudio! Sirs! Awake!
1911Claudio!

LUCIUS

1912The strings, my lord, are false.

BRUTUS

1913He thinks he still is at his instrument.
1914Lucius, awake!

LUCIUS

1915My lord?

BRUTUS

1916Did'st thou dream, Lucius, that thou so criedst out?

LUCIUS

1917My Lord, I do not know that I did cry.

BRUTUS

1918Yes that thou did'st. Did'st thou see anything?

LUCIUS

1919Nothing my Lord.

BRUTUS

1920Sleep again, Lucius. Sirrah Claudio!
1921Fellow! Thou! Awake!

VARRUS

Fellow! Thou! Awake! My lord?

CLAUDIO

Fellow! Thou! Awake! My lord? My lord?

BRUTUS

1922Why did you so cry out, sirs, in your sleep?

VARRUS; CLAUDIO

1923Did we my Lord?

BRUTUS

Did we my Lord? Ay. Saw you anything?

VARRUS

1924No, my lord. I saw nothing.

CLAUDIO

No, my lord. I saw nothing. Nor I my Lord.

BRUTUS

1925Go, and commend me to my brother Cassius.
1926Bid him set on his powers betimes before,
1927And we will follow.

VARRUS; CLAUDIO

And we will follow. It shall be done, my lord.

Exeunt
