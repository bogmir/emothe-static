ACT 5

ACT 5 SCENE 1

[Enter] LA FOOLE, CLERIMONT, DAW.

LA FOOLE

1103Where had you our swords, Master Clerimont?

CLERIMONT

1104Why, Dauphine took ’em from the madman.

LA FOOLE

1105And he took ’em from our boys, I warrant you.

CLERIMONT

1106Very like, sir.

LA FOOLE

1107Thank you, good Master Clerimont. Sir John Daw and I are both beholden to you.

CLERIMONT

1108Would I knew how to make you so, gentlemen.

DAW

1109Sir Amorous and I are your servants, sir.

[Enter MAVIS.]

MAVIS

1110Gentlemen, have any of you a pen and ink? I would fain write out a riddle in Italian for Sir Dauphine to translate.

CLERIMONT

1111Not I, in troth, lady, I am no scrivener.

DAW

1112I can furnish you, I think, lady.

[Exeunt DAW and MAVIS.]

CLERIMONT

1113He has it in the haft of a knife, I believe!

LA FOOLE

1114No, he has his box of instruments.

CLERIMONT

1115Like a surgeon!

LA FOOLE

1116For the mathematics: his square, his compasses, his brass pens, and black lead, to draw maps of every place and person where he comes.

CLERIMONT

1117How, maps of persons!

LA FOOLE

1118Yes, sir, of Nomentack, when he was here, and of the Prince of Moldavia, and of his mistress, Mistress Epicene.

CLERIMONT

1119Away! He has not found out her latitude, I hope.

LA FOOLE

1120You are a pleasant gentleman, sir.

[Re-enter DAW.]

CLERIMONT

1121Faith, now we are in private, let’s wanton it a little and talk waggishly. Sir John, I am telling Sir Amorous here that you two govern the ladies; where’er you come, you carry the feminine gender afore you.

DAW

1122They shall rather carry us afore them, if they will, sir.

CLERIMONT

1123Nay, I believe that they do, withal; but that you are the prime men in their affections, and direct all their actions−

DAW

1124Not I; Sir Amorous is.

LA FOOLE

1125I protest, Sir John is.

DAW

1126As I hope to rise in the state, Sir Amorous, you ha’ the person.

LA FOOLE

1127Sir John, you ha’ the person, and the discourse too.

DAW

1128Not I, sir. I have no discourse−and then you have activity beside.

LA FOOLE

1129I protest, Sir John, you come as high from Tripoly as I do every whit, and lift as many joined stools and leap over ’em, if you would use it−

CLERIMONT

1130Well, agree on’t together, knights, for between you you divide the kingdom or commonwealth of ladies’ affections: I see it and can perceive a little how they observe you, and fear you, indeed. You could tell strange stories, my masters, if you would, I know.

DAW

1131Faith, we have seen somewhat, sir.

LA FOOLE

1132That we have: velvet petticoats and wrought smocks or so.

DAW

1133Ay, and−

CLERIMONT

1134Nay, out with it, Sir John; do not envy your friend the pleasure of hearing, when you have had the delight of tasting.

DAW

1135Why−a−you speak, Sir Amorous.

LA FOOLE

1136No, do you, Sir John Daw.

DAW

1137I’ faith, you shall.

LA FOOLE

1138I’ faith, you shall.

DAW

1139Why, we have been−

LA FOOLE

1140In the great bed at Ware together in our time. On, Sir John.

DAW

1141Nay, do you, Sir Amorous.

CLERIMONT

1142And these ladies with you, knights?

LA FOOLE

1143No, excuse us, sir.

DAW

1144We must not wound reputation.

LA FOOLE

1145No matter; they were these, or others. Our bath cost us fifteen pound, when we came home.

CLERIMONT

1146Do you hear, Sir John, you shall tell me but one thing truly, as you love me.

DAW

1147If I can, I will, sir.

CLERIMONT

1148You lay in the same house with the bride here?

DAW

1149Yes, and conversed with her hourly, sir.

CLERIMONT

1150And what humour is she of? Is she coming and open, free?

DAW

1151Oh, exceeding open, sir. I was her servant, and Sir Amorous was to be.

CLERIMONT

1152Come, you have both had favours from her? I know and have heard so much.

DAW

1153Oh no, sir.

LA FOOLE

1154You shall excuse us, sir: we must not wound reputation.

CLERIMONT

1155Tut, she is married now, and you cannot hurt her with any report, and therefore speak plainly: how many times, i’ faith? Which of you led first? Ha?

LA FOOLE

1156Sir John had her maidenhead, indeed.

DAW

1157Oh, it pleases him to say so, sir, but Sir Amorous knows what is what as well.

CLERIMONT

1158Dost thou i’faith, Amorous?

LA FOOLE

1159In a manner, sir.

CLERIMONT

1160Why, I commend you lads. Little knows Don Bridegroom of this. Nor shall he, for me.

DAW

1161Hang him, mad ox.

CLERIMONT

1162Speak softly: here comes his nephew, with the Lady Haughty. He’ll get the ladies from you, sirs, if you look not to him in time.

LA FOOLE

1163Why, if he do, we’ll fetch them home again, I warrant you.

[Exeunt.]

ACT 5 SCENE 2

[Enter] HAUGHTY, DAUPHINE.

HAUGHTY

1164I assure you, Sir Dauphine, it is the price and estimation of your virtue only that hath embarked me to this adventure, and I could not but make out to tell you so; nor can I repent me of the act, since it is always an argument of some virtue in ourselves that we love and affect it so in others.

DAUPHINE

1165Your ladyship sets too high a price on my weakness.

HAUGHTY

1166Sir, I can distinguish gems from pebbles−

DAUPHINE

1167[Aside.] Are you so skilful in stones?

HAUGHTY

1168And howsover I may suffer in such a judgment as yours, by admitting equality of rank or society with Centaure or Mavis−

DAUPHINE

1169You do not, madam; I perceive they are your mere foils.

HAUGHTY

1170Then are you a friend to truth, sir. It makes me love you the more. It is not the outward but the inward man that I affect. They are not apprehensive of an eminent perfection, but love flat and dully.

CENTAURE

1171[Within.] Where are you, my lady Haughty?

HAUGHTY

1172I come presently, Centaure.−My chamber, sir, my page shall show you; and Trusty, my woman, shall be ever awake for you; you need not fear to communicate anything with her, for she is a Fidelia. I pray you wear this jewel for my sake, sir Dauphine. [Enter CENTAURE.] Where’s Mavis, Centaure?

CENTAURE

1173Within, madam, a-writing. I’ll follow you presently. I’ll but speak a word with Sir Dauphine.

[Exit HAUGHTY.]

DAUPHINE

1174With me, madam?

CENTAURE

1175Good Sir Dauphine, do not trust Haughty, nor make any credit to her, whatever you do besides. Sir Dauphine, I give you this caution: she is a perfect courtier and loves nobody but for her uses; and for her uses she loves all. Besides, her physicians give her out to be none o’ the clearest−whether she pay ’em or no, heav’n knows; and she’s above fifty too, and pargets! See her in a forenoon. Here comes Mavis, a worse face then she! You would not like this by candlelight. If you’ll come to my chamber one o’ these mornings early, or late in an evening, I’ll tell you more. [Enter MAVIS.] Where’s Haughty, Mavis?

MAVIS

1176Within, Centaure.

CENTAURE

1177What ha’ you there?

MAVIS

1178An Italian riddle for Sir Dauphine. (You shall not see it i’faith, Centaure.) Good sir Dauphine, solve it for me. I’ll call for it anon.

[Exeunt MAVIS and CENTAURE]

CLERIMONT

1179How now, Dauphine? How dost thou quit thyself of these females?

DAUPHINE

1180’Slight, they haunt me like fairies, and give me jewels here; I cannot be rid of ’em.

CLERIMONT

1181Oh, you must not tell though.

DAUPHINE

1182Mass, I forgot that; I was never so assaulted. One loves for virtue, and bribes me with this. Another loves me with caution, and so would possess me. A third brings me a riddle here, and all are jealous and rail each at other.

CLERIMONT

1183A riddle? Pray’ le’ me see’t? He reads the paper. Sir Dauphine, I chose this way of intimation for privacy. The ladies here, I know, have both hope and purpose to make a collegiate and servant of you. If I might be so honoured as to appear at any end of so noble a work, I would enter into a fame of taking physic tomorrow and continue it four or five days or longer, for your visitation. Mavis. −By my faith, a subtle one! Call you this a riddle? What’s their plain dealing, trow?

DAUPHINE

1184We lack Truewit to tell us that.

CLERIMONT

1185We lack him for somewhat else too: his knights reformados are wound up as high and insolent as ever they were.

DAUPHINE

1186You jest.

CLERIMONT

1187No drunkards, either with wine or vanity, ever confessed such stories of themselves. I would not give a fly’s leg in balance against all the women’s reputations here, if they could be but thought to speak truth; and for the bride, they have made their affidavit against her directly−

DAUPHINE

1188What, that they have lain with her?

CLERIMONT

1189Yes; and tell times and circumstances, with the cause why and the place where. I had almost brought ’em to affirm that they had done it today.

DAUPHINE

1190Not both of ’em?

CLERIMONT

1191Yes, faith; with a sooth or two more I had effected it. They would ha’ set it down under their hands.

DAUPHINE

1192Why, they will be our sport, I see, still! Whether we will or no.

ACT 5 SCENE 3

[Enter] TRUEWIT.

TRUEWIT

1193Oh, are you here? Come, Dauphine. Go call your uncle presently. I have fitted my divine and my canonist, dyed their beards and all; the knaves do not know themselves, they are so exalted and altered. Preferment changes any man. Thou shalt keep one door and I another, and then Clerimont in the midst, that he may have no means of escape from their cavilling when they grow hot once. And then the women (as I have given the bride her instructions) to break in upon him i’ the l’envoy. Oh, ‘twill be full and twanging! Away, fetch him. [Exit DAUPHINE.]Enter CUTBEARD disguised as a canon lawyer, OTTER as a divine.] Come, master doctor and master parson, look to your parts now and discharge ’em bravely; you are well set forth, perform it as well. If you chance to be out, do not confess it with standing still or humming or gaping one at another, but go on and talk aloud and eagerly, use vehement action, and only remember your terms, and you are safe. Let the matter go where it will: you have many will do so. But at first be very solemn and grave like your garments, though you loose yourselves after and skip out like a brace of jugglers on a table. Here he comes! Set your faces, and look superciliously while I present you.

[Enter DAUPHINE and MOROSE.]

MOROSE

1194Are these the two learned men?

TRUEWIT

1195Yes, sir; please you salute ’em?

MOROSE

1196Salute ’em? I had rather do anything than wear out time so unfruitfully, sir. I wonder how these common forms, as ‘God save you’ and ‘You are welcome’, are come to be a habit in our lives! Or ‘I am glad to see you!’ when I cannot see what the profit can be of these words, so long as it is no whit better with him whose affairs are sad and grievous that he hears this salutation.

TRUEWIT

1197’Tis true, sir; we’ll go to the matter then. Gentlemen, master doctor and master parson, I have acquainted you sufficiently with the business for which you are come hither. And you are not now to inform yourselves in the state of the question, I know. This is the gentleman who expects your resolution, and therefore, when you please, begin.

OTTER

1198Please you, master doctor.

CUTBEARD

1199Please you, good master parson.

OTTER

1200I would hear the canon law speak first.

CUTBEARD

1201It must give place to positive divinity, sir.

MOROSE

1202Nay, good gentlemen, do not throw me into circumstances. Let your comforts arrive quickly at me, those that are. Be swift in affording me my peace, if so I shall hope any. I love not your disputations or your court tumults. And that it be not strange to you, I will tell you. My father, in my education, was wont to advise me that I should always collect and contain my mind, not suff’ring it to flow loosely; that I should look to what things were necessary to the carriage of my life, and what not, embracing the one and eschewing the other. In short, that I should endear myself to rest and avoid turmoil: which now is grown to be another nature to me. So that I come not to your public pleadings, or your places of noise; not that I neglect those things that make for the dignity of the commonwealth, but for the mere avoiding of clamours and impertinencies of orators, that know not how to be silent. And for the cause of noise am I now a suitor to you. You do not know in what a misery I have been exercised this day, what a torrent of evil! My very house turns round with the tumult! I dwell in a windmill! The perpetual motion is here, and not at Eltham.

TRUEWIT

1203Well, good master doctor, will you break the ice? Master parson will wade after.

CUTBEARD

1204Sir, though unworthy, and the weaker, I will presume.

OTTER

1205’Tis no presumption, domine doctor.

MOROSE

1206Yet again!

CUTBEARD

1207Your question is, for how many causes a man may have divortium legitimum, a lawful divorce. First, you must understand the nature of the word, divorce, a divertendo−

MOROSE

1208No excursions upon words, good doctor; to the question briefly.

CUTBEARD

1209I answer then, the canon law affords divorce but in few cases, and the principal is in the common case, the adulterous case: But there are duodecim impedimenta, twelve impediments (as we call ’em) all which do not dirimere contractum, but irritum reddere matrimonium, as we say in the canon law, not take away the bond, but cause a nullity therein.

MOROSE

1210I understood you before; good sir, avoid your impertinency of translation.

OTTER

1211He cannot open this too much, sir, by your favour.

MOROSE

1212Yet more!

TRUEWIT

1213Oh, you must give the learned men leave, sir. To your impediments, master doctor.

CUTBEARD

1214The first is impedimentum erroris.

OTTER

1215Of which there are several species.

CUTBEARD

1216Ay, as error personae.

OTTER

1217If you contract yourself to one person, thinking her another.

CUTBEARD

1218Then, error fortunae.

OTTER

1219If she be a beggar, and you thought her rich.

CUTBEARD

1220Then, error qualitatis.

OTTER

1221If she prove stubborn or headstrong, that you thought obedient.

MOROSE

1222How? Is that, sir, a lawful impediment? One at once, I pray you, gentlemen.

OTTER

1223Ay, ante copulam, but not post copulam, sir.

CUTBEARD

1224Master parson says right. Nec post nuptiarum benedictionem. It doth indeed but irrita reddere sponsalia, annul the contract; after marriage it is of no obstancy.

TRUEWIT

1225Alas, sir, what a hope are we fall’n from, by this time!

CUTBEARD

1226The next is conditio: if you thought her freeborn, and she prove a bondwoman, there is impediment of estate and condition.

OTTER

1227Ay, but master doctor, those servitudes are sublatae now, among us Christians.

CUTBEARD

1228By your favour, master parson−

OTTER

1229You shall give me leave, master doctor.

MOROSE

1230Nay, gentlemen, quarrel not in that question; it concerns not my case:pass to the third.

CUTBEARD

1231Well then, the third is votum. If either party have made a vow of chastity. But that practice, as master parson said of the other, is taken away among us, thanks be to discipline. The fourth is cognatio: if the persons be of kin within the degrees.

OTTER

1232Ay. Do you know what the degrees are, sir?

MOROSE

1233No, nor I care not, sir; they offer me no comfort in the question, I am sure.

CUTBEARD

1234But there is a branch of this impediment may, which is cognatio spiritualis. If you were her godfather, sir, then the marriage is incestuous.

OTTER

1235That comment is absurd and superstitious, master doctor. I cannot endure it. Are we not all brothers and sisters, and as much akin in that as godfathers and god-daughters?

MOROSE

1236Oh me! To end the controversy, I never was a godfather, I never was a godfather in my life, sir. Pass to the next.

CUTBEARD

1237The fifth is crimen adulterii: the known case. The sixth, cultus disparitas, difference of religion: have you ever examined her what religion she is of?

MOROSE

1238No, I would rather she were of none, than be put to the trouble of it!

OTTER

1239You may have it done for you, sir.

MOROSE

1240By no means, good sir; on to the rest. Shall you ever come to an end, think you?

TRUEWIT

1241Yes, he has done half, sir. (On to the rest.) Be patient and expect, sir.

CUTBEARD

1242The seventh is vis: if it were upon compulsion or force.

MOROSE

1243Oh no, it was too voluntary, mine; too voluntary.

CUTBEARD

1244The eight is ordo: if ever she have taken holy orders.

OTTER

1245That’s supersitious too.

MOROSE

1246No matter, master parson: would she would go into a nunnery yet.

CUTBEARD

1247The ninth is ligament: if you were bound, sir, to any other before.

MOROSE

1248I thrust myself too soon into these fetters.

CUTBEARD

1249The tenth is publica honestas, which is inchoate quaedam affinitas.

OTTER

1250Ay, or affinitas orta ex sponsalibus, and is but leve impedimentum.

MOROSE

1251I feel no air of comfort blowing to me in all this.

CUTBEARD

1252The eleventh is affinitas ex fornicatione.

OTTER

1253Which is no less vera affinitas, than the other, master doctor.

CUTBEARD

1254True, quae oritur ex legitimo matrimonio.

OTTER

1255You say right, venerable doctor. And nascitur ex eo, quod per conjugium duae personae efficiuntur una caro−

MOROSE

1256Heyday, now they begin.

CUTBEARD

1257I conceive you, master parson. Ita per fornicationem aeque est verus pater, qui sic generat−

OTTER

1258Et vere filius qui sic generatur−

MOROSE

1259What’s all this to me?

CLERIMONT

1260[Aside] Now it grows warm.

CUTBEARD

1261The twelfth and last is si forte coire nequibis.

OTTER

1262Ay, that is impedimentum gravissimum. It doth utterly annul and annihilate, that. If you have manifestam frigiditatem, you are well, sir.

TRUEWIT

1263Why, there is comfort come at length, sir. Confess yourself but a man unable, and she will sue to be divorced first.

OTTER

1264Ay, or if there be morbus perpetuus et insanabilis; as paralysis, elephantiasis, or so−

DAUPHINE

1265Oh, but frigiditas is the fairer way, gentlemen.

OTTER

1266You say troth, sir, and as it is in the canon, master doctor.

CUTBEARD

1267I conceive you, sir.

CLERIMONT

1268[Aside] Before he speaks!

OTTER

1269That ‘a boy or child under years is not fit for marriage because he cannot reddere debitum’. So your omnipotentes−

TRUEWIT

1270[To Otter] Your impotentes, you whoreson lobster.

OTTER

1271Your impotentes, I should say, are minime apti ad contrahenda matrimonium.

TRUEWIT

1272[To Otter] Matrimonium? We shall have most unmatrimonial Latin with you: matrimonia, and be hanged.

DAUPHINE

1273[To Truewit] You put ’em out, man.

CUTBEARD

1274But then there will arise a doubt, master parson, in our case, post matrimonium: that frigiditate praeditus−(do you conceive me, sir?)

OTTER

1275Very well, sir.

CUTBEARD

1276Who cannot uti uxore pro uxore, may habere eam pro sorore.

OTTER

1277Absurd, absurd, absurd, and merely apostatical.

CUTBEARD

1278You shall pardon me, master parson, I can prove it.

OTTER

1279You can prove a will, master doctor, you can prove nothing else. Does not the verse of your own canon say, Haec socianda vetant conubia, facta retractant?

CUTBEARD

1280I grant you, but how do they retractare, master parson?

MOROSE

1281(Oh, this was it I feared.)

OTTER

1282In aeternum, sir.

CUTBEARD

1283That’s false in divinity, by your favour.

OTTER

1284’Tis false in humanity to say so. Is he not prorsus inutilis ad thorum? Can he praestare fidem datam? I would fain know.

CUTBEARD

1285Yes: how if he do convalere?

OTTER

1286He cannot convalere, it is impossible.

TRUEWIT

1287[To Morose] Nay, good sir, attend the learned men; they’ll think you neglect ’em else.

CUTBEARD

1288Or if he do simulare himself frigidum, odio uxoris, or so?

OTTER

1289I say he is adulter manifestus then.

DAUPHINE

1290(They dispute it very learnedly, i’faith.)

OTTER

1291And prostitutor uxoris, and this is positive.

MOROSE

1292Good sir, let me escape.

TRUEWIT

1293You will not do me that wrong, sir?

OTTER

1294And therefore, if he be manifeste frigidus, sir−

CUTBEARD

1295Ay, if he be manifeste frigidus, I grant you−

OTTER

1296Why, that was my conclusion.

CUTBEARD

1297And mine too.

TRUEWIT

1298Nay, hear the conclusion, sir.

OTTER

1299Then frigiditatis causa−

CUTBEARD

1300Yes, causa frigiditatis−

MOROSE

1301Oh, mine ears!

OTTER

1302She may have libellum divortii against you.

CUTBEARD

1303Ay, divortii libellum she will sure have.

MOROSE

1304Good echoes, forbear.

OTTER

1305If you confess it.

CUTBEARD

1306Which I would do, sir−

MOROSE

1307I will do anything−

OTTER

1308And clear myself in foro conscientiae−

CUTBEARD

1309Because you want indeed−

MOROSE

1310Yet more?

OTTER

1311Exercendi potestate.

ACT 5 SCENE 4

[Enter] EPICENE, HAUGHTY, CENTAURE, MAVIS, MISTRESS OTTER, DAW, LA FOOLE.

EPICENE

1312I will not endure it any longer. Ladies, I beseech you help me. This is such a wrong as never was offered to poor bride before. Upon her marriage-day, to have her husband conspire against her, and a couple of mercenary companions to be brought in for form’s sake, to persuade a separation! If you had blood or virtue in you, gentlemen, you would not suffer such earwigs about a husband, or scorpions to creep between man and wife−

MOROSE

1313Oh the variety and changes of my torment!

HAUGHTY

1314Let ’em be cudgelled out of doors by our grooms.

CENTAURE

1315I’ll lend you my footman.

MAVIS

1316We’ll have our men blanket ’em in the hall.

MISTRESS OTTER

1317As there was one at our house, madam, for peeping in at the door.

DAW

1318Content, i’faith.

TRUEWIT

1319Stay, ladies and gentlemen, you’ll hear before you proceed?

MAVIS

1320I’d ha’ the bridegroom blanketed too.

CENTAURE

1321Begin with him first.

HAUGHTY

1322Yes, by my troth.

MOROSE

1323Oh, mankind generation!

DAUPHINE

1324Ladies, for my sake forbear.

HAUGHTY

1325Yes, for Sir Dauphine’s sake.

CENTAURE

1326He shall command us.

LA FOOLE

1327He is as fine a gentleman of his inches, madam, as any is about the town, and wears as good colours when he lists.

TRUEWIT

1328[To Morose] Be brief, sir, and confess your infirmity, she’ll be afire to be quit of you; if she but hear that named once, you shall not entreat her to stay. She’ll fly you like one that had the marks upon him.

MOROSE

1329Ladies, I must crave all your pardons−

TRUEWIT

1330Silence, ladies.

MOROSE

1331For a wrong I have done to your whole sex in marrying this fair and virtuous gentlewoman−

CLERIMONT

1332Hear him, good ladies.

MOROSE

1333Being guilty of an infirmity which, before I conferred with these learned men, I thought I might have concealed−

TRUEWIT

1334But now being better informed in his conscience by them, he is to declare it and give satisfaction by asking your public forgiveness.

MOROSE

1335I am no man, ladies.

ALL

1336How!

MOROSE

1337Utterly unabled in nature, by reason of frigidity, to perform the duties or any the least office of a husband.

MAVIS

1338Now out upon him, prodigious creature!

CENTAURE

1339Bridegroom uncarnate.

HAUGHTY

1340And would you offer it, to a young gentlewoman?

MISTRESS OTTER

1341A lady of her longings?

EPICENE

1342Tut, a device, a device, this, it smells rankly, ladies. A mere comment of his own.

TRUEWIT

1343Why, if you suspect that, ladies, you may have him searched.

DAW

1344As the custom is, by a jury of physicians.

LA FOOLE

1345Yes, faith, ’twill be brave.

MOROSE

1346Oh me, must I undergo that?

MISTRESS OTTER

1347No, let women search him, madam: we can do it ourselves.

MOROSE

1348Out on me, worse!

EPICENE

1349No, ladies, you shall not need; I’ll take him with all his faults.

MOROSE

1350Worst of all!

CLERIMONT

1351Why, then ’tis no divorce, doctor, if she consent not?

CUTBEARD

1352No, if the man be frigidus, it is de parte uxoris that we grant libellum divortii in the law.

OTTER

1353Ay, it is the same in theology.

MOROSE

1354Worse, worse than worst!

TRUEWIT

1355Nay, sir, be not utterly disheartened, we have yet a small relic of hope left, as near as our comfort is blown out. [To Clermimont] Clerimont, produce your brace of knights.− What was that, master parson, you told me in errore qualitatis, e’en now? [To Dauphine] Dauphine, whisper the bride that she carry it as if she were guilty and ashamed.

OTTER

1356Marry, sir, in errore qualitatis (which master doctor did forbear to urge) if she be found corrupta, that is, vitiated or broken up, that was pro virgine desponsa, espoused for a maid−

MOROSE

1357What then, sir?

OTTER

1358It doth dirimere contractum, and irritum reddere too.

TRUEWIT

1359If this be true, we are happy again, sir, once more. Here are an honourable brace of knights that shall affirm so much.

DAW

1360Pardon us, good Master Clerimont.

LA FOOLE

1361You shall excuse us, Master Clerimont.

CLERIMONT

1362Nay, you must make it good now, knights, there is no remedy; I’ll eat no words for you nor no men: you know you spoke it to me?

DAW

1363Is this gentleman-like, sir?

TRUEWIT

1364[To Daw] Jack Daw, he’s worse then Sir Amorous, fiercer a great deal. [To La Foole] Sir Amorous, beware, there be ten Daws in this Clerimont.

LA FOOLE

1365I’ll confess it, sir.

DAW

1366Will you, Sir Amorous? Will you wound reputation?

LA FOOLE

1367I am resolved.

TRUEWIT

1368So should you be too, Jack Daw: what should keep you off? She is but a woman, and in disgrace. He’ll be glad on’t.

DAW

1369Will he? I thought he would ha’ been angry.

CLERIMONT

1370You will dispatch, knights; it must be done, i’faith.

TRUEWIT

1371Why, an’ it must, it shall, sir, they say. They’ll ne’er go back.
[To Daw and La Foole] Do not tempt his patience.

DAW

1372It is true indeed, sir?

LA FOOLE

1373Yes, I assure you, sir.

MOROSE

1374What is true, gentlemen? What do you assure me?

DAW

1375That we have known your bride, sir−

LA FOOLE

1376In good fashion. She was our mistress, or so−

CLERIMONT

1377Nay, you must be plain, knights, as you were to me.

OTTER

1378Ay, the question is, if you have carnaliter or no?

LA FOOLE

1379Carnaliter? What else, sir?

OTTER

1380It is enough: a plain nullity.

EPICENE

1381I am undone, I am undone!

MOROSE

1382Oh, let me worship and adore you, gentlemen!

EPICENE

1383I am undone!

MOROSE

1384Yes, to my hand, I thank these knights. Master parson, let me thank you otherwise.

[Gives Otter money.]

HAUGHTY

1385And ha’ they confessed?

MAVIS

1386Now out upon ’em, informers!

TRUEWIT

1387You see what creatures you may bestow your favours on, madams.

HAUGHTY

1388I would except against ‘em as beaten knights, wench, and not good witnesses in law.

MISTRESS OTTER

1389Poor gentlewoman, how she takes it!

HAUGHTY

1390Be comforted, Morose, I love you the better for’t.

CENTAURE

1391So do I, I protest.

CUTBEARD

1392But, gentlemen, you have not known her since matrimonium?

DAW

1393Not today, master doctor.

LA FOOLE

1394No, sir, not today.

CUTBEARD

1395Why, then I say, for any act before, the matrimonium is good and perfect, unless the worshipful bridegroom did precisely, before witness, demand if she were virgo ante nuptias.

EPICENE

1396No, that he did not, I assure you, master doctor.

CUTBEARD

1397If he cannot prove that, it is ratum conjugium, notwithstanding the premises. And they do no way impedire. And this is my sentence, this I pronounce.

OTTER

1398I am of master doctor’s resolution too, sir, if you made not that demand ante nuptias.

MOROSE

1399O my heart! Wilt thou break? Wilt thou break? This is worst of all worst worsts! that hell could have devised! Marry a whore! and so much noise!

DAUPHINE

1400Come, I see now plain confederacy in this doctor and this parson, to abuse a gentleman. You study his affliction. I pray be gone, companions. And gentlemen, I begin to suspect you for having parts with ’em. Sir, will it please you hear me?

MOROSE

1401Oh, do not talk to me, take not from me the pleasure of dying in silence, nephew.

DAUPHINE

1402Sir, I must speak to you. I have been long your poor despised kinsman, and many a hard thought has strengthened you against me; but now it shall appear if either I love you or your peace, and prefer them to all the world beside. I will not be long or grievous to you, sir. If I free you of this unhappy match absolutely and instantly after all this trouble, and almost in your despair now−

MOROSE

1403(It cannot be.)

DAUPHINE

1404Sir, that you be never troubled with a murmur of it more, what shall I hope for or deserve of you?

MOROSE

1405Oh, what thou wilt, nephew! Thou shalt deserve me and have me.

DAUPHINE

1406Shall I have your favour perfect to me, and love hereafter?

MOROSE

1407That and anything beside. Make thine own conditions. My whole estate is thine. Manage it, I will become thy ward.

DAUPHINE

1408Nay, sir, I will not be so unreasonable.

EPICENE

1409Will sir Dauphine be mine enemy too?

DAUPHINE

1410You know I have been long a suitor to you, uncle, that out of your estate, which is fifteen hundred a year, you would allow me but five hundred during life, and assure the rest upon me after; to which I have often by myself and friends tendered you a writing to sign, which you would never consent or incline to. If you please but to effect it now−

MOROSE

1411Thou shalt have it, nephew. I will do it, and more.

DAUPHINE

1412If I quit you not presently and for ever of this cumber, you shall have power instantly, afore all these, to revoke your act, and I will become whose slave you will give me to for ever.

MOROSE

1413Where is the writing? I will seal to it, that, or to a blank, and write thine own conditions.

EPICENE

1414Oh me, most unfortunate wretched gentlewoman!

HAUGHTY

1415Will Sir Dauphine do this?

EPICENE

1416[Weeps.] Good sir, have some compassion on me.

MOROSE

1417Oh, my nephew knows you belike; away, crocodile!

HAUGHTY

1418He does it not, sure, without good ground.

DAUPHINE

1419Here, sir.

[Gives him papers.]

MOROSE

1420Come, nephew, give me the pen. I will subscribe to anything, and seal to what thou wilt, for my deliverance. Thou art my restorer. Here, I deliver it thee as my deed. If there be a word in it lacking or writ with false orthography, I protest before− I will not take the advantage.

[Returns papers.]

DAUPHINE

1421Then here is your release, sir: He takes off Epicene’s peruke. you have married a boy: a gentleman’s son that I have brought up this half year at my great charges, and for this composition which I have now made with you. What say you, master doctor? This is justum impedimentum, I hope, error personae?

OTTER

1422Yes sir, in primo gradu.

CUTBEARD

1423In primo gradu.

DAUPHINE

1424I thank you, good Doctor Cutbeard and Parson Otter. He pulls off their beards and disguise. You are beholden to ’em, sir, that have taken this pains for you; and my friend, Master Truewit, who enabled ’em for the business. Now you may go in and rest, be as private as you will, sir. I’ll not trouble you till you trouble me with your funeral, which I care not how soon it come. [Exit MOROSE.] Cutbeard, I’ll make your lease good. Thank me not but with your leg, Cutbeard. And Tom Otter, your princess shall be reconciled to you.−How now, gentlemen! Do you look at me?

CLERIMONT

1425A boy.

DAUPHINE

1426Yes, Mistress Epicene.

TRUEWIT

1427Well, Dauphine, you have lurched your friends of the better half of the garland, by concealing this part of the plot! But much good do it thee, thou deserv’st it, lad. And Clerimont, for thy unexpected bringing in these two to confession, wear my part of it freely. Nay, sir Daw and Sir La Foole, you see the gentlewoman that has done you the favours! We are all thankful to you, and so should the womankind here, specially for lying on her, though not with her! You meant so, I am sure? But that we have stuck it upon you today in your own imagined persons, and so lately, this Amazon, the champion of the sex, should beat you now thriftily for the common slanders which ladies receive from such cuckoos as you are. You are they that, when no merit or fortune can make you hope to enjoy their bodies, will yet lie with their reputations and make their fame suffer. Away, you common moths of these and all ladies’ honours. Go, travail to make legs and faces, and come home with some new matter to be laughed at: you deserve to live in an air as corrupted as that wherewith you feed rumour. [Exeunt DAW and LA FOOLE.] Madams, you are mute upon this new metamorphosis! But here stands she that has vindicated your fames. Take heed of such insectae hereafter. And let it not trouble you that you have discovered any mysteries to this young gentleman. He is (a’most) of years, and will make a good visitant within this twelvemonth. In the meantime we’ll all undertake for his secrecy, that can speak so well of his silence. [Coming forward.] Spectators, if you like this comedy, rise cheerfully, and now Morose is gone in, clap your hands. It may be that noise will cure him, at least please him.

[Exeunt.]
