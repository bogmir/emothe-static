Epicoene, or the silent woman

ACT 4

ACT 4 SCENE I

[Enter] TRUEWIT, CLERIMONT.

TRUEWIT

618Was there ever poor bridegroom so tormented? Or man, indeed?

CLERIMONT

619I have not read of the like in the chronicles of the land.

TRUEWIT

620Sure, he cannot but go to a place of rest after all this purgatory.

CLERIMONT

621He may presume it, I think.

TRUEWIT

622The spitting, the coughing, the laughter, the neezing, the farting, dancing, noise of the music, and her masculine and loud commanding and urging the whole family, makes him think he has married a Fury.

CLERIMONT

623And she carries it up bravely.

TRUEWIT

624Ay, she takes any occasion to speak: that’s the height on’t.

CLERIMONT

625And how soberly Dauphine labours to satisfy him that it was none of his plot!

TRUEWIT

626And has almost brought him to the faith i’the article. [Enter DAUPHINE.] Here he comes.−Where is he now? What’s become of him, Dauphine?

DAUPHINE

627Oh, hold me up a little, I shall go away i’the jest else. He has got on his whole nest of nightcaps, and locked himself up i’the top o’the house, as high as ever he can climb from the noise. I peeped in at a cranny and saw him sitting over a cross-beam o’the roof, like him o’ the saddler’s horse in Fleet Street, upright; and he will sleep there.

CLERIMONT

628But where are your Collegiates?

DAUPHINE

629Withdrawn with the bride in private.

TRUEWIT

630Oh, they are instructing her i’the college grammar. If she have grace with them, she knows all their secrets instantly.

CLERIMONT

631Methinks the lady Haughty looks well today, for all my dispraise of her in the morning. I think, I shall come about to thee again, Truewit.

TRUEWIT

632Believe it, I told you right. Women ought to repair the losses time and years have made i’ their features with dressings. And an intelligent woman, if she know by herself the least defect, will be most curious to hide it; and it becomes her. If she be short, let her sit much, lest when she stands she be thought to sit. If she have an ill foot, let her wear her gown the longer, and her shoe the thinner. If a fat hand and scald nails, let her carve the less, and act in gloves. If a sour breath, let her never discourse fasting, and always talk at her distance. If she have black and rugged teeth, let her offer the less at laughter, especially if she laugh wide and open.

CLERIMONT

633Oh, you shall have some women, when they laugh, you would think they brayed, it is so rude, and−

TRUEWIT

634Ay, and others, that will stalk i’ their gait like an ostrich, and take huge strides. I cannot endure such a sight. I love measure i’n the feet and number i’n the voice: they are gentlenesses that oftentimes draw no less than the face.

DAUPHINE

635How cam’st thou to study these creatures so exactly? I would thou wouldst make me a proficient.

TRUEWIT

636Yes, but you must leave to live i’your chamber, then, a month together upon Amadis de Gaula or Don Quixote, as you are wont, and come abroad where the matter is frequent, to court, to tiltings, public shows and feasts, to plays, and church sometimes: thither they come to show their new tires too, to see, and to be seen. In these places a man shall find whom to love, whom to play with, whom to touch once, whom to hold ever. The variety arrests his judgment. A wench to please a man comes not down dropping from the ceiling, as he lies on his back droning a tobacco-pipe. He must go where she is.

DAUPHINE

637Yes, and be never the near.

TRUEWIT

638Out, heretic! That diffidence makes thee worthy it should be so.

CLERIMONT

639He says true to you, Dauphine.

DAUPHINE

640Why?

TRUEWIT

641A man should not doubt to overcome any woman. Think he can vanquish’em, and he shall; for though they deny, their desire is to be tempted. Penelope herself cannot hold out long. Ostend, you saw, was taken at last. You must persever and hold to your purpose. They would solicit us, but that they are afraid. Howsoever, they wish in their hearts we should solicit them. Praise’em, flatter ’em, you shall never want eloquence or trust; even the chastest delight to feel themselves that way rubbed. With praises you must mix kisses too. If they take them, they’ll take more. Though they strive, they would be overcome.

CLERIMONT

642Oh, but a man must beware of force.

TRUEWIT

643It is to them an acceptable violence, and has ofttimes the place of the greatest courtesy. She that might have been forced, and you let her go free without touching, though then she seem to thank you, will ever hate you after; and glad i’ the face, is assuredly sad at the heart.

CLERIMONT

644But all women are not to be taken all ways.

TRUEWIT

645’Tis true. No more than all birds or all fishes. If you appear learned to an ignorant wench, or jocund to a sad, or witty to a foolish, why, she presently begins to mistrust herself. You must approach them i’ their own height, their own line; for the contrary makes many that fear to commit themselves to noble and worthy fellows run into the embraces of a rascal. If she love wit, give verses, though you borrow ’em of a friend, or buy ’em, to have good. If valour, talk of your sword, and be frequent in the mention of quarrels, though you be staunch in fighting. If activity, be seen o’ your barbary often, or leaping over stools, for the credit of your back. If she love good clothes or dressing, have your learned council about you every morning, your French tailor, barber, linener, etcetera. Let your powder, your glass, and your comb be your dearest acquaintance. Take more care for the ornament of your head than the safety; and wish the commonwealth rather troubled than a hair about you. That will take her. Then, if she be covetous and craving, do you promise anything, and perform sparingly; so shall you keep her in appetite still. Seem as you would give, but be like a barren field that fields little, or unlucky dice to foolish and hoping gamesters. Let your gifts be slight and dainty, rather than precious. Let cunning be above cost. Give cherries at time of year, or apricots; and say they were sent you out o’ the country, though you bought ’em in Cheapside. Admire her tires; like her in all fashions, compare her in every habit to some deity, invent excellent dreams to flatter her, and riddles; or, if she be a great one, perform always the second parts to her: like what she likes, praise whom she praises, and fail not to make the household and servants yours, yea, the whole family, and salute ’em by their names (’tis but light cost if you can purchase ’em so) and make her physician your pensioner, and her chief woman. Nor will it be out of your gain to make love to her too, so she follow, not usher, her lady’s pleasure. All blabbing is taken away when she comes to be a part of the crime.

DAUPHINE

646On what courtly lap hast thou late slept, to come forth so sudden and absolute a courtling?

TRUEWIT

647Good faith, I should rather question you, that are so harkening after these mysteries. I begin to suspect your diligence, Dauphine. Speak, art thou in love in earnest?

DAUPHINE

648Yes, by my troth am I; ’twere ill dissembling before thee.

TRUEWIT

649With which of ’em, I pray you?

DAUPHINE

650With all the Collegiates.

CLERIMONT

651Out on thee. We’ll keep you at home, believe it, i’ the stable, an’ you be such a stallion.

TRUEWIT

652No; I like him well. Men should love wisely, and all women: some one for the face, and let her please the eye; another for the skin, and let her please the touch; and third for the voice, and let her please the ear; and where the objects mix, let the senses so too. Thou wouldst think it strange if I should make ’em all in love with thee afore night!

DAUPHINE

653I would say thou hadst the best philtre i’ the world, and couldst do more than Madam Medea or Doctor Forman.

TRUEWIT

654If I do not, let me play the mountebank for my meat while I live, and the bawd for my drink.

DAUPHINE

655So be it, I say.

ACT 4 SCENE 2

[Enter] OTTER, [with his cups], DAW, and LA FOOLE.

OTTER

656Oh lord, gentlemen, how my knights and I have missed you here!

CLERIMONT

657Why, captain, what service, what service?

OTTER

658To see me bring up my bull, bear and horse to fight.

DAW

659Yes, faith, the captain says we shall be his dogs to bait ’em.

DAUPHINE

660A good employment.

TRUEWIT

661Come on, let’s see a course then.

LA FOOLE

662I am afraid my cousin will be offended if she come.

OTTER

663Be afraid of nothing. Gentlemen, I have placed the drum and the trumpets, and one to give them the sign when you are ready. Here’s my bull for myself, and my bear for Sir John Daw, and my horse for Sir Amorous. Now set your foot to mine, and yours to his, and−

LA FOOLE

664Pray God my cousin come not.

OTTER

665Saint George and Saint Andrew, fear no cousins. Come, sound, sound. Et rauco strepuerunt cornua cantu.

[Drum and trumpets sound. They drink.]

TRUEWIT

666Well said, captain, i’faith; well fought at the bull.

CLERIMONT

667Well held at the bear.

TRUEWIT

668’Loo, ’loo, captain!

DAUPHINE

669Oh, the horse has kicked off his dog already.

LA FOOLE

670I cannot drink it, as I am a knight.

TRUEWIT

671Gods so! Off with his spurs, somebody.

LA FOOLE

672It goes against my conscience. My cousin will be angry with it.

DAW

673I ha’ done mine.

TRUEWIT

674You fought high and fair, Sir John.

CLERIMONT

675At the head.

DAUPHINE

676Like an excellent bear-dog.

CLERIMONT

677[To Daw] You take no notice of the business, I hope.

DAW

678[To Clerimont] Not a word, sir; you see we are jovial.

OTTER

679Sir Amorous, you must not equivocate. It must be pulled down, for all my cousin.

CLERIMONT

680[To La Foole.] ‘Sfoot, if you take not your drink, they’ll think you are discontented with something; you’ll betray all if you take the least notice.

LA FOOLE

681[To Clerimont] Not I; I’ll both drink and talk then.

OTTER

682You must pull the horse on his knees, Sir Amorous. Fear no cousins. Iacta est alea.

TRUEWIT

683[To Dauphine and Clerimont] Oh, now he’s in his vein, and bold. The least hint given him of his wife now will make him rail desperately.

CLERIMONT

684Speak to him of her.

TRUEWIT

685Do you, and I’ll fetch her to the hearing of it.

[Exit.]

DAUPHINE

686Captain he-Otter, your she-Otter is coming, your wife.

OTTER

687Wife! buz! Titivilitium. There’s no such thing in nature. I confess, gentlemen, I have a cook, a laundress, a housedrudge, that serves my necessary turns and goes under that title; but he’s an ass that will be so uxorious to tie his affections to one circle. Come, the name dulls appetite. Here, replenish again: another bout. Wives are nasty sluttish animals.

[He fills the cups again]

DAUPHINE

688Oh, captain!

OTTER

689As ever the earth bare, tribus verbis. Where’s Master Truewit?

DAW

690He’s slipped aside, sir.

CLERIMONT

691But you must drink and be jovial.

DAW

692Yes, give it me.

LA FOOLE

693And me too.

DAW

694Let’s be jovial.

LA FOOLE

695As jovial as you will.

OTTER

696Agreed. Now you shall ha’ the bear, cousin, and Sir John Daw the horse, and I’ll ha’ the bull still. Sound, Tritons o’ the Thames. Nunc est bibendum, nunc pede libero−

[They drink.]MOROSE speaks from above. The trumpets sounding.

MOROSE

697Villains, murderers, sons of the earth and traitors, what do you there?

CLERIMONT

698Oh, now the trumpets have waked him we shall have his company.

OTTER

699A wife is a scurvy clogdogdo, an unlucky thing, a very foresaid bear-whelp, without any good fashion or breeding: mala bestia.

His wife is brought out to hear him [by TRUEWIT].

DAUPHINE

700Why did you marry one then, captain?

OTTER

701A pox!−I married with six thousand pound, I. I was in love with that. I ha’ not kissed my Fury these forty weeks.

CLERIMONT

702The more to blame you, captain.

TRUEWIT

703Nay, Mistress Otter, hear him a little first.

OTTER

704She has a breath worse than my grandmother’s, profecto.

MISTRESS OTTER

705Oh treacherous liar! Kiss me, sweet Master Truewit, and prove him a slandering knave.

TRUEWIT

706I’ll rather believe you, lady.

OTTER

707And she has a peruke that’s like a pound of hemp made up in shoe-threads.

MISTRESS OTTER

708Oh, viper, mandrake!

OTTER

709A most vile face! And yet she spends me forty pound a year in mercury and hogs’ bones. All her teeth were made i’ the Blackfriars, both her eyebrows in the Strand, and her hair in Silver Street. Every part o’ the town owns a piece of her.

MISTRESS OTTER

710I cannot hold.

OTTER

711She takes herself asunder still when she goes to bed, into some twenty boxes, and about next day noon is put together again, like a great German clock; and so comes forth and rings a tedious larum to the whole house, and then is quiet again for an hour, but for her quarters.− Ha’ you done me right, gentlemen?

MISTRESS OTTER

712No, sir, I’ll do you right with my quarters, with my quarters.

She falls upon him ad beats him.

OTTER

713Oh, hold, good princess.

TRUEWIT

714Sound, sound!

[Drum and trumpets sound.]

CLERIMONT

715A battle, a battle!

MISTRESS OTTER

716You notorious stinkardly bearward, does my breath smell?

OTTER

717Under correction, dear princess. Look to my bear and my horse, gentlemen.

MISTRESS OTTER

718Do I want teeth and eyebrows, thou bull-dog?

TRUEWIT

719Sound, sound still.

[They sound again.]

OTTER

720No, I protest, under correction−

MISTRESS OTTER

721Ay, now you are under correction, you protest; but you did not protest before correction, sir. Thou Judas, to offer to betray thy princess! I’ll make thee an example−

MOROSE descends with a long sword.

MOROSE

722I will have no such examples in my house, Lady Otter.

MISTRESS OTTER

723Ah!

[She runs off, with DAW and LA FOOLE.]

MOROSE

724Mistress Mary Ambree, your examples are dangerous.−Rogues, hell-hounds, Stentors, out of my doors, you sons of noise and tumult, begot on an ill May-day, or when the galley-foist is afloat to Westminster! A trumpeter could not be conceived but then!

[Drives out the musicians.]

DAUPHINE

725What ails you, sir?

MOROSE

726They have rent my roof, walls and all my windows asunder, with their brazen throats.

[Exit.]

TRUEWIT

727Best follow him, Dauphine.

DAUPHINE

728So I will.

[Exit.]

CLERIMONT

729Where’s Daw and La Foole?

OTTER

730They are both run away, sir. Good gentlemen, help to pacify my princess, and speak to the great ladies for me. Now must I go lie with the bears this fortnight, and keep out o’ the way till my peace be made, for this scandal she has taken. Did you not see my bull-head, gentlemen?

CLERIMONT

731Is’t not on, captain?

TRUEWIT

732No:− [To Clerimont] but he may make a new one, by that is on.

OTTER

733Oh, here ‘tis. An you come over, gentlemen, and ask for Tom Otter, we’ll go down to Ratcliffe, and have a course i’faith, for all these disasters. There’s bona spes left.

TRUEWIT

734Away, captain, get off while you are well.

[Exit OTTER.]

CLERIMONT

735I am glad we are rid of him.

TRUEWIT

736You had never been, unless we had put his wife upon him. His humour is as tedious at last, as it was ridiculous at first.

ACT 4 SCENE 3

[Enter] HAUGHTY, MISTRESS OTTER, MAVIS, DAW, LA FOOLE, CENTAURE, AND EPICENE. [TRUEWIT and CLERIMONT observe them.]

HAUGHTY

737We wondered why you shrieked so, Mistress Otter.

MISTRESS OTTER

738O God, madam, he came down with a huge long naked weapon in both his hands, and looked so dreadfully! Sure he’s beside himself.

MAVIS

739Why, what made you there, Mistress Otter?

MISTRESS OTTER

740Alas, Mistress Mavis, I was chastising my subject, and thought nothing of him.

DAW

741[To Epicene] Faith, mistress, you must do so too. Learn to chastise. Mistress Otter corrects her husband so, he dares not speak but under correction.

LA FOOLE

742And with his hat off to her: ’twould do you good to see.

HAUGHTY

743In sadness, ’tis good and mature counsel: practice it, Morose. I’ll call you Morose still now, as I call Centaure and Mavis: we four will be all one.

CENTAURE

744And you’ll come to the college and live with us?

HAUGHTY

745Make him give milk and honey.

MAVIS

746Look how you manage him at first, you shall have him ever after.

CENTAURE

747Let him allow you your coach and four horses, your woman, your chambermaid, your page, your gentleman-usher, your French cook, and four grooms.

HAUGHTY

748And go with us to Bedlam, to the china-houses, and to the Exchange.

CENTAURE

749It will open the gate to your fame.

HAUGHTY

750Here’s Centaure has immortalised herself with taming of her wild male.

MAVIS

751Ay, she has done the miracle of the kingdom.

EPICENE

752But ladies, do you count it lawful to have such plurality of servants, and do ’em all graces?

HAUGHTY

753Why not? Why should women deny their favours to men? Are they the poorer, or the worse?

DAW

754Is the Thames the less for the dyer’s water, mistress?

LA FOOLE

755Or a torch for lighting many torches?

TRUEWIT

756[Aside] Well said, La Foole; what a new one he has got!

CENTAURE

757They are empty losses women fear in this kind.

HAUGHTY

758Besides, ladies should be mindful of the approach of age, and let no time want his due use. The best of our days pass first.

MAVIS

759We are rivers, that cannot be called back, madam: she that now excludes her lovers may live to lie a forsaken beldame in a frozen bed.

CENTAURE

760’Tis true, Mavis. And who will wait on us to coach then? or write, or tell us the news then? Make anagrams of our names, and invite us to the cockpit, and kiss our hands all the play-time, and draw their weapons for our honours?

HAUGHTY

761Not one.

DAW

762Nay, my mistress is not altogether unintelligent of these things; here be in presence have tasted of her favours.

TRUEWIT

763[Aside] What a neighing hobby-horse is this!

EPICENE

764But not with intent to boast ’em again, servant. And have you those excellent receipts, madam, to keep yourselves from bearing of children?

HAUGHTY

765Oh yes, Morose. How should we maintain our youth and beauty else? Many births of a woman make her old, as many crops make the earth barren.

ACT 4 SCENE 4

[Enter] MOROSE and DAUPHINE [who speak apart.]

MOROSE

766Oh my cursed angel, that instructed me to this fate!

DAUPHINE

767Why, sir?

MOROSE

768That I should be seduced by so foolish a devil as a barber will make!

DAUPHINE

769I would I had been worthy, sir, to have partaken your counsel; you should never have trusted it to such a minister.

MOROSE

770Would I could redeem it with the loss of an eye (nephew), a hand, or any other member.

DAUPHINE

771Marry, God forbid, sir, that you should geld yourself to anger your wife.

MOROSE

772So it would rid me of her! And that I did supererogatory penance, in a belfry, at Westminster Hall, i’n the Cockpit, at the fall of a stag, the Tower Wharf (what place is there else?) London Bridge, Paris Garden, Billinsgate, when the noises are at their height and loudest. Nay, I would sit out a play, that were nothing but fights at sea, drum, trumpet and target!

DAUPHINE

773I hope there shall be no such need, sir. Take patience, good uncle. This is but a day, and ’tis well worn too now.

MOROSE

774O, ’twill be so for ever, nephew, I foresee it, for ever. Strife and tumult are the dowry that comes with a wife.

TRUEWIT

775I told you so, sir, and you would not believe me.

MOROSE

776Alas, do not rub those wounds, Master Truewit, to blood again; ’twas my negligence. Add not affliction to affliction. I have perceived the effect of it, too late, in Madam Otter.

EPICENE

777[Coming forward] How do you, sir?

MOROSE

778Did you ever hear a more unnecessary question? As if she did not see! Why, I do as you see, empress, empress.

EPICENE

779You are not well, sir! You look very ill! Something has distempered you.

MOROSE

780Oh horrible, monstrous impertinencies! Would not one of these have served? Do you think, sir? Would not one of these have served?

TRUEWIT

781Yes, sir, but these are but notes of female kindness, sir; certain tokens that she has a voice, sir.

MOROSE

782Oh, is’t so? Come, and’t be no otherwise−what say you?

EPICENE

783How do you feel yourself, sir?

MOROSE

784Again that!

TRUEWIT

785Nay, look you, sir: you would be friends with your wife upon unconscionable terms, her silence−

EPICENE

786They say you are run mad, sir.

MOROSE

787Not for love, I assure you, of you; do you see?

EPICENE

788O lord, gentlemen! Lay hold on him for God’s sake. What shall I do? Who’s his physician (can you tell) that knows the state of his body best, that I might send for him? Good sir, speak. I’ll send for one of my doctors else.

MOROSE

789What, to poison me, that I might die intestate and leave you possessed of all?

EPICENE

790Lord, how idly he talks, and how his eyes sparkle! He looks green about the temples! Do you see what blue spots he has?

TRUEWIT

791Ay, it’s melancholy.

EPICENE

792Gentlemen, for heaven’s sake counsel me. Ladies! Servant, you have read Pliny and Paracelsus: ne’er a word now to comfort a poor gentlewoman? Ay me! What fortune had I to marry a distracted man?

DAW

793I’ll tell you, mistress−

TRUEWIT

794[To Clerimont] How rarely she holds it up!

[TRUEWIT and CLERIMONT prevent MOROSE from leaving.]

MOROSE

795What mean you, gentlemen?

EPICENE

796What will you tell me, servant?

DAW

797The disease in Greek is called mania, in Latin insania, furor, vel ecstasis melancholica, that is, egressio, when a man ex melancholico evadit fanaticus.

MOROSE

798Shall I have a lecture read upon me alive?

DAW

799But he may be but phreneticus yet, mistress, and phrenetis is only delirium, or so−

EPICENE

800Ay, that is for the disease, servant; but what is this to the cure? We are sure enough of the disease.

MOROSE

801Let me go!

TRUEWIT

802Why, we’ll entreat her to hold her peace, sir.

MOROSE

803Oh no, labour not to stop her. She is like a conduit-pipe, that will gush out with more force when she opens again.

HAUGHTY

804I’ll tell you, Morose, you must talk divinity to him altogether, or moral philosophy.

LA FOOLE

805Ay, and there’s an excellent book of moral philosophy, madam, of Reynard the Fox and all the beasts, called Doni’s Philosophy.

CENTAURE

806There is, indeed, sir Amorous La Foole.

MOROSE

807Oh misery!

LA FOOLE

808I have read it, my lady Centaure, all over to my cousin, here.

MISTRESS OTTER

809Ay, and ’tis a very good book as any is of the moderns.

DAW

810Tut, he must have Seneca read to him, and Plutarch and the ancients; the moderns are not for this disease.

CLERIMONT

811Why, you discommended them too, today, Sir John.

DAW

812Ay, in some cases; but in these they are best, and Aristotle’s Ethics.

MAVIS

813Say you so sir John? I think you are deceived: you took it upon trust.

HAUGHTY

814Where’s Trusty, my woman? I’ll end this difference. I prithee, Otter, call her. Her father and mother were both mad when they put her to me.

[Exit MISTRESS OTTER.]

MOROSE

815I think so.−Nay, gentlemen, I am tame. This is but an exercise, I know, a marriage ceremony, which I must endure.

HAUGHTY

816And one of ’em (I know not which) was cured with the Sick Man’s Salve; and the other with Greene’s Groat’s-worth of Wit.

TRUEWIT

817A very cheap cure, madam.

HAUGHTY

818Ay, it’s very feasible.

[Enter MISTRESS OTTER with TRUSTY.]

MISTRESS OTTER

819My lady called for you, Mistress Trusty; you must decide a controversy.

HAUGHTY

820Oh, Trusty, which was it you said, your father or your mother, that was cured with the Sick Man’s Salve?

TRUSTY

821My mother, madam, with the Salve.

TRUEWIT

822Then it was The sick woman’s Salve?

TRUSTY

823And my father with The Groat’s-worth of Wit. But there was other means used: we had a preacher that would preach folk asleep still; and so they were prescribed to go to church by an old woman that was their physician, thrice a week−

EPICENE

824To sleep?

TRUSTY

825Yes, forsooth; and every night they read themselves asleep on those books.

EPICENE

826Good faith, it stands with great reason. I would I knew where to procure those books.

MOROSE

827Oh!

LA FOOLE

828I can help you with one of ’em, Mistress Morose, the Groat’s-worth of Wit.

EPICENE

829But I shall disfurnish you, Sir Amorous. Can you spare it?

LA FOOLE

830Oh, yes, for a week or so; I’ll read it myself to him.

EPICENE

831No, I must do that, sir; that must be my office.

MOROSE

832Oh, oh!

EPICENE

833Sure, he would do well enough, if he could sleep.

MOROSE

834No, I should do well enough, if you could sleep. Have I no friend that will make her drunk? or give her a little laudanum? or opium?

TRUEWIT

835Why, sir, she talks ten times worse in her sleep.

MOROSE

836How!

CLERIMONT

837Do you not know that, sir? Never ceases all night.

TRUEWIT

838And snores like a porpoise.

MOROSE

MISTRESS OTTER

1078Oh me! Madam, he comes again, the madman! Away!

[Exeunt hastily HAUGHTY, CENTAURE, EPICENE, MAVIS, MISTRESS OTTER, TRUSTY, DAW and LA FOOLE.]

ACT 4 SCENE 7

[Enter] MOROSE [with a sword in each hand;] he had found the two swords drawn within.

MOROSE

1079What make these naked weapons here, gentlemen?

TRUEWIT

1080Oh, sir! Here hath like to been murder since you went! A couple of knights fallen out about the bride’s favours. We were fain to take away their weapons, your house had been begged by this time else−

MOROSE

1081For what?

CLERIMONT

1082For manslaughter, sir, as being accessary.

MOROSE

1083And for her favours?

TRUEWIT

1084Ay, sir, heretofore, not present. Clerimont, carry ’em their swords now. They have done all the hurt they will do.

[Exit CLERIMONT with the two swords]

DAUPHINE

1085Ha’you spoke with the lawyer, sir?

MOROSE

1086Oh no! There is such a noise i’ the court that they have frighted me home with more violence then I went! Such speaking and counter-speaking, with their several voices of citations, appellations, allegations, certificates, attachments, intergatories, references, convictions, and afflictions indeed, among the doctors and proctors, that the noise here is silence to’t! A kind of calm midnight!

TRUEWIT

1087Why, sir, if you would be resolved indeed, I can bring you hither a very sufficient lawyer and a learned divine, that shall enquire into every least scruple for you.

MOROSE

1088Can you, Master Truewit?

TRUEWIT

1089Yes, and are very sober grave persons, that will dispatch it in a chamber, with a whisper or two.

MOROSE

1090Good sir, shall I hope this benefit from you, and trust myself into your hands?

TRUEWIT

1091Alas, sir! Your nephew and I have been ashamed, and oft-times mad, since you went, to think how you are abused. Go in, good sir, and lock yourself up till we call you; we’ll tell you more anon, sir.

MOROSE

1092Do your pleasure with me gentlemen; I believe in you, and that deserves no delusion−

TRUEWIT

1093You shall find none, sir− [Exit Morose] but heaped, heaped plenty of vexation.

DAUPHINE

1094What wilt thou do now, Wit?

TRUEWIT

1095Recover me hither Otter and the barber if you can, by any means, presently.

DAUPHINE

1096Why? To what purpose?

TRUEWIT

1097Oh, I’ll make the deepest divine and gravest lawyer out o’ them two for him−

DAUPHINE

1098Thou canst not, man; these are waking dreams.

TRUEWIT

1099Do not fear me. Clap but a civil gown with a welt o’ the one, and a canonical cloak with sleeves o’ the other, and give ’em a few terms i’ their mouths; if there come not forth as able a doctor and complete a parson for this turn as may be wished, trust not my election. And I hope, without wronging the dignity of either profession, since they are but persons put on, and for mirth’s sake, to torment him. The barber smatters Latin, I remember.

DAUPHINE

1100Yes, and Otter too.

TRUEWIT

1101Well then, if I make ’em not wrangle out this case to his no comfort, let me be thought a Jack Daw, or La Foole, or anything worse. Go you to your ladies, but first send for them.

DAUPHINE

1102I will.

[Exeunt.]
