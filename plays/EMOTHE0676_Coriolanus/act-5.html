Act 5

⸢Scene 1⸣

Enter Menenius, Cominius, Sicinius, Brutus (the two Tribunes), with others.

MENENIUS

1No, I’ll not go. You hear what he hath said
2Which was sometime his general, who loved him
3In a most dear particular. He called me father,
4But what o’ that? Go you that banished him;
5A mile before his tent, fall down, and knee
6The way into his mercy. Nay, if he coyed
7To hear Cominius speak, I’ll keep at home.

COMINIUS

8He would not seem to know me.

MENENIUS

He would not seem to know me. Do you hear?

COMINIUS

9Yet one time he did call me by my name.
10I urged our old acquaintance, and the drops
11That we have bled together. “Coriolanus”
12He would not answer to, forbade all names.
13He was a kind of nothing, titleless,
14Till he had forged himself a name o’ th’ fire
15Of burning Rome.

MENENIUS

Of burning Rome. ⸢to the Tribunes⸣ Why, so; you have made good work!
16A pair of tribunes that have wracked Rome
17To make coals cheap! A noble memory!

COMINIUS

18I minded him how royal ’twas to pardon
19When it was less expected. He replied
20It was a bare petition of a state
21To one whom they had punished.

MENENIUS

To one whom they had punished. Very well.
22Could he say less?

COMINIUS

23I offered to awaken his regard
24For ’s private friends. His answer to me was
25He could not stay to pick them in a pile
26Of noisome musty chaff. He said ’twas folly
27For one poor grain or two to leave unburnt
28And still to nose th’ offense.

MENENIUS

For one poor grain or two!

30I am one of those! His mother, wife, his child,
31And this brave fellow too, we are the grains;
32You are the musty chaff, and you are smelt
33Above the moon. We must be burnt for you.

SICINIUS

34Nay, pray, be patient. If you refuse your aid
35In this so-never-needed help, yet do not
36Upbraid ’s with our distress. But sure, if you
37Would be your country’s pleader, your good tongue,
38More than the instant army we can make,
39Might stop our countryman.

MENENIUS

Might stop our countryman. No, I’ll not meddle.

SICINIUS

Pray you, go to him.

MENENIUS

What should I do?

BRUTUS

42Only make trial what your love can do
43For Rome, towards Martius.

MENENIUS

For Rome, towards Martius. Well, and say that Martius
44Return me, as Cominius is returned, unheard,
45What then? But as a discontented friend,
46Grief-shot with his unkindness? Say ’t be so?

SICINIUS

Yet your good will

48Must have that thanks from Rome after the measure
49As you intended well.

MENENIUS

As you intended well. I’ll undertake ’t.
50I think he’ll hear me. Yet to bite his lip
51And hum at good Cominius much unhearts me.
52He was not taken well; he had not dined.
53The veins unfilled, our blood is cold, and then
54We pout upon the morning, are unapt
55To give or to forgive; but when we have stuffed
56These pipes and these conveyances of our blood
57With wine and feeding, we have suppler souls
58Than in our priestlike fasts. Therefore I’ll watch him
59Till he be dieted to my request,
60And then I’ll set upon him.

BRUTUS

61You know the very road into his kindness
62And cannot lose your way.

MENENIUS

And cannot lose your way. Good faith, I’ll prove him,
63Speed how it will. I shall ere long have knowledge
64Of my success.

COMINIUS

Of my success. He exits. He’ll never hear him.

SICINIUS

Of my success. He exits. He’ll never hear him. Not?

COMINIUS

65I tell you, he does sit in gold, his eye
66Red as ’twould burn Rome; and his injury
67The jailor to his pity. I kneeled before him;
68’Twas very faintly he said “Rise”; dismissed me
69Thus with his speechless hand. What he would do
70He sent in writing after me; what he
71Would not, bound with an oath to yield to his
72Conditions. So that all hope is vain
73Unless his noble mother and his wife,
74Who, as I hear, mean to solicit him
75For mercy to his country. Therefore let’s hence
76And with our fair entreaties haste them on.

They exit.

⸢Scene 2⸣

Enter Menenius to the Watch, or Guard.

FIRST WATCH

Stay! Whence are you?

SECOND WATCH

Stand, and go back.

MENENIUS

3You guard like men; ’tis well. But by your leave,
4I am an officer of state and come
5To speak with Coriolanus.

FIRST WATCH

From whence?

MENENIUS

From Rome.

FIRST WATCH

8You may not pass; you must return. Our general
9Will no more hear from thence.

SECOND WATCH

10You’ll see your Rome embraced with fire before
11You’ll speak with Coriolanus.

MENENIUS

You’ll speak with Coriolanus. Good my friends,
12If you have heard your general talk of Rome
13And of his friends there, it is lots to blanks
14My name hath touched your ears. It is Menenius.

FIRST WATCH

15Be it so; go back. The virtue of your name
16Is not here passable.

MENENIUS

Is not here passable. I tell thee, fellow,
17Thy general is my lover. I have been
18The book of his good acts, whence men have read
19His fame unparalleled happily amplified;
20For I have ever verified my friends—
21Of whom he’s chief—with all the size that verity
22Would without lapsing suffer. Nay, sometimes,
23Like to a bowl upon a subtle ground,
24I have tumbled past the throw, and in his praise
25Have almost stamped the leasing. Therefore, fellow,
26I must have leave to pass.

FIRST WATCH

Faith, sir, if you had told as many lies in his behalf as you have uttered words in your own, you should not pass here, no, though it were as virtuous to lie as to live chastely. Therefore, go back.

MENENIUS

Prithee, fellow, remember my name is Menenius, always factionary on the party of your general.

SECOND WATCH

Howsoever you have been his liar, as you say you have, I am one that, telling true under him, must say you cannot pass. Therefore, go back.

MENENIUS

Has he dined, can’st thou tell? For I would not speak with him till after dinner.

FIRST WATCH

You are a Roman, are you?

MENENIUS

I am, as thy general is.

FIRST WATCH

Then you should hate Rome as he does. Can you, when you have pushed out your gates the very defender of them, and, in a violent popular ignorance given your enemy your shield, think to front his revenges with the easy groans of old women, the virginal palms of your daughters, or with the palsied intercession of such a decayed dotant as you seem to be? Can you think to blow out the intended fire your city is ready to flame in with such weak breath as this? No, you are deceived. Therefore, back to Rome and prepare for your execution. You are condemned. Our general has sworn you out of reprieve and pardon.

MENENIUS

Sirrah, if thy captain knew I were here, he would use me with estimation.

FIRST WATCH

Come, my captain knows you not.

MENENIUS

I mean thy general.

FIRST WATCH

My general cares not for you. Back, I say, go, lest I let forth your half pint of blood. Back! That’s the utmost of your having. Back!

MENENIUS

Nay, but fellow, fellow—

Enter Coriolanus with Aufidius.

CORIOLANUS

What’s the matter?

MENENIUS

⸢to First Watch⸣ Now, you companion, I’ll say an errand for you. You shall know now that I am in estimation; you shall perceive that a Jack guardant cannot office me from my son Coriolanus. Guess but ⸢by⸣ my entertainment with him if thou stand’st not i’ th’ state of hanging or of some death more long in spectatorship and crueler in suffering; behold now presently, and swoon for what’s to come upon thee. ⸢(To Coriolanus.)⸣ The glorious gods sit in hourly synod about thy particular prosperity and love thee no worse than thy old father Menenius does! O my son, my son! ⸢(He weeps.)⸣ Thou art preparing fire for us; look thee, here’s water to quench it. I was hardly moved to come to thee; but being assured none but myself could move thee, I have been blown out of your gates with sighs, and conjure thee to pardon Rome and thy petitionary countrymen. The good gods assuage thy wrath and turn the dregs of it upon this varlet here, this, who, like a block, hath denied my access to thee.

CORIOLANUS

Away!

MENENIUS

How? Away?

CORIOLANUS

45Wife, mother, child, I know not. My affairs
46Are servanted to others. Though I owe
47My revenge properly, my remission lies
48In Volscian breasts. That we have been familiar,
49Ingrate forgetfulness shall poison rather
50Than pity note how much. Therefore, begone.
51Mine ears against your suits are stronger than
52Your gates against my force. Yet, for I loved thee,
53Take this along; I writ it for thy sake,

⸢He gives Menenius a paper.⸣

54And would have sent it. Another word, Menenius,
55I will not hear thee speak.—This man, Aufidius,
56Was my beloved in Rome; yet thou behold’st.

They exit.

AUFIDIUS

You keep a constant temper.

The Guard and Menenius remain.

FIRST WATCH

Now, sir, is your name Menenius?

SECOND WATCH

’Tis a spell, you see, of much power. You know the way home again.

FIRST WATCH

Do you hear how we are shent for keeping your Greatness back?

SECOND WATCH

What cause do you think I have to swoon?

He exits.

MENENIUS

I neither care for th’ world nor your general. For such things as you, I can scarce think there’s any, you’re so slight. He that hath a will to die by himself fears it not from another. Let your general do his worst. For you, be that you are, long; and your misery increase with your age! I say to you, as I was said to, away!

FIRST WATCH

A noble fellow, I warrant him.

SECOND WATCH

The worthy fellow is our general. He’s the rock, the oak not to be wind-shaken.

Watch exit.

⸢Scene 3⸣

Enter Coriolanus and Aufidius.

CORIOLANUS

1We will before the walls of Rome tomorrow
2Set down our host. My partner in this action,
3You must report to th’ Volscian lords how plainly
4I have borne this business.

AUFIDIUS

I have borne this business. Only their ends
5You have respected, stopped your ears against
6The general suit of Rome, never admitted
7A private whisper, no, not with such friends
8That thought them sure of you.

CORIOLANUS

That thought them sure of you. This last old man,
9Whom with a cracked heart I have sent to Rome,
10Loved me above the measure of a father,
11Nay, godded me indeed. Their latest refuge
12Was to send him, for whose old love I have—
13Though I showed sourly to him—once more offered
14The first conditions, which they did refuse
15And cannot now accept, to grace him only
16That thought he could do more. A very little
17I have yielded to. Fresh embassies and suits,
18Nor from the state nor private friends, hereafter
19Will I lend ear to.

(Shout within.)

Will I lend ear to. Ha? What shout is this?
20Shall I be tempted to infringe my vow
21In the same time ’tis made? I will not.

Enter Virgilia, Volumnia, Valeria, young Martius, with Attendants.

22My wife comes foremost, then the honored mold
23Wherein this trunk was framed, and in her hand
24The grandchild to her blood. But out, affection!
25All bond and privilege of nature, break!
26Let it be virtuous to be obstinate.

⸢Virgilia curtsies.⸣

27What is that curtsy worth? Or those doves’ eyes,
28Which can make gods forsworn? I melt and am not
29Of stronger earth than others.
Of stronger earth than others. ⸢Volumnia bows.⸣ My mother bows,
30As if Olympus to a molehill should
31In supplication nod; and my young boy
32Hath an aspect of intercession which
33Great Nature cries “Deny not!” Let the Volsces
34Plow Rome and harrow Italy, I’ll never
35Be such a gosling to obey instinct, but stand
36As if a man were author of himself,
37And knew no other kin.

VIRGILIA

And knew no other kin. My lord and husband.

CORIOLANUS

38These eyes are not the same I wore in Rome.

VIRGILIA

39The sorrow that delivers us thus changed
40Makes you think so.

CORIOLANUS

Makes you think so. Like a dull actor now,
41I have forgot my part, and I am out,
42Even to a full disgrace. Best of my flesh,
43Forgive my tyranny, but do not say
44For that “Forgive our Romans.”
For that “Forgive our Romans.” ⸢They kiss.⸣ O, a kiss
45Long as my exile, sweet as my revenge!
46Now, by the jealous queen of heaven, that kiss
47I carried from thee, dear, and my true lip
48Hath virgined it e’er since. You gods! I ⸢prate⸣
49And the most noble mother of the world
50Leave unsaluted. Sink, my knee, i’ th’ earth;

(Kneels.)

51Of thy deep duty more impression show
52Than that of common sons.

VOLUMNIA

Than that of common sons. O, stand up blest,

⸢He rises.⸣

53Whilst with no softer cushion than the flint
54I kneel before thee and unproperly
55Show duty, as mistaken all this while
56Between the child and parent.

⸢She kneels.⸣

CORIOLANUS

Between the child and parent. What’s this?
57Your knees to me? To your corrected son?

⸢He raises her up.⸣

58Then let the pebbles on the hungry beach
59Fillip the stars! Then let the mutinous winds
60Strike the proud cedars ’gainst the fiery sun,
61Murdering impossibility to make
62What cannot be slight work.

VOLUMNIA

What cannot be slight work. Thou art my warrior;
63I ⸢holp⸣ to frame thee. Do you know this lady?

CORIOLANUS

64The noble sister of Publicola,
65The moon of Rome, chaste as the icicle
66That’s curdied by the frost from purest snow
67And hangs on Dian’s temple!—Dear Valeria.

VOLUMNIA

68⸢presenting young Martius⸣ This is a poor epitome of yours,
69Which by th’ interpretation of full time
70May show like all yourself.

CORIOLANUS

⸢to young Martius⸣ The god of soldiers,

72With the consent of supreme Jove, inform
73Thy thoughts with nobleness, that thou mayst prove
74To shame unvulnerable, and stick i’ th’ wars
75Like a great seamark standing every flaw
76And saving those that eye thee.

VOLUMNIA

And saving those that eye thee. ⸢to young Martius⸣ Your knee, sirrah.

⸢He kneels.⸣

CORIOLANUS

That’s my brave boy!

VOLUMNIA

78Even he, your wife, this lady, and myself
79⸢Young Martius rises.⸣ Are suitors to you.

CORIOLANUS

⸢Young Martius rises.⸣ Are suitors to you. I beseech you, peace;
80Or if you’d ask, remember this before:
81The thing I have forsworn to grant may never
82Be held by you denials. Do not bid me
83Dismiss my soldiers or capitulate
84Again with Rome’s mechanics. Tell me not
85Wherein I seem unnatural; desire not
86T’ allay my rages and revenges with
87Your colder reasons.

VOLUMNIA

Your colder reasons. O, no more, no more!
88You have said you will not grant us anything;
89For we have nothing else to ask but that
90Which you deny already. Yet we will ask,
91That if you fail in our request, the blame
92May hang upon your hardness. Therefore hear us.

CORIOLANUS

93Aufidius, and you Volsces, mark, for we’ll
94Hear naught from Rome in private. ⸢He sits.⸣ Your request?

VOLUMNIA

95Should we be silent and not speak, our raiment
96And state of bodies would bewray what life
97We have led since thy exile. Think with thyself
98How more unfortunate than all living women
99Are we come hither; since that thy sight, which should
100Make our eyes flow with joy, hearts dance with comforts,
101Constrains them weep and shake with fear and sorrow,
102Making the mother, wife, and child to see
103The son, the husband, and the father tearing
104His country’s bowels out. And to poor we
105Thine enmity’s most capital. Thou barr’st us
106Our prayers to the gods, which is a comfort
107That all but we enjoy. For how can we—
108Alas, how can we—for our country pray,
109Whereto we are bound, together with thy victory,
110Whereto we are bound? Alack, or we must lose
111The country, our dear nurse, or else thy person,
112Our comfort in the country. We must find
113An evident calamity, though we had
114Our wish, which side should win, for either thou
115Must as a foreign recreant be led
116With manacles through our streets, or else
117Triumphantly tread on thy country’s ruin
118And bear the palm for having bravely shed
119Thy wife and children’s blood. For myself, son,
120I purpose not to wait on fortune till
121These wars determine. If I cannot persuade thee
122Rather to show a noble grace to both parts
123Than seek the end of one, thou shalt no sooner
124March to assault thy country than to tread—
125Trust to ’t, thou shalt not—on thy mother’s womb
126That brought thee to this world.

VIRGILIA

That brought thee to this world. Ay, and mine,
127That brought you forth this boy to keep your name
128Living to time.

YOUNG MARTIUS

Living to time. He shall not tread on me.
129I’ll run away till I am bigger, but then I’ll fight.

CORIOLANUS

130Not of a woman’s tenderness to be
131Requires nor child nor woman’s face to see.—
132I have sat too long.

⸢He rises.⸣

VOLUMNIA

I have sat too long. Nay, go not from us thus.
133If it were so, that our request did tend
134To save the Romans, thereby to destroy
135The Volsces whom you serve, you might condemn us
136As poisonous of your honor. No, our suit
137Is that you reconcile them, while the Volsces
138May say “This mercy we have showed,” the Romans
139“This we received,” and each in either side
140Give the all-hail to thee and cry “Be blest
141For making up this peace!” Thou know’st, great son,
142The end of war’s uncertain, but this certain,
143That, if thou conquer Rome, the benefit
144Which thou shalt thereby reap is such a name
145Whose repetition will be dogged with curses,
146Whose chronicle thus writ: “The man was noble,
147But with his last attempt he wiped it out,
148Destroyed his country, and his name remains
149To th’ ensuing age abhorred.” Speak to me, son.
150Thou hast affected the ⸢fine⸣ strains of honor
151To imitate the graces of the gods,
152To tear with thunder the wide cheeks o’ th’ air
153And yet to ⸢charge⸣ thy sulfur with a bolt
154That should but rive an oak. Why dost not speak?
155Think’st thou it honorable for a noble man
156Still to remember wrongs?—Daughter, speak you.
157He cares not for your weeping.—Speak thou, boy.
158Perhaps thy childishness will move him more
159Than can our reasons.—There’s no man in the world
160More bound to ’s mother, yet here he lets me prate
161Like one i’ th’ stocks. Thou hast never in thy life
162Showed thy dear mother any courtesy
163When she, poor hen, fond of no second brood,
164Has ⸢clucked⸣ thee to the wars and safely home,
165Loaden with honor. Say my request’s unjust
166And spurn me back; but if it be not so,
167Thou art not honest, and the gods will plague thee
168That thou restrain’st from me the duty which
169To a mother’s part belongs.—He turns away.—
170Down, ladies! Let us shame him with our knees.
171To his surname Coriolanus ’longs more pride
172Than pity to our prayers. Down! An end.

⸢They kneel.⸣

173This is the last. So, we will home to Rome
174And die among our neighbors.—Nay, behold ’s.
175This boy that cannot tell what he would have,
176But kneels and holds up hands for fellowship,
177Does reason our petition with more strength
178Than thou hast to deny ’t.—Come, let us go.

⸢They rise.⸣

179This fellow had a Volscian to his mother,
180His wife is in Corioles, and his child
181Like him by chance.—Yet give us our dispatch.
182I am hushed until our city be afire,
183And then I’ll speak a little.

⸢He⸣ holds her by the hand, silent.

CORIOLANUS

And then I’ll speak a little. O mother, mother!
184What have you done? Behold, the heavens do ope,
185The gods look down, and this unnatural scene
186They laugh at. O, my mother, mother, O!
187You have won a happy victory to Rome,
188But, for your son—believe it, O, believe it!—
189Most dangerously you have with him prevailed,
190If not most mortal to him. But let it come.—
191Aufidius, though I cannot make true wars,
192I’ll frame convenient peace. Now, good Aufidius,
193Were you in my stead, would you have heard
194A mother less? Or granted less, Aufidius?

AUFIDIUS

195I was moved withal.

CORIOLANUS

I was moved withal. I dare be sworn you were.
196And, sir, it is no little thing to make
197Mine eyes to sweat compassion. But, good sir,
198What peace you’ll make advise me. For my part,
199I’ll not to Rome. I’ll back with you; and pray you,
200Stand to me in this cause.—O mother!—Wife!

⸢He speaks with them aside.⸣

AUFIDIUS

201I am glad thou hast set thy mercy and thy honoraparte
202At difference in thee. Out of that I’ll workaparte
203Myself a former fortune.aparte

CORIOLANUS

⸢to the Women⸣ Ay, by and by;

205But we will drink together, and you shall bear
206A better witness back than words, which we,
207On like conditions, will have countersealed.
208Come, enter with us. Ladies, you deserve
209To have a temple built you. All the swords
210In Italy, and her confederate arms,
211Could not have made this peace.

They exit.

⸢Scene 4⸣

Enter Menenius and Sicinius.

MENENIUS

See you yond coign o’ th’ Capitol, yond cornerstone?

SICINIUS

Why, what of that?

MENENIUS

If it be possible for you to displace it with your little finger, there is some hope the ladies of Rome, especially his mother, may prevail with him. But I say there is no hope in ’t. Our throats are sentenced and stay upon execution.

SICINIUS

Is ’t possible that so short a time can alter the condition of a man?

MENENIUS

There is differency between a grub and a butterfly, yet your butterfly was a grub. This Martius is grown from man to dragon. He has wings; he’s more than a creeping thing.

SICINIUS

He loved his mother dearly.

MENENIUS

So did he me; and he no more remembers his mother now than an eight-year-old horse. The tartness of his face sours ripe grapes. When he walks, he moves like an engine, and the ground shrinks before his treading. He is able to pierce a corslet with his eye, talks like a knell, and his hum is a battery. He sits in his state as a thing made for Alexander. What he bids be done is finished with his bidding. He wants nothing of a god but eternity and a heaven to throne in.

SICINIUS

Yes, mercy, if you report him truly.

MENENIUS

I paint him in the character. Mark what mercy his mother shall bring from him. There is no more mercy in him than there is milk in a male tiger. That shall our poor city find, and all this is long of you.

SICINIUS

The gods be good unto us.

MENENIUS

No, in such a case the gods will not be good unto us. When we banished him, we respected not them; and he returning to break our necks, they respect not us.

Enter a Messenger.

MESSENGER

12⸢to Sicinius⸣ Sir, if you’d save your life, fly to your house.
13The plebeians have got your fellow tribune
14And hale him up and down, all swearing if
15The Roman ladies bring not comfort home,
16They’ll give him death by inches.

Enter another Messenger.

SICINIUS

They’ll give him death by inches. What’s the news?

⸢SECOND⸣ MESSENGER

17Good news, good news! The ladies have prevailed.
18The Volscians are dislodged and Martius gone.
19A merrier day did never yet greet Rome,
20No, not th’ expulsion of the Tarquins.

SICINIUS

No, not th’ expulsion of the Tarquins. Friend,
21Art thou certain this is true? Is ’t most certain?

⸢SECOND⸣ MESSENGER

22As certain as I know the sun is fire.
23Where have you lurked that you make doubt of it?
24Ne’er through an arch so hurried the blown tide
25As the recomforted through th’ gates. Why, hark you!

Trumpets, hautboys, drums beat, all together.

26The trumpets, sackbuts, psalteries, and fifes,
27Tabors and cymbals, and the shouting Romans
28Make the sun dance. Hark you!

A shout within.

MENENIUS

Make the sun dance. Hark you! This is good news.
29I will go meet the ladies. This Volumnia
30Is worth of consuls, senators, patricians
31A city full; of tribunes such as you
32A sea and land full. You have prayed well today.
33This morning for ten thousand of your throats
34I’d not have given a doit. Hark, how they joy!

Sound still with the shouts.

SICINIUS

⸢to Second Messenger⸣ First, the gods bless you for your tidings; next, accept my thankfulness.

⸢SECOND⸣ MESSENGER

36Sir, we have all great cause to give great thanks.

SICINIUS

They are near the city?

⸢SECOND⸣ MESSENGER

Almost at point to enter.

SICINIUS

We’ll meet them, and help the joy.

They exit.

⸢Scene 5⸣

Enter two Senators, with Ladies ⸢(Volumnia, Virgilia, Valeria)⸣ passing over the stage, with other Lords.

SENATOR

1Behold our patroness, the life of Rome!
2Call all your tribes together, praise the gods,
3And make triumphant fires. Strew flowers before them,
4Unshout the noise that banished Martius,
5Repeal him with the welcome of his mother.
6Cry “Welcome, ladies, welcome!”

ALL

Welcome, ladies, welcome!

A flourish with drums and trumpets.

⸢They exit.⸣

⸢Scene 6⸣

Enter Tullus Aufidius, with Attendants.

AUFIDIUS

1Go tell the lords o’ th’ city I am here.
2Deliver them this paper.
Deliver them this paper. ⸢(He gives them a paper.)⸣ Having read it,
3Bid them repair to th’ marketplace, where I,
4Even in theirs and in the commons’ ears,
5Will vouch the truth of it. Him I accuse
6The city ports by this hath entered and
7Intends t’ appear before the people, hoping
8To purge himself with words. Dispatch.

⸢The Attendants exit.⸣

Enter three or four Conspirators of Aufidius’s faction.

To purge himself with words. Dispatch. Most welcome!

FIRST CONSPIRATOR

9How is it with our general?

AUFIDIUS

How is it with our general? Even so
10As with a man by his own alms empoisoned
11And with his charity slain.

SECOND CONSPIRATOR

And with his charity slain. Most noble sir,
12If you do hold the same intent wherein
13You wished us parties, we’ll deliver you
14Of your great danger.

AUFIDIUS

Of your great danger. Sir, I cannot tell.
15We must proceed as we do find the people.

THIRD CONSPIRATOR

16The people will remain uncertain whilst
17’Twixt you there’s difference, but the fall of either
18Makes the survivor heir of all.

AUFIDIUS

Makes the survivor heir of all. I know it,
19And my pretext to strike at him admits
20A good construction. I raised him, and I pawned
21Mine honor for his truth, who, being so heightened,
22He watered his new plants with dews of flattery,
23Seducing so my friends; and to this end,
24He bowed his nature, never known before
25But to be rough, unswayable, and free.

THIRD CONSPIRATOR

Sir, his stoutness

27When he did stand for consul, which he lost
28By lack of stooping—

AUFIDIUS

By lack of stooping— That I would have spoke of.
29Being banished for ’t, he came unto my hearth,
30Presented to my knife his throat. I took him,
31Made him joint servant with me, gave him way
32In all his own desires; nay, let him choose
33Out of my files, his projects to accomplish,
34My best and freshest men; served his designments
35In mine own person; holp to reap the fame
36Which he did end all his; and took some pride
37To do myself this wrong; till at the last
38I seemed his follower, not partner; and
39He waged me with his countenance as if
40I had been mercenary.

FIRST CONSPIRATOR

I had been mercenary. So he did, my lord.
41The army marvelled at it, and, in the last,
42When he had carried Rome and that we looked
43For no less spoil than glory—

AUFIDIUS

For no less spoil than glory— There was it
44For which my sinews shall be stretched upon him.
45At a few drops of women’s rheum, which are
46As cheap as lies, he sold the blood and labor
47Of our great action. Therefore shall he die,
48And I’ll renew me in his fall. But hark!

Drums and trumpets sounds, with great shouts of the people.

FIRST CONSPIRATOR

49Your native town you entered like a post
50And had no welcomes home, but he returns
51Splitting the air with noise.

SECOND CONSPIRATOR

Splitting the air with noise. And patient fools,
52Whose children he hath slain, their base throats tear
53With giving him glory.

THIRD CONSPIRATOR

With giving him glory. Therefore at your vantage,
54Ere he express himself or move the people
55With what he would say, let him feel your sword,
56Which we will second. When he lies along,
57After your way his tale pronounced shall bury
58His reasons with his body.

AUFIDIUS

His reasons with his body. Say no more.

Enter the Lords of the city.

59Here come the lords.
60You are most welcome home.

AUFIDIUS

You are most welcome home. I have not deserved it.
61But, worthy lords, have you with heed perused
62What I have written to you?

ALL ⸢LORDS⸣

We have.

FIRST LORD

And grieve to hear ’t.

65What faults he made before the last, I think
66Might have found easy fines, but there to end
67Where he was to begin and give away
68The benefit of our levies, answering us
69With our own charge, making a treaty where
70There was a yielding—this admits no excuse.

Enter Coriolanus marching with Drum and Colors, the Commoners being with him.

AUFIDIUS

He approaches. You shall hear him.

CORIOLANUS

72Hail, lords! I am returned your soldier,
73No more infected with my country’s love
74Than when I parted hence, but still subsisting
75Under your great command. You are to know
76That prosperously I have attempted, and
77With bloody passage led your wars even to
78The gates of Rome. Our spoils we have brought home
79Doth more than counterpoise a full third part
80The charges of the action. We have made peace
81With no less honor to the Antiates
82Than shame to th’ Romans, and we here deliver,
83Subscribed by’ th’ Consuls and patricians,
84Together with the seal o’ th’ Senate, what
85We have compounded on.

⸢He offers the lords a paper.⸣

AUFIDIUS

Read it not, noble lords,

87But tell the traitor in the highest degree
88He hath abused your powers.

CORIOLANUS

“Traitor”? How now?

AUFIDIUS

Ay, traitor, Martius.

CORIOLANUS

Martius?

AUFIDIUS

92Ay, Martius, Caius Martius. Dost thou think
93I’ll grace thee with that robbery, thy stol’n name
94Coriolanus, in Corioles?
95You lords and heads o’ th’ state, perfidiously
96He has betrayed your business and given up
97For certain drops of salt your city Rome—
98I say your city—to his wife and mother,
99Breaking his oath and resolution like
100A twist of rotten silk, never admitting
101Counsel o’ th’ war, but at his nurse’s tears
102He whined and roared away your victory,
103That pages blushed at him and men of heart
104Looked wond’ring each at ⸢other.⸣

CORIOLANUS

Looked wond’ring each at ⸢other.⸣ Hear’st thou, Mars?

AUFIDIUS

Name not the god, thou boy of tears.

CORIOLANUS

Ha?

AUFIDIUS

No more.

CORIOLANUS

108Measureless liar, thou hast made my heart
109Too great for what contains it. “Boy”? O slave!—
110Pardon me, lords, ’tis the first time that ever
111I was forced to scold. Your judgments, my grave lords,
112Must give this cur the lie; and his own notion—
113Who wears my stripes impressed upon him, that
114Must bear my beating to his grave—shall join
115To thrust the lie unto him.

FIRST LORD

Peace, both, and hear me speak.

CORIOLANUS

117Cut me to pieces, Volsces. Men and lads,
118Stain all your edges on me. “Boy”? False hound!
119If you have writ your annals true, ’tis there
120That like an eagle in a dovecote, I
121⸢Fluttered⸣ your Volscians in Corioles,
122Alone I did it. “Boy”!

AUFIDIUS

Alone I did it. “Boy”! Why, noble lords,
123Will you be put in mind of his blind fortune,
124Which was your shame, by this unholy braggart,
125’Fore your own eyes and ears?

ALL CONSPIRATORS

’Fore your own eyes and ears? Let him die for ’t.

ALL PEOPLE

Tear him to pieces! Do it presently! He killed my son! My daughter! He killed my cousin Marcus! He killed my father!

SECOND LORD

Peace, ho! No outrage! Peace!

128The man is noble, and his fame folds in
129This orb o’ th’ Earth. His last offenses to us
130Shall have judicious hearing. Stand, Aufidius,
131And trouble not the peace.

CORIOLANUS

And trouble not the peace. ⸢drawing his sword⸣ O, that I had him,
132With six Aufidiuses, or more, his tribe,
133To use my lawful sword.

AUFIDIUS

To use my lawful sword. Insolent villain!

ALL CONSPIRATORS

Kill, kill, kill, kill, kill him!

Draw the Conspirators, and kills Martius, who falls. Aufidius stands on him.

LORDS

Hold, hold, hold, hold!

AUFIDIUS

136My noble masters, hear me speak.

FIRST LORD

My noble masters, hear me speak. O Tullus!

SECOND LORD

137Thou hast done a deed whereat valor will weep.

THIRD LORD

138Tread not upon him.—Masters, all be quiet.—
139Put up your swords.

AUFIDIUS

140My lords, when you shall know—as in this rage,
141Provoked by him, you cannot—the great danger
142Which this man’s life did owe you, you’ll rejoice
143That he is thus cut off. Please it your Honors
144To call me to your senate, I’ll deliver
145Myself your loyal servant or endure
146Your heaviest censure.

FIRST LORD

Your heaviest censure. Bear from hence his body,
147And mourn you for him. Let him be regarded
148As the most noble corse that ever herald
149Did follow to his urn.

SECOND LORD

Did follow to his urn. His own impatience
150Takes from Aufidius a great part of blame.
151Let’s make the best of it.

AUFIDIUS

Let’s make the best of it. My rage is gone,
152And I am struck with sorrow.—Take him up.
153Help, three o’ th’ chiefest soldiers; I’ll be one.—
154Beat thou the drum that it speak mournfully.—
155Trail your steel pikes. Though in this city he
156Hath widowed and unchilded many a one,
157Which to this hour bewail the injury,
158Yet he shall have a noble memory.
159Assist.

They exit bearing the body of Martius.

A dead march sounded.
