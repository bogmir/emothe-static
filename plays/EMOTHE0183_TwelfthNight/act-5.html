Act V

5.1

Enter Clown [with a letter] and Fabian.

FABIAN

Now as thou lov'st me, let me see his letter.

CLOWN

Good Master Fabian, grant me another request.

FABIAN

Anything.

CLOWN

Do not desire to see this letter.

FABIAN

This is to give a dog, and in recompense desire my dog again.

Enter Orsino, Viola [as Cesario], Curio, and Lords.

ORSINO

Belong you to the Lady Olivia, friends?

CLOWN

Ay, sir, we are some of her trappings.

ORSINO

I know thee well. How dost thou, my good fellow?

CLOWN

Truly, sir, the better for my foes, and the worse for my friends.

ORSINO

Just the contrary; the better for thy friends.

CLOWN

No, sir, the worse.

ORSINO

How can that be?

CLOWN

Marry, sir, they praise me, and make an ass of me. Now, my foes tell me plainly I am an ass, so that by my foes, sir, I profit in the knowledge of myself, and by my friends I am abused. So that, conclusions to be as kisses, if your four negatives make your two affirmatives, why then, the worse for my friends and the better for my foes.

ORSINO

Why, this is excellent.

CLOWN

By my troth, sir, no; though it please you to be one of my friends.

ORSINO

Thou shalt not be the worse for me; there's gold.

[Orsino gives him a gold coin.]

CLOWN

But that it would be double-dealing, sir, I would you could make it another.

ORSINO

O you give me ill counsel.

CLOWN

Put your grace in your pocket, sir, for this once, and let your flesh and blood obey it.

ORSINO

Well, I will be so much a sinner to be a double-dealer; there's another.

[Orsino gives him another gold coin.]

CLOWN

Primo, secundo, tertio is a good play; and the old saying is, "the third pays for all"; the triplex, sir, is a good tripping measure; or the bells of Saint Bennet, sir, may put you in mind: one, two, three.

ORSINO

You can fool no more money out of me at this throw. If you will let your lady know I am here to speak with her, and bring her along with you, it may awake my bounty further.

CLOWN

Marry, sir, lullaby to your bounty till I come again. I go, sir, but I would not have you to think that my desire of having is the sin of covetousness—but as you say, sir, let your bounty take a nap; I will awake it anon.

Exit.

Enter Antonio and Officers [guarding him].

VIOLA

654Here comes the man, sir, that did rescue me.

ORSINO

655That face of his I do remember well;
656Yet when I saw it last, it was besmeared
657As black as Vulcan in the smoke of war.
658A baubling vessel was he captain of,
659For shallow draught and bulk, unprizable;
660With which such scatheful grapple did he make
661With the most noble bottom of our fleet,
662That very envy, and the tongue of loss,
663Cried fame and honor on him. What's the matter?

FIRST OFFICER

664Orsino, this is that Antonio
665That took the Phoenix, and her fraught from Candy,
666And this is he that did the Tiger board
667When your young nephew Titus lost his leg.
668Here in the streets, desperate of shame and state,
669In private brabble did we apprehend him.

VIOLA

670He did me kindness, sir, drew on my side,
671But in conclusion put strange speech upon me;
672I know not what 'twas, but distraction.

ORSINO

673Notable pirate, thou saltwater thief,
674What foolish boldness brought thee to their mercies
675Whom thou, in terms so bloody and so dear,
676Hast made thine enemies?

ANTONIO

Hast made thine enemies? Orsino, noble sir,
677Be pleased that I shake off these names you give me.
678Antonio never yet was thief, or pirate,
679Though I confess, on base and ground enough,
680Orsino's enemy. A witchcraft drew me hither:
681That most ingrateful boy there by your side
682From the rude sea's enraged and foamy mouth
683Did I redeem. A wrack past hope he was.
684His life I gave him, and did thereto add
685My love without retention or restraint,
686All his in dedication. For his sake
687Did I expose myself, pure for his love,
688Into the danger of this adverse town;
689Drew to defend him, when he was beset;
690Where being apprehended, his false cunning,
691Not meaning to partake with me in danger,
692Taught him to face me out of his acquaintance,
693And grew a twenty years' removèd thing
694While one would wink; denied me mine own purse,
695Which I had recommended to his use
696Not half an hour before.

VIOLA

Not half an hour before. How can this be?

ORSINO

697When came he to this town?

ANTONIO

698Today, my lord; and for three months before,
699No int'rim, not a minute's vacancy,
700Both day and night did we keep company.

Enter Olivia and Attendants.

ORSINO

701Here comes the countess, now heaven walks on earth.
702[To Antonio] But for thee, fellow—fellow, thy words are madness.
703Three months this youth hath tended upon me;
704But more of that anon. [To Officers] Take him aside.

OLIVIA

705What would my lord, but that he may not have,
706Wherein Olivia may seem serviceable?
707[To Viola] Cesario, you do not keep promise with me.

[Viola and Orsino speak at the same time.]

VIOLA

708Madam—

ORSINO

709Gracious Olivia—

OLIVIA

710What do you say, Cesario? [Silencing Orsino] Good my lord.

VIOLA

711My lord would speak, my duty hushes me.

OLIVIA

712If it be aught to the old tune, my lord,
713It is as fat and fulsome to mine ear
714As howling after music.

ORSINO

715Still so cruel?

OLIVIA

Still so cruel? Still so constant, lord.

ORSINO

716What, to perverseness? You uncivil lady,
717To whose ingrate and unauspicious altars
718My soul the faithfull'st off'rings have breathed out
719That e'er devotion tendered! What shall I do?

OLIVIA

720Even what it please my lord, that shall become him

ORSINO

721Why should I not, had I the heart to do it,
722Like to th'Egyptian thief at point of death,
723Kill what I love?—a savage jealousy,
724That sometime savors nobly. But hear me this:
725Since you to non-regardance cast my faith,
726And that I partly know the instrument
727That screws me from my true place in your favor,
728Live you the marble-breasted tyrant still.
729But [Seizing Viola] this your minion, whom I know you love,
730And whom, by heaven, I swear I tender dearly,
731Him will I tear out of that cruel eye
732Where he sits crownèd in his master's spite.
733Come, boy, with me; my thoughts are ripe in mischief.
734I'll sacrifice the lamb that I do love,
735To spite a raven's heart within a dove.

[He moves to exit with Viola.]

VIOLA

736And I most jocund, apt, and willingly,
737To do you rest, a thousand deaths would die.

OLIVIA

738Where goes Cesario?

VIOLA

Where goes Cesario? After him I love
739More than I love these eyes, more than my life,
740More, by all mores, than e'er I shall love wife.
741If I do feign, you witnesses above
742Punish my life, for tainting of my love.

OLIVIA

743Ay me, detested! How am I beguiled!

VIOLA

744Who does beguile you? Who does do you wrong?

OLIVIA

745Hast thou forgot thyself? Is it so long?
746Call forth the holy father.

[Exit an Attendant.]

ORSINO

Call forth the holy father. [To Cesario] Come, away.

OLIVIA

747Whither, my lord? Cesario, husband, stay!

ORSINO

748Husband?

OLIVIA

Husband? Ay, husband. Can he that deny?

ORSINO

749Her husband, sirrah?

VIOLA

Her husband, sirrah? No, my lord, not I.

OLIVIA

750Alas, it is the baseness of thy fear
751That makes thee strangle thy propriety.
752Fear not, Cesario, take thy fortunes up,
753Be that thou know'st thou art, and then thou art
754As great as that thou fear'st.

Enter Priest.

755O welcome, father!
756Father, I charge thee by thy reverence
757Here to unfold (though lately we intended
758To keep in darkness what occasion now
759Reveals before 'tis ripe) what thou dost know
760Hath newly passed between this youth and me.

PRIEST

761A contract of eternal bond of love,
762Confirmed by mutual joinder of your hands,
763Attested by the holy close of lips,
764Strengthened by interchangement of your rings,
765And all the ceremony of this compact
766Sealed in my function, by my testimony;
767Since when, my watch hath told me, toward my grave
768I have travelled but two hours.

ORSINO

769[To Viola] O thou dissembling cub! What wilt thou be
770When time hath sowed a grizzle on thy case?
771Or will not else thy craft so quickly grow
772That thine own trip shall be thine overthrow?
773Farewell, and take her, but direct thy feet
774Where thou and I henceforth may never meet.

VIOLA

775My lord, I do protest—

OLIVIA

My lord, I do protest— O, do not swear,
776Hold little faith, though thou hast too much fear.

Enter Sir Andrew [with his head bloody].

SIR ANDREW

For the love of God, a surgeon! Send one presently to Sir Toby.

OLIVIA

What's the matter?

SIR ANDREW

He's broke my head across, and has given Sir Toby a bloody coxcomb too. For the love of God, your help! I had rather than forty pound I were at home.

OLIVIA

Who has done this, Sir Andrew?

SIR ANDREW

The count's gentleman, one Cesario. We took him for a coward, but he's the very devil incardinate.

ORSINO

My gentleman Cesario?

SIR ANDREW

[Seeing Viola] [and recoiling in fear]. 'Od's lifelings, here he is! [To her] You broke my head for nothing; and that that I did, I was set on to do't by Sir Toby.

VIOLA

777Why do you speak to me? I never hurt you.
778You drew your sword upon me without cause,
779But I bespake you fair, and hurt you not.

Enter Sir Toby [limping, his head bloody,] and [supported by] Clown.

SIR ANDREW

If a bloody coxcomb be a hurt, you have hurt me; I think you set nothing by a bloody coxcomb. Here comes Sir Toby halting; you shall hear more. But if he had not been in drink, he would have tickled you othergates than he did.

ORSINO

How now, gentleman? How is't with you?

SIR TOBY

That's all one, he's hurt me, and there's th'end on't. [To Clown] Sot, didst see Dick Surgeon, sot?

CLOWN

Oh, he's drunk, Sir Toby, an hour agone; his eyes were set at eight i'th'morning.

SIR TOBY

Then he's a rogue, and a passy-measures pavan. I hate a drunken rogue.

OLIVIA

Away with him! Who hath made this havocwith them?

SIR ANDREW

I'll help you, Sir Toby, because we'll be dressed together.

SIR TOBY

Will you help? An ass-head, and a coxcomb, and a knave? A thin-faced knave, a gull!

OLIVIA

Get him to bed, and let his hurt be looked to.

[Exeunt Sir Toby and Sir Andrew led off by Clown and Fabian.]

Enter Sebastian. [Everyone else observes the identically dressed Sebastian and Viola.]

SEBASTIAN

780I am sorry, madam, I have hurt your kinsman;
781But had it been the brother of my blood,
782I must have done no less with wit and safety.
783You throw a strange regard upon me, and by that
784I do perceive it hath offended you.
785Pardon me, sweet one, even for the vows
786We made each other but so late ago.

ORSINO

787One face, one voice, one habit, and two persons:
788A natural perspective, that is, and is not!

SEBASTIAN

789Antonio! Oh, my dear Antonio,
790How have the hours racked and tortured me
791Since I have lost thee!

ANTONIO

792Sebastian, are you?

SEBASTIAN

Sebastian, are you? Fear'st thou that, Antonio?

ANTONIO

793How have you made division of yourself?
794An apple cleft in two is not more twin
795Than these two creatures. Which is Sebastian?

OLIVIA

796Most wonderful.

SEBASTIAN

797[Seeing Viola] Do I stand there? I never had a brother;
798Nor can there be that deity in my nature
799Of here and everywhere. I had a sister,
800Whom the blind waves and surges have devoured.
801Of charity, what kin are you to me?
802What countryman? What name? What parentage?

VIOLA

803Of Messaline. Sebastian was my father.
804Such a Sebastian was my brother too;
805So went he suited to his watery tomb.
806If spirits can assume both form and suit,
807You come to fright us.

SEBASTIAN

You come to fright us. A spirit I am indeed,
808But am in that dimension grossly clad
809Which from the womb I did participate.
810Were you a woman, as the rest goes even,
811I should my tears let fall upon your cheek,
812And say, "Thrice welcome, drownèd Viola."

VIOLA

813My father had a mole upon his brow.

SEBASTIAN

814And so had mine.

VIOLA

815And died that day when Viola from her birth
816Had numbered thirteen years.

SEBASTIAN

817Oh, that record is lively in my soul.
818He finishèd indeed his mortal act
819That day that made my sister thirteen years.

VIOLA

820If nothing lets to make us happy both,
821But this my masculine usurped attire,
822Do not embrace me, till each circumstance
823Of place, time, fortune, do cohere and jump
824That I am Viola; which to confirm,
825I'll bring you to a captain in this town,
826Where lie my maiden weeds, by whose gentle help
827I was preserved to serve this noble count.
828All the occurrence of my fortune since
829Hath been between this lady and this lord.

SEBASTIAN

830[To Olivia] So comes it, lady, you have been mistook.
831But nature to her bias drew in that.
832You would have been contracted to a maid;
833Nor are you therein, by my life, deceived:
834You are betrothed both to a maid and man.

ORSINO

835[To Olivia] Be not amazed, right noble is his blood.
836If this be so—as yet the glass seems true—
837I shall have share in this most happy wrack.
838[To Viola] Boy, thou hast said to me a thousand times
839Thou never shouldst love woman like to me.

VIOLA

840And all those sayings will I overswear,
841And all those swearings keep as true in soul
842As doth that orbèd continent the fire
843That severs day from night.

ORSINO

That severs day from night. Give me thy hand,
844And let me see thee in thy woman's weeds.

VIOLA

845The captain that did bring me first on shore
846Hath my maid's garments; he upon some action
847Is now in durance, at Malvolio's suit,
848A gentleman and follower of my lady's.

OLIVIA

849He shall enlarge him. Fetch Malvolio hither—
850And yet, alas, now I remember me,
851They say, poor gentleman, he's much distract.

Enter Clown with a letter, and Fabian.

852A most extracting frenzy of mine own
853From my remembrance clearly banished his.
854[To Clown] How does he, sirrah?

CLOWN

Truly, madam, he holds Beelzebub at the stave's end as well as a man in his case may do. He's here writ a letter to you. I should have given't you today morning, but as a madman's epistles are no gospels, so it skills not much when they are delivered.

OLIVIA

Open't, and read it.

CLOWN

[Reading madly] Look then to be well edified, when the fool delivers the madman. "By the Lord, madam—"

OLIVIA

How now, art thou mad?

CLOWN

No, madam, I do but read madness. An your ladyship will have it as it ought to be, you must allow vox .

OLIVIA

Prithee, read i'thy right wits.

CLOWN

So I do, madonna. But to read his right wits is to read thus. Therefore perpend, my princess, and give ear.

[Clown prepares to read madly again; Olivia seizes the letter and gives it to Fabian.]

OLIVIA

[To Fabian] Read it you, sirrah.

FABIAN

(Reads.) "By the Lord, madam, you wrong me, and the world shall know it. Though you have put me into darkness, and given your drunken cousin rule over me, yet have I the benefit of my senses as well as your ladyship. I have your own letter, that induced me to the semblance I put on; with the which I doubt not but to do myself much right, or you much shame. Think of me as you please. I leave my duty a little unthought of, and speak out of my injury. The madly-used Malvolio."

OLIVIA

Did he write this?

CLOWN

Ay, madam.

ORSINO

855This savors not much of distraction.

OLIVIA

856See him delivered, Fabian, bring him hither.

[Exit Fabian.]

857[To Orsino] My Lord, so please you, these things further thought on,
858To think me as well a sister as a wife,
859One day shall crown th'alliance on't, so please you,
860Here at my house, and at my proper cost.

ORSINO

861Madam, I am most apt t'embrace your offer.
862[To Viola] Your master quits you; and for your service done him,
863So much against the mettle of your sex,
864So far beneath your soft and tender breeding,
865And since you called me master for so long,
866Here is my hand; you shall from this time be
867Your master's mistress.

OLIVIA

Your master's mistress. A sister, you are she!

Enter [Fabian and] Malvolio [with Maria's letter].

ORSINO

868Is this the madman?

OLIVIA

Is this the madman? Ay, my lord, this same.
869[To Malvolio] How now, Malvolio?

MALVOLIO

[To Malvolio] How now, Malvolio? Madam, you have done me wrong,
870Notorious wrong.

OLIVIA

Notorious wrong. Have I, Malvolio? No.

MALVOLIO

871Lady, you have. Pray you peruse that letter.
872[Giving her the letter] You must not now deny it is your hand.
873Write from it if you can, in hand, or phrase,
874Or say 'tis not your seal, not your invention.
875You can say none of this. Well, grant it then,
876And tell me, in the modesty of honor,
877Why you have given me such clear lights of favor,
878Bade me come smiling and cross-gartered to you,
879To put on yellow stockings, and to frown
880Upon Sir Toby, and the lighter people;
881And acting this in an obedient hope,
882Why have you suffered me to be imprisoned,
883Kept in a dark house, visited by the priest,
884And made the most notorious geck and gull
885That ere invention played on? Tell me, why?

OLIVIA

886Alas, Malvolio, this is not my writing,
887Though I confess much like the character;
888But out of question, 'tis Maria's hand.
889And now I do bethink me, it was she
890First told me thou wast mad; then cam'st in smiling,
891And in such forms which here were presupposed
892Upon thee in the letter. Prithee, be content.
893This practice hath most shrewdly past upon thee;
894But when we know the grounds and authors of it,
895Thou shalt be both the plaintiff and the judge
896Of thine own cause.

FABIAN

Of thine own cause. Good madam, hear me speak,
897And let no quarrel, nor no brawl to come,
898Taint the condition of this present hour,
899Which I have wondered at. In hope it shall not,
900Most freely I confess myself and Toby
901Set this device against Malvolio here,
902Upon some stubborn and uncourteous parts
903We had conceived against him. Maria writ
904The letter, at Sir Toby's great importance,
905In recompense whereof he hath married her.
906How with a sportful malice it was followed
907May rather pluck on laughter than revenge,
908If that the injuries be justly weighed
909That have on both sides passed.

OLIVIA

910[To Malvolio] Alas, poor fool, how have they baffled thee!

CLOWN

[To Malvolio] Why, "Some are born great, some achieve greatness, and some have greatness thrown upon them." I was one, sir, in this interlude, one Sir Topaz, sir; but that's all one. "By the Lord, fool, I am not mad!" But do you remember: "Madam, why laugh you at such a barren rascal? An you smile not, he's gagged." And thus the whirligig of time brings in his revenges.

MALVOLIO

911I'll be revenged on the whole pack of you!

[Exit.]

OLIVIA

912He hath been most notoriously abused.

ORSINO

913[To Fabian] Pursue him, and entreat him to a peace.

[Exit Fabian.]

914He hath not told us of the captain yet.
915When that is known, and golden time convents,
916A solemn combination shall be made
917Of our dear souls. [To Olivia] Meantime, sweet sister,
918We will not part from hence. [To Viola] Cesario, come—
919For so you shall be while you are a man;
920But when in other habits you are seen,
921Orsino's mistress, and his fancy's queen.

Exeunt [all except Clown].

CLOWN

922(Sings) When that I was and a little tiny boy,
923With hey, ho, the wind and the rain,
924A foolish thing was but a toy,
925For the rain it raineth every day.
926But when i came to man’s estate,
927With hey, ho, the wind and the rain,
928‘Gainst knaves and thieves men shut their gate,
929For the rain it raineth every day.
930But when i came, alas, to wive,
931With hey, ho, the wind and the rain,
932By swaggering could i never thrive,
933For the rain it raineth every day.
934But when i came unto my beds,
935With hey, ho, the wind and the rain,
936With tosspots still ‘had drunken heads,
937For the rain it raineth every day.
938A great while ago the World begun,
939With hey, ho, the wind and the rain,
940But that’s all one, our play is done,
941And we’ll Struve to please you every day.

[Exit.]
