Félix Lope de Vega y Carpio

New Rules for Writing Plays at This Time

Act I

Addressed to the academy of Madrid

They've told me, learned gentlemen, who are
2the cream of Spain's intelligentsia
3(and whose Academy will soon outshine
4not only those that Cicero so labelled,
5in envy of the Greeks, near Lake Averno
6in Italy, but also that of Athens,
7in whose renowned Lyceum, led by Plato,
8such eminent philosophers assembled),
9to write a set of rules for plays that's based
10on what will satisfy our public's taste.
11It seems an easy matter, and indeed
12it would be so for any one of you,
13who's written fewer of them, and knows more
14about the established laws -and everything;
15but what in this regard must give me pause
16is having written them without such laws.
17It wasn't that I didn't know those precepts.
18I read, thank heaven, the volumes that contain them
19when first I learnt the rudiments of grammar;
20the sun had not ten times traversed the skies
21between the Ram and Fish before my eyes.
22Rather, in fact it was because I found
23the plays performed in Spain around that time
24were written not as those who'd led the way
25considered that they should be everywhere,
26but scribbled by a host of barbarous men
27who taught the common folk their own crude ways,
28and so they flooded in on such a scale
29that any playwright now who keeps the rules
30dies without fame or recompense, because
31for those who lack the light to guide their art
32Custom, not reason, plays the greater part.
33It's true that I have written on occasion
34according to the precepts so few know,
35but when by contrast I see sally forth
36monstrosities replete with stage effects,
37and how the common folk and women flock
38to canonize such lamentable labours,
39I too revert to that barbaric custom,
40and when I plan to write a play, I put
41the precepts firmly under lock and key,
42and ban Plautus and Terence from my room,
43so that they may not cry me shame (for Truth
44will often shout aloud from silent books),
45and substitute the rules that were invented
46by those who sought the plaudits of the mob,
47for since it is the mob that pays, it's right
48to act the fool to give such fools delight.
49True comedy, like any kind of poem
50or poesis , has its specific aim,
51a purpose which has been established as:
52to imitate the actions of mankind
53and represent the customs of the day.
54Moreover, any imitative poem
55consists of three components, which are discourse,
56sweet-sounding verse, and harmony or music.
57All this it shared with tragedy, from which
58it differs only in that it depicts
59the acts of common folk, but tragedy
60the deeds of kings and others so exalted.
61See in how many ways ours can be faulted!
62Such comedies indeed were labelled acts
63as aping those of ordinary men.
64In Spain Lope de Rueda set the example
65by following those precepts, and today
66you'll find such plays in prose by him in print
67that simply feature rude mechanicals,
68and how a farrier's daughter fared in love.
69Thus it's become the custom now to give
70those comedies in which the ancient precepts
71retain their force the name of entremeses ,
72for these are set among the common folk,
73since entremeses never feature kings;
74and so it shows a sorry lack of style,
75when those who've come to scorn the ancient rules
76write comedies with kings in, fit for fools.
77In his Poetics Aristoteles
78describes, though not too clearly, how plays started,
79the rivalry of Athens and Megara
80concerning which had had theatre first;
81for the Megarians claimed that Epicharmus
82invented it, but the Athenians, Magnes.
83Elius Donatus tells us drama had
84its roots in ancient sacrificial rites;
85he states that tragedy began with Thespis
86(following Horace, who had said the same),
87and comedy with Aristophanes.
88The Odyssey of Homer was composed
89in comic vein, and yet his Iliad was
90a tragedy applauded as a model,
91which I was imitating when I called
92my own Jerusalem a "tragic epic",
93whereas the Inferno , Purgatory and Heaven
94by that famed poet Dante Aligheri
95all call a comedy, although Manetti
96says in his prologue that it is a pity.
97The comic play, regarded with suspicion,
98as all men know, fell silent for a while,
99and so it was that satire had its birth,
100but proved more savage, and more quickly died,
101and so allowed New Comedy to appear.
102Choruses were its basis, only later
103were individual persons introduced;
104though Terence, as Menander had before him,
105scorned and eschewed the choruses as tedious.
106Terence was more attentive to the precepts,
107and never sought to raise the comic style
108to tragic grandeur (something which in Plautus
109so many have regarded as a vice),
110being in this more prudent, more precise.
111Tragedy's plots are based on history,
112but those of comedy are humble fictions,
113and comedy was said to be "flat-footed"
114on that account, though also since the actor
115played on an simple stage and wore no buskins.
116It had its kinds: paliata, atelana,
117togata, tabernaria, pantomime...
118-as many then as at the present time.
119With Attic elegance the men of Athens
120used comedies to castigate its vices
121and foolish customs, and awarded prizes
122both to their writers and to their performers;
123and Tully therefore said they were a mirror
124of human life, an image of the truth,
125which is so noble a description that
126it means they vie with history for renown.
127Think then; don't they deserve a glorious crown?
128But I suspect you're muttering to each other
129I'm just translating books, and boring you
130by listing such a muddled mass of facts.
131You must believe I needed to remind you
132of some such matters, so that you might see
133you're wanting me to give a set of rules
134for writing comedies in Spain today,
135where all those written go against the rules;
136and asking how they should be now, despite
137those ancient precepts, which are based on Reason,
138is seeking my opinion from experience,
139and not that set of rules, for they depict
140a truth the unlettered mob all contradict.
141If you want rules, I beg you clever men
142to read the very learned Robortello
143of Utina on Aristotle's treatise,
144as well as what he wrote in De comedia ;
145you'll find what many books diffusely say,
146for all is in confusion here today.
147But if you want opinions on the plays
148that hold the stage at present, when the mob
149must formulate their own laws for producing
150the vile chimera of that comic monster,
151I'll state my own -begging your pardon, since
152I must obey the mandate of my master-,
153gloss over the misjudgement of the mob,
154and say how I would wish plays to be written,
155now keeping to the rules is "in your dreams",
156seeking a mean between those two extremes.
157First choose your subject-matter; don't be bothered,
158despite the precepts, if it features kings,
159although I understand the prudent Philip,
160who ruled in Spain as master of us all,
161was angry when he saw a king involved,
162whether he held it contravened the rules,
163or thought a person so august and holy
164should not be shown consorting with the lowly.
165Yet ancient comedy affords a model,
166for there we find that Plautus featured gods,
167as Jupiter in his Amphitryon shows.
168It pains me to approve of it, God knows,
169for Plutarch, when discoursing on Menander,
170is critical of ancient comedy.
171But since we stray so far now from the rules,
172and here in Spain commit crime after crime,
173let men of learning hold their tongue this time.
174To mix the tragic and the comic modes,
175Terence with Seneca, perhaps producing,
176like Pasiphae of Crete, a Minotaur,
177makes one part serious, one ridiculous;
178and that variety gives great delight.
179Nature provides a pattern clear to see;
180its beauty lies in such variety.
181One needs to bear in mind the matter covered
182should have a single action, and ensure
183the plot-line is in no way episodic;
184I mean, does not involve extraneous things
185that deviate from the essential aim,
186and that no part of it could be removed
187without the whole play crashing to the ground.
188All need not happen in a single day,
189though Aristotle counselled that it should,
190because we lost respect for him before
191by mixing baser, comic elements
192with tragedy's sententious seriousness.
193The events should happen in as short a time
194as possible, unless, that is, the plot
195is based on history, and some years must pass,
196or if a character must make a journey,
197though time-shifts that result may well be placed in
198the gaps between the acts, and if that licence
199gives such offence to people "in the know",
200those who find plays offend them shouldn't go.
201How many now are scandalized to see
202years pass in plays the precepts say may cover
203only the hours between sunrise and sunset;
204they wouldn't even give all twenty-four!
205I find the fiery temper of a Spaniard
206who sits to see a play will not be calmed
207unless he's told a story, in two hours,
208from Genesis right up to Judgment Day;
209and I say, if the aim is to delight,
210whatever serves that purpose must be right.
211Write out the plot in prose, dividing it
212into three acts, three sequences of time,
213contriving, if you can, within each one,
214not to disrupt the time-frame of a day.
215Captain Virués, a fine, outstanding author,
216first used the three-act structure, for before him,
217since plays were only little children then,
218they crawled, like year-old infants, on all fours;
219and when eleven or twelve I wrote some like them,
220in four short acts, in just four gatherings,
221since each in its eight pages held one act,
222because back then a little entremés
223would be performed in all three intervals.
224Now there's just one, if that, and then a jig,
225though in comedy the dance is so important
226that Aristotle gives it his approval
227-and others write of it, like Athenaeus,
228Plato, and Xenophon (though he rebukes
229Callipedes for making it licentious)
230and so it seems quite like the ancient chorus.
231Distinguishing two sections in the action,
232show how the plot develops from the start
233up to the point when things begin to change,
234but don't allow the outcome to be obvious
235until you reach the very final scene,
236for once the audience guess the end they turn
237their faces to the exit, and their backs
238on actors who've been three hours on the go;
239how all concludes is all they want to know.
240The stage should very rarely be left empty,
241without a person speaking. If there are
242such gaps, you'll find the public will get restless.
243Besides, the play will go on much too long,
244which is a serious fault, and that apart,
245avoiding them displays more skill and art.
246Begin then, using plain and simple language,
247not serious thoughts or fanciful conceits,
248when you portray two characters or three
249in conversation on domestic things;
250but when the person you depict is seeking
251to counsel or persuade for or against,
252such aphorisms and conceits are called for,
253and this is truly imitating life,
254for when a man's advising or persuading
255to one view or another, he adopts
256a style quite different from his usual speech.
257Aristides the rhetorician says
258the language in a comedy should be
259plain, clear and comprehensible, and based
260on people's everyday, habitual parlance,
261insisting on the other hand that when
262the context is political, the lexis
263should be high-sounding, ornamented, striking.
264Don't cite the scriptures, or distort the discourse
265by using far-fetched terms, for if the task
266is imitating speech, we do not want
267Metauros, demiurges and Pancayas,
268centaurs and hippogriffs parading by us.
269If the king speaks, assume as best you may
270a regal gravitas , and if they greybeard,
271contrive a modest, mild sententiousness;
272portray your lovers in such tender terms
273as will extremely move all those who listen;
274cast your soliloquies in such a way
275that he who speaks them will be quite transformed,
276and being changed, change those who hear; he should
277put questions to himself, and answer them.
278If he complains, take care he always shows
279the courtesy we owe to womankind.
280Don't let your ladies be unladylike,
281and if they should disguise themselves as men,
282since such cross-dressing never fails to please,
283ensure they do in ways that may be excused.
284Beware the impossible, for imitation
285must only seek verisimilitude.
286Your comic shouldn't prate on lofty matters,
287or spout conceits like some that we have heard
288in certain plays imported from abroad,
289and characters should absolutely never
290belie what they themselves have said before;
291I mean, fail to recall, as Sophocles
292is criticized for letting Oedipus
293forget that he himself had murdered Laius.
294Conclude your episodes of action neatly
295with aphoristic wit, with well-turned verses,
296in order that when actors leave the stage
297the audience isn't left dissatisfied.
298Set out the situation in Act One,
299and in Act Two so complicate the plot
300that hardly anyone can guess before
301the middle of Act Three how it will end.
302Continually beguile the public's fancy,
303and when you find they think they know what's coming,
304make things turn out in quite another way.
305Shrewdly select the verse-forms you employ
306to fit the various matters that you deal with.
307The décima is suited to complaints,
308the sonnet's fine for characters left waiting;
309narrative speeches call for ballad-metre,
310though they sound splendid in ottava rima ;
311tercets will serve for solemn, weighty matters,
312and redondillas for romantic scenes.
313Don't fail to use rhetorical devices
314such as anadiplosis (repetition
315of single words), anaphora (that is,
316a set of lines that all begin the same),
317aporia (or doubts), and ironies,
318plus exclamations and apostrophes.
319Deceiving with the truth is a device
320that Miguel Sánchez used in all his plays
321and audiences have liked, so that he's worthy
322to be remembered for discovering it.
323Ambiguous speech and devious double-entendre
324have always gone down well with common folk,
325for each spectator fancies he alone
326is smart enough to grasp what's being said.
327Matters of honour are the best as subjects;
328they powerfully move all kinds of folk;
329and so do acts of high heroic virtue,
330for virtue is admired on every hand;
331and so we see that if an actor happens
332to play a traitor, everyone so hates him
333that when he goes out shopping they won't serve him,
334and common people shun him when they meet him;
335but if he plays a hero, all acclaim him,
336and even men of rank bow down before him,
337seek out, reward, make much of and adore him.
338Each act should be four gatherings, no more;
339ninety-six pages is the proper length
340to suit the time and audiences' patience.
341When you're satirical, be sure your strictures
342aren't blatant or direct, for comedies,
343as well you know , in Greece and Italy
344were banned by law for giving such offence.
345Mock without malice; if you put to shame,
346don't hope to win applause or merit fame.
347These those among you who are not so bothered
348about the ancient rules may take as maxims,
349for time permits no more on this occasion;
350and also it's the actor-manager
351who must provide, for all three kinds of play
352Vitruvius lists, the settings that Valerius
353and Horace or Crinitus specify:
354their painted cloths, their leafy trees or bowers,
355their hovels, houses or fake marble towers.
356On costumes we might turn to Julius Pollux,
357if that were necessary, though in Spain
358you're liable to see in plays today,
359as well as many other barbarous things,
360Turks wearing Christian ruffs about their necks,
361and Ancient Romans sporting fancy kecks.
362But I can hardly say that anyone's
363more barbarous than I am, since I dare
364to give you rules against the rules, and merely
365go with the flow where Italy and France
366will label me an arrant ignoramus.
367What can I do though, if the plays I've written,
368including one I finished just this week,
369add up to -let me count-four eighty-three?
370For but for half a dozen, all the rest
371sinned grievously against the hallowed precepts.
372In fine, I stand by what I've done, and know
373that though they might, if different, have been better,
374they'd not have proved as pleasing as they did;
375for sometimes what is anything but right
376will for that very reason give delight.
377You want to know why comedy can hold
378the mirror up to nature, what it offers
379to young and old, beyond its witty sallies,
380its cultured language and its eloquence?
381What weighty thoughts appear amid its jesting,
382what serious matters mingle with its play?
383How faithless servants prove, how full of guile
384and trickery all women always are,
385how wretched, stupid and inept are lovers,
386how rarely good beginnings turn out well?
387Don't hold debates about the ancient rules;
388go to the play, and pay close heed -that way,
389you'll find it tell you all there is to say.
