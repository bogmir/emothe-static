George Etherege

The Man of Mode, or Sir Fopling Flutter

← ACT IV THE EPILOGUE →

ACT V

SCENE I

Enter Mistress LOVEIT and PERT, her woman.

Pert

1213Well, in my eyes Sir Fopling is no such despicable person.

Loveit

1214You are an excellent judge!

Pert

1215He's as handsome a man as Mr. Dorimant, and as great a gallant.

Loveit

1216Intolerable! is't not enough I submit to his impertinences, but I must be plagued with yours too?

Pert

1217Indeed, madam –

Loveit

1218'Tis false, mercenary malice –

Enter her Footman.

Footman

1219Mrs. Belinda, madam –

Loveit

1220What of her?

Footman

1221She's below.

Loveit

1222How came she?

Footman

1223In a chair; ambling Harry brought her.

Loveit

1224He bring her! His chair stands near Dorimant's door, and always brings me from thence –Run and ask him where he took her up; go, there is no truth in friendship neither. Women as well as men –all are false, or all are so to me at least.

Pert

1225You are jealous of her too.

Loveit

1226You had best tell her I am. 'Twill become the liberty you take of late. This fellow's bringing of her, her going out by five o'clock –I know not what to think. Enter BELINDA.
Belinda, you are grown an early riser, I hear.

Belinda

1227Do you not wonder, my dear, what made me abroad so soon?

Loveit

1228You do not use to be so.

Belinda

1229The country gentlewomen I told you of (Lord! they have the oddest diversions!) would never let me rest till I promised to go with them to the markets this morning to eat fruit and buy nosegays.

Loveit

1230Are they so fond of a filthy nosegay?

Belinda

1231They complain of the stinks of the town, and are never well but when they have their noses in one.

Loveit

1232These are essences and sweet waters.

Belinda

1233Oh! they cry out upon perfumes they are unwholesome; one of 'em was falling into a fit with the smell of these narolii.

Loveit

1234Methinks, in complaisance you should have had a nosegay too.

Belinda

1235Do you think, my dear, I could be so loathsome to trick myself up with carnations and stock gillyflowers? I begged their pardon, and told them I never wore anything but orange flowers and tuberose. That which made me willing to go was a strange desire I had to eat some fresh nectarines.

Loveit

1236And had you any?

Belinda

1237The best I ever tasted.

Loveit

1238Whence came you now?

Belinda

1239From their lodgings, where I crowded out of a coach, and took a chair to come and see you, my dear.

Loveit

1240Whither did you send for that chair?

Belinda

1241'Twas going by empty.

Loveit

1242Where do these country gentlewomen lodge, I pray?

Belinda

1243In the Strand, over against the Exchange.

Pert

1244That place is never without a nest of 'em; they are always as one goes by fleering in balconies or staring out of windows.

Enter Footman.

Loveit

1245[whispers to the Footman] Come hither.

Belinda

1246[aside] This fellow by her order has been questioning the chairmen –I threatened 'em with the name of Dorimant; if they should have told truth I am lost for ever.

Loveit

1247In the Strand, said you?

Footman

1248Yes, madam, over against the Exchange.

[Exit Footman.

Loveit

1249She's innocent, and I am much to blame.

Belinda

1250[aside] I am so frighted my countenance will betray me.

Loveit

1251Belinda! what makes you look so pale?

Belinda

1252Want of my usual rest, and jolting up and down so long in an odious hackney.

Enter Footman.

Footman

1253Madam, Mr. Dorimant!

Loveit

1254What makes him here?

Belinda

1255[aside] Then I am betrayed indeed; he's broke his word, and I love a man that does not care for me.

Loveit

1256Lord! you faint, Belinda.

Belinda

1257I think I shall; such an oppression here on the sudden.

Pert

1258She has eaten too much fruit, I warrant you.

Loveit

1259Not unlikely!

Pert

1260'Tis that lies heavy on her stomach.

Loveit

1261Have her into chamber, give her some surfeit water, and let her lie down a little.

Pert

1262Come, madam, I was a strange devourer of fruit when I was young, so ravenous –

[Exit BELINDA, PERT leading her off.

Loveit

1263Oh, that my love would be put calm awhile! that I might receive this man all the scorn and indignation he deserves.

Enter DORIMANT.

Dorimant

1264Now for a touch of Sir Fopling to being with. Hey – page –give positive order that none of my people stir –let the canaille wait as they should do: since noise and nonsense have such powerful charms,

I, that I may successful prove,
Transform myself to what you love.

Loveit

1265If that would do, you need not change from what you are; you can be vain and loud enough.

Dorimant

1266But not with so good a grace as Sir Fopling. Hey, Hampshire! –Oh! that sound! that sound becomes the mouth of a man of quality.

Loveit

1267Is there a thing so hateful as a senseless mimic?

Dorimant

1268He's a great grievance indeed to all who like yourself, madam, love to play the fool in quiet.

Loveit

1269A ridiculous animal who has more of the ape than the ape has of the man in him.

Dorimant

1270I have as mean an opinion of a sheer mimic as yourself; yet were he all ape I should prefer him to the gay, the giddy, brisk, insipid, noisy fool you dote on.

Loveit

1271Those noisy fools, however you despise 'em, have good qualities, which weigh more (or ought at least) with us women than all the pernicious wit you have to boast of.

Dorimant

1272That I may hereafter have a just value for their merit, pray do me the favour to name 'em.

Loveit

1273You'll despise 'em as the dull effects of ignorance and vanity, yet I care not if I mention some. First, they really admire us, while you at best but flatter us well.

Dorimant

1274Take heed! fools can dissemble too –

Loveit

1275They may, but not so artificially as you: there is no fear they should deceive us. Then they are assiduous, sir; they are ever offering us their service, and always waiting on our will.

Dorimant

1276You owe that to their excessive idleness; they know not how to entertain themselves at home, and find so little welcome abroad, they are fain to fly to you who countenance 'em as a refuge against the solitude they would be otherwise condemned to.

Loveit

1277Their conversation too diverts us better.

Dorimant

1278Playing with your fan, smelling to your gloves, commending your hair, and taking notice how 'tis cut and shaded after the new way.

Loveit

1279Were it sillier than you can make it, you must allow 'tis pleasanter to laugh at others than to be laughed at ourselves, though never so wittily. Then though they want skill to flatter us, they flatter themselves so well they save us the labour; we need not take that care and pains to satisfy 'em of our love, which we so often lose on you.

Dorimant

1280They commonly indeed believe too well of themselves, and always better of you than you deserve.

Loveit

1281You are in the right; they have an implicit faith in us which keeps 'em from prying narrowly into our secrets, and saves us the vexations trouble of clearing doubts which your subtle and causeless jealousies every moment raise.

Dorimant

1282There is an inbred falsehood in women which inclines 'em still to them whom they may most easily deceive.

Loveit

1283The man who loves above his quality does not suffer more from the insolent impertinence of his mistress than the woman who loves above her understanding does from the arrogant presumptions of her friend.

Dorimant

1284You mistake the use of fools: they are designed for properties, and not for friends. You have an indifferent stock of reputation left yet. Lose it all like a frank gamester on the square; 'twill then be time enough to turn rook and cheat it up again on a good substantial bubble.

Loveit

1285The old and the ill-favoured are only fit for properties indeed, but young and handsome fools have met with kinder fortunes.

Dorimant

1286They have, to the shame of your sex be it spoken; 'twas this, the thought of this, made me, by a timely jealousy, endeavour to prevent the good fortune you are providing for Sir Fopling – but against a woman's frailty all our care is vain.

Loveit

1287Had I not with a dear experience bought the knowledge of your falsehood, you might have fooled me yet. This is not the first jealousy you have feigned to make a quarrel with me and get a week to throw away on some such unknown inconsiderable slut as you have been lately lurking with at plays.

Dorimant

1288Women, when they would break off with a man, never want th' address to turn the fault on him.

Loveit

1289You take a pride of late in using of me ill, that the town may know the power you have over me, which now (as unreasonably as yourself) expects that I (do me all the injuries you can) must love you still.

Dorimant

1290I am so far from expecting that you should, I begin to think you never did love me.

Loveit

1291Would the memory of it were so wholly worn out in me that I did doubt it too! What made you come to disturb my growing quiet?

Dorimant

1292To give you joy of your growing infamy.

Loveit

1293Insupportable! insulting devil! this from you, the only author of my shame! This from another had been but justice, but from you 'tis a hellish and inhuman outrage. What have I done?

Dorimant

1294A thing that puts you below my scorn and makes my anger as ridiculous as you have made my love.

Loveit

1295I walked last night with Sir Fopling.

Dorimant

1296You did, madam, and you talked and laughed aloud, ha, ha, ha! –Oh! that laugh! that laugh becomes the confidence of a woman of quality.

Loveit

1297You, who have more pleasure in the ruin of a woman's reputation than in the endearments of her love, reproach me not with yourself, and I defy you to name the man can lay a blemish on my fame.

Dorimant

1298To be seen publicly so transported with the vain follies of that notorious fop, to me is an infamy below the sin of prostitution with another man.

Loveit

1299Rail on, I am satisfied in the justice of what I did; you had provoked me to't.

Dorimant

1300What I did was the effect of a passion whose extravagances you have been willing to forgive.

Loveit

1301And what I did was the effect of a passion you may forgive if you think fit.

Dorimant

1302Are you so indifferent grown?

Loveit

1303I am.

Dorimant

1304Nay! then 'tis time to part. I'll send you back your letters you have so often asked for. I have two or three of 'em about me.

Loveit

1305Give 'em me.

Dorimant

1306You snatch as if you thought I would not –there– and may the perjuries in 'em be mine if e'er I see you more.

[Offers to go, she catches him.

Loveit

1307Stay!

Dorimant

1308I will not.

Loveit

1309You shall.

Dorimant

1310What have you to say?

Loveit

1311I cannot speak it yet.

Dorimant

1312Something more in commendation of the fool. Death! I want patience, let me go.

Loveit

1313[aside] I cannot. I can sooner part with the limbs that hold him. I hate that nauseous fool, you know I do.

Dorimant

1314Was it the scandal you were fond of then?

Loveit

1315You'd raised my anger equal to my love, a thing you ne'er could do before, and in revenge I did –I know not what I did. –Would you would not think on't any more!

Dorimant

1316Should I be willing to forget it, I shall be daily minded of it, 'twill be a commonplace for all the town to laugh at me; and Medley, when he is rhetorically drunk, will ever be declaiming on it in my ears.

Loveit

1317'Twill be believed a jealous spite! Come, forget, it.

Dorimant

1318Let me consult my reputation; you are too careless of it.
[pauses.] You shall meet Sir Fopling in the Mall again to-night.

Loveit

1319What mean you?

Dorimant

1320I have thought on it, and you must: 'tis necessary to justify my love to the world; you can handle a coxcomb as he deserves when you are not out of humour, madam.

Loveit

1321Public satisfaction for the wrong I have done you! This is some new device to make me more ridiculous.

Dorimant

1322Hear me.

Loveit

1323I will not.

Dorimant

1324You will be persuaded.

Loveit

1325Never.

Dorimant

1326Are you so obstinate?

Loveit

1327Are you so base?

Dorimant

1328You will not satisfy my love?

Loveit

1329I would die to satisfy that, but I will not to save from a thousand racks do a shameless thing to please your vanity.

Dorimant

1330Farewell, false woman!

Loveit

1331Do! go!

Dorimant

1332You will call me back again.

Loveit

1333Exquisite fiend! I knew you came but to torment me.

Enter BELINDA and PERT.

Dorimant

1334[surprised] Belinda here!

Belinda

1335[aside] He starts and looks pale; the sight of me has touched his guilty soul.

Pert

1336'Twas but a qualm, as I said, a little indigestion; the surfeit water did it, madam, mixed with a little mirabilis.

Dorimant

1337I am confounded, and cannot guess how she came hither!

Loveit

1338'Tis your fortune, Belinda, ever to be here when I am abused by this prodigy of ill-nature.

Belinda

1339I am amazed to find him here! How has he the face to come near you?

Dorimant

1340[aside] Here is fine work towards! I never was at such a loss before.

Belinda

1341One who makes a public profession of breach of faith and ingratitude; I loathe the sight of him.

Dorimant

1342[aside] There is no remedy; I must submit to their tongues now, and some other time bring myself off as well as I can.

Belinda

1343Other men are wicked, but then they have some sense of shame: he is never well but when he triumphs, nay, glories to a woman's face in his villainies.

Loveit

1344You are in the right, Belinda; but methinks your kindness for me makes you concern yourself too much with him.

Belinda

1345It does indeed, my dear; his barbarous carriage to you yesterday made me hope you ne'er would see him more, and the very next day to find him here again provokes me strangely; but, because I know you love him, I have done.

Dorimant

1346You have reproached me handsomely, and I deserve it for coming hither; but –

Pert

1347You must expect it, sir; all women will hate you for my lady's sake.

Dorimant

1348[aside to BELINDA]. Nay, if she begins too, 'tis time to fly; I shall be scolded to death else. I am to blame in some circumstances, I confess; but as to the main, I am not so guilty as you imagine. I shall seek a more convenient time to clear myself.

Loveit

1349Do it now! what impediments are here?

Dorimant

1350I want time, and you want temper.

Loveit

1351These are weak pretences!

Dorimant

1352You were never more mistaken in your life, and so farewell.

[DORIMANT flings off.

Loveit

1353Call a footman, Pert, quickly; I will have him dogged.

Pert

1354I wish you would not for my quiet and your own.

Loveit

1355I'll find out the infamous cause of all our quarrels, pluck her mask off, and expose her barefaced to the world.

Belinda

1356[aside] Let me but escape this time I'll never venture more.

Loveit

1357Belinda! you shall go with me.

Belinda

1358I have such a heaviness hangs on me with what I did this morning, I would fain go home and sleep, my dear.

Loveit

1359Death and eternal darkness! I shall never sleep again. Raging fevers seize the world, and make mankind as restless all as I am!

[Exit LOVEIT.

Belinda

1360I knew him false, and helped to make him so. Was not her ruin enough to fright me from the danger? It should have been, but love can take no warning.

[Exit BELINDA.

SCENE II. –

Lady TOWNLEY'S house

Enter MEDLEY, YOUNG BELLAIR, Lady TOWNLEY, EMILIA, and Chaplain.

Medley

1361Bear up, Bellair, and do not let us see that repentance in thine we daily do in married faces.

Lady Townley

1362This wedding will strangely surprise my brother when he knows it.

Medley

1363Your nephew ought to conceal it for a time, madam, since marriage has lost its good name; prudent men seldom expose their own reputations till 'tis convenient to justify their wives.

Old Bellair

1364[without] Where are you all there? Out, adod, will nobody hear?

Lady Townley

1365My brother! quickly, Mr. Smirk, into this closet; you must not be seen yet.

[He goes into the closet.

Enter OLD BELLAIR and Lady TOWNLEY's Page.

Old Bellair

1366Desire Mr. Fourbes to walk into the lover parlour, I will be with him presently.
[To YOUNG BELLAIR.] Where have you been, sir, you could not wait on me to-day?

Young Bellair

1367About a business.

Old Bellair

1368Are you so good at business? Adod, I have a business too you shall despatch out of hand, sir. Send for a parson, sister; my Lady Woodvil and her daughter are coming.

Lady Townley

1369What need you huddle up things thus?

Old Bellair

1370Out a pise! youth is apt to play the fool, and 'tis not good it should be in their power.

Lady Townley

1371You need not fear your son.

Old Bellair

1372He's been idling this morning, and, adod, I do not like him.
[To EMILIA] How dost thou do, sweetheart?

Emilia

1373You are very severe, sir; married in such haste.

Old Bellair

1374Go to, thou'rt a rogue, and I will talk with thee anon. Here's my Lady Woodvil come. Enter Lady WOODVIL, HARRIET, and BUSY.
Welcome, madam; Mr. Fourbes is below with the writings.

Lady Woodvil

1375Let us down, and make an end then.

Old Bellair

1376Sister, show the way. [To YOUNG BELLAIR, who is talking to HARRIET.]
Harry, your business lies not there yet; excuse him till we have done, lady, and then, adod, he shall be for thee. Mr. Medley, we must trouble you to be a witness.

Medley

1377I luckily came for that purpose, sir.

[Exeunt OLD BELLAIR, MEDLEY, YOUNG BELLAIR, Lady TOWNLEY, and Lady WOODVIL.

Busy

1378What will you do, madam?

Harriet

1379Be carried back and mewed up in the country again, run away here, anything rather than be married to a man I do not care for –Dear Emilia, do thou advise me.

Emilia

1380Mr. Bellair is engaged, you know.

Harriet

1381I do; but know not what the fear of losing an estate may fright him to.

Emilia

1382In the desperate condition you are in you should consult with some judicious man; what think you of Mr. Dorimant?

Harriet

1383I do not think of him at all.

Busy

1384She thinks of nothing else, I am sure.

Emilia

1385How fond your mother was of Mr. Courtage!

Harriet

1386Because I contrived the mistake to make a little mirth you believe I like the man.

Emilia

1387Mr. Bellair believes you love him.

Harriet

1388Men are seldom in the right when they guess at a woman's mind; would she whom he loves loved him no better!

Busy

1389[aside] That's e'en well enough, on all conscience.

Emilia

1390Mr. Dorimant has a great deal of wit.

Harriet

1391And takes a great deal of pains to show it.

Emilia

1392He's extremely well-fashioned.

Harriet

1393Affectedly grave or ridiculous wild and apish.

Busy

1394You defend him still against your mother.

Harriet

1395I would not were he justly rallied, but I cannot hear any one undeservedly railed at.

Emilia

1396Has your woman learnt the song you were so taken with?

Harriet

1397I was fond of a new thing; 'tis dull at second hearing.

Emilia

1398Mr. Dorimant made it.

Busy

1399She knows it, madam, and has made me sing it at least a dozen times this morning.

Harriet

1400Thy tongue is as impertinent as thy fingers.

Emilia

1401You have provoked her.

Busy

1402'Tis but singing the song, and I shall appease her.

Emilia

1403Prithee do.

Harriet

1404She has a voice will grate your ears worse than a cat-call, and dresses so ill she's scarce fit to trick up a yeoman's daughter on a holiday.

Busy

1405[sings.] As Amoret with Phyllis sat
One evening on the plain,
And saw the charming Strephon wait
To tell the nymph his pain,
The threat'ning danger to remove
She whisper'd in her ear,
Ah, Phyllis! if you would not love,
This shepherd do not hear.
None ever had so strange an art
His passion to convey
Into a list'ning virgin's heart,
And steal her soul away.
Fly, fly betimes, for fear you give
Occasion for your fate.
In vain, said she, in vain I strive,
Alas! 'tis now too late.

Enter DORIMANT.

Dorimant

1406Music so softens and disarms the mind –

Harriet

1407That not one arrow does resistance find.

Dorimant

1408Let us make use of the lucky minute then.

Harriet

1409[aside, turning from DORIMANT]. My love springs with my blood into my face, I dare not look upon him yet.

Dorimant

1410What have we here, the picture of celebrated beauty giving audience in public to a declared lover?

Harriet

1411Play the dying fop and make the piece complete, sir.

Dorimant

1412What think you if the hint were well improved –the whole mystery of making love pleasantly designed and wrought in a suit of hangings?

Harriet

1413'Twere needless to execute fools in effigy who suffer daily in their own persons.

Dorimant

1414[aside to EMILIA] Mrs. Bride, for such I know this happy day has made you.

Emilia

1415Defer the formal joy you are to give me and mind your business with her.
[Aloud] Here are dreadful preparations, Mr. Dorimant, writings sealing, and a parson sent for.

Dorimant

1416To marry this lady?

Busy

1417Condemned she is, and what will become of her I know not, without you generously engage in a rescue.

Dorimant

1418In this sad condition, madam, I can do no less than offer you my service.

Harriet

1419The obligation is not great; you are the common sanctuary for all young women who run from their relations.

Dorimant

1420I have always my arms pen to receive the distressed. But I will open my heart, and receive you where none yet did ever enter: you have filled it with a secret, might I but let you know it –

Harriet

1421Do not speak it if you would have me believe it; your tongue is so famed for falsehood 'twill do the truth an injury.

[Turns away her head.

Dorimant

1422Turn not away then; but look on me and guess it.

Harriet

1423Did you not tell me there was no credit to be given to faces? that women nowadays have their passions as much at will as they have their complexions, and put on joy and sadness, scorn and kindness, with the same ease they do their paint and patches –Are they the only counterfeits?

Dorimant

1424You wrong your own while you suspect my eyes; by all the hope I have in you, the inimitable colour in your cheeks is not more free from art than are the sighs I offer.

Harriet

1425In men who have been long hardened in sin we have reason to mistrust the first signs of repentance.

Dorimant

1426The prospect of such a heaven will make me persevere and give you marks that are infallible.

Harriet

1427What are those?

Dorimant

1428I will renounce all the joys I have in friendship and in wine, sacrifice to you all the interest I have in other women –

Harriet

1429Hold! –though I wish you devout I would not have you turn fanatic –Could you neglect these awhile and make a journey into the country?

Dorimant

1430To be with you I could live there and never send one thought to London.

Harriet

1431Whate'er you say, I know all beyond High Park's a desert to you, and that no gallantry can draw you farther.

Dorimant

1432That has been the utmost limit of my love, but now my passion knows no bounds, and there's no measure to be taken of what I'll do for you from anything I ever did before.

Harriet

1433When I hear you talk thus in Hampshire I shall begin to think there may be some truth enlarged upon.

Dorimant

1434Is this all? –will you not promise me?–

Harriet

1435I hate to promise! What we do then is expected from us, and wants much of the welcome it finds when it surprises.

Dorimant

1436May I not hope?

Harriet

1437That depends on you and not on me; and 'tis to no purpose to forbid it.

[Turns to BUSY]

Busy

1438Faith, madam, now I perceive the gentleman loves you too; e'en let him know your mind, and torment yourselves no longer.

Harriet

1439Dost think I have no sense of modesty?

Busy

1440Think, if you lose this you may never have another opportunity.

Harriet

1441May he hate me –a curse that frights me when I speak it –if ever I do a thing against the rules of decency and honour!

Dorimant

1442[to EMILIA] I am beholding to you for your good intentions, madam.

Emilia

1443I thought the concealing of our marriage from her might have done you better service.

Dorimant

1444Try her again.

Emilia

1445What have you resolved, madam? The time draws near.

Harriet

1446To be obstinate, and protest against this marriage.

Enter Lady TOWNLEY in haste.

Lady Townley

1447[To EMILIA] Quickly, quickly, let Mr. Smirk out of the closet.

[SMIRK comes out of the closet.

Harriet

1448A parson! had you laid him in here?

Dorimant

1449I knew nothing of him.

Harriet

1450Should it appear you did, your opinion of my easiness may cost you dear.

Enter OLD BELLAIR, YOUNG BELLAIR, MEDLEY, and Lady WOODVIL.

Old Bellair

1451Out a pise! the canonical hour is almost past. Sister, is the man of God come?

Lady Townley

1452He waits your leisure.

Old Bellair

1453By your favour, sir. Adod, a pretty spruce fellow! what may we call him?

Lady Townley

1454Mr. Smirk, my Lady Biggot's chaplain.

Old Bellair

1455A wise woman! adod, she is. The man will serve for the flesh as well as the spirit. Please you, sir, to commission a young couple to go to bed together i' God's name? Harry.

Young Bellair

1456Here, sir.

Old Bellair

1457Out a pise! without your mistress in your hand!

Smirk

1458Is this the gentleman?

Old Bellair

1459Yes, sir.

Smirk

1460Are you not mistaken, sir?

Old Bellair

1461Adod, I think not, sir.

Smirk

1462Sure you are, sir.

Old Bellair

1463You look as if you would forbid the banns; Mr. Smirk, I hope you have no pretension to the lady?

Smirk

1464Wish him joy, sir! I have done him the good office to-day already.

Old Bellair

1465Out a pise! what do I hear?

Lady Townley

1466Never storm, brother, the truth is out.

Old Bellair

1467How say you, sir? is this your wedding-day?

Young Bellair

1468It is, sir.

Old Bellair

1469And, adod, it shall be mine too; give me thy hand, sweetheart.
[To EMILIA] What dost thou mean? give me thy hand, I say.

[EMILIA kneels, and YOUNG BELLAIR.

Lady Townley
