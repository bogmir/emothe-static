ACT III

SCENE I. –

Lady WOODVIL'S Lodgings.

Enter HARRIET and BUSY her woman.

Busy

419Dear madam! Let me set that curl in order.

Harriet

420Let me alone, I will shake 'em all out of order.

Busy

421Will you never leave this wildness?

Harriet

422Torment me not.

Busy

423Look! there's a knot falling off.

Harriet

424Let it drop.

Busy

425But one pin, dear madam.

Harriet

426How do I daily suffer under thy officious fingers!

Busy

427Ah, the difference that is between you and my Lady Dapper! How uneasy she is if the least thing be amiss about her!

Harriet

428She is indeed most exact; nothing is ever wanting to make her ugliness remarkable.

Busy

429Jeering people say so.

Harriet

430Her powdering, painting, and her patching never fail in public to draw the tongues and eyes of all the men upon her.

Busy

431She is indeed a little too pretending.

Harriet

432That women should set up for beauty as much in spite of nature as some men have done for wit!

Busy

433I hope, without offence, one may endeavour to make oneself agreeable.

Harriet

434Not when 'tis impossible. Women then ought to be no more fond of dressing than fools should be of talking. Hoods and modesty, masks and silence, things that shadow and conceal: they should think of nothing else.

Busy

435Jesu! madam, what will your mother think is become of you? For heaven's sake, go in again.

Harriet

436I won't.

Busy

437This is the extravagant'st thing that ever you did in your life, to leave her and a gentleman who is to be your husband.

Harriet

438My husband! Hast thou so little wit to think I spoke what I meant when I overjoyed her in the country with a low curtsey and What you please, madam, I shall ever be obedient?

Busy

439Nay, I know not, you have so many fetches.

Harriet

440And this was one to get her up to London; nothing else, I assure thee.

Busy

441Well, the man, in my mind, is a fine man.

Harriet

442The man indeed wears his clothes fashionably, and has a pretty negligent way with him, very courtly and much affected; he bows, and talks, and smiles so agreeably as he thinks.

Busy

443I never saw anything so genteel.

Harriet

444Varnished over with good breeding many a blockhead makes a tolerable show.

Busy

445I wonder you do not like him.

Harriet

446I think I might be brought to endure him, and that is all a reasonable woman should expect in a husband; but there is duty i' the case –and like the haughty Merab,

I find much aversion in my stubborn mind,
Which is bred by being promised and design'd.

Busy

447I wish you do not design your own ruin! I partly guess your inclinations, madam, –that Mr. Dorimant –

Harriet

448Leave your prating, and sing some foolish song or other.

Busy

449I will; the song you love so well ever since you saw Mr. Dorimant.

When first Amintas charm'd my heart,
My heedless sheep began to stray;
The wolves soon stole the greatest part,
And all will now be made a prey.
Ah! let not love your thoughts possess,
'Tis fatal to al shepherdess;
The dang'rous passion you must shun,
Or else, like me, be quite undone.

Harriet

450Shall I be paid down by a covetous parent for a purchase? I need no land; no, I'll lay myself out all in love. It is decreed –

Enter YOUNG BELLAIR.

Young Bellair

451What generous resolution are you making, madam?

Harriet

452Only to be disobedient, sir.

Young Bellair

453Let me join hands with you in that.

Harriet

454With all my heart; I never thought I should have given you mine so willingly. Here I, Harriet –

Young Bellair

455And I, Harry –

Harriet

456Do solemnly protest –

Young Bellair

457And vow –

Harriet

458That I with you –

Young Bellair

459And I with you –

Both.

460Will never marry.

Harriet

461A match!

Young Bellair

462And no match! How do you like this indifference now?

Harriet

463You expect I should take it ill, I see.

Young Bellair

464'Tis not unnatural for you women to be a little angry [if] you miss a conquest, though you would slight the poor man were he in your power.

Harriet

465There are some, it may be, have an eye like Bartholomew, big enough for the whole fair, but I am not of the number, and you may keep your gingerbread: 'twill be more acceptable to the lady whose dear image it wears, sir.

Young Bellair

466I must confess, madam, you came a day after the fair.

Harriet

467You own then you are in love.

Young Bellair

468I do.

Harriet

469The confidence is generous, and in return I could almost find in my heart to let you know my inclinations.

Young Bellair

470Are you in love?

Harriet

471Yes, with this dear town, to that degree I can scarce endure the country in landscapes and in hangings.

Young Bellair

472What a dreadful thing 'twould be to be hurried back to Hampshire?

Harriet

473Ah! name it not!

Young Bellair

474As for us, I find we shall agree well enough! Would we could do something to deceive the grave people!

Harriet

475Could we delay their quick proceeding, 'twere well; a reprieve is a good step towards the getting of a pardon.

Young Bellair

476If we give over the game we are undone; what think you of playing it on booty?

Harriet

477What do you mean?

Young Bellair

478Pretend to be in love with one another; 'twill make some dilatory excuses we may feign pass the better.

Harriet

479Let us do't, if it be but for the dear pleasure of dissembling.

Young Bellair

480Can you play your part?

Harriet

481I know not what it is to love, but I have made pretty remarks by being now and then where lovers meet. Where did you leave their gravities?

Young Bellair

482I' th' next room; your mother was censuring our modern gallant.

Enter OLD BELLAIR and Lady WOODVIL.

Harriet

483Peace! Here they come, I will lean against this wall and look bashfully down upon my fan, while you like an amorous spark modishly entertain me.

Lady Woodvil

484Never go about to excuse 'em; come, come, it was not so when I was a young woman.

Old Bellair

485Adod, they're something disrespectful.

Lady Woodvil

486Quality was then considered, and not rallied by every fleering fellow.

Old Bellair

487Youth will have its jest, adod it will.

Lady Woodvil

488'Tis good breeding now to be civil to none but players and Exchange women; they are treated by 'em as much above their condition as others are below theirs.

Old Bellair

489Out, a pise on 'em! talk no more; the rogues ha got an ill habit of preferring beauty, no matter where they find it.

Lady Woodvil

490See your son and my daughter, they have improved their acquaintance since they were within.

Old Bellair

491Adod, methinks they have; let's keep back and observe.

Young Bellair

492Now for a look and gestures that may persuade 'em I am saying all the passionate things imaginable.

Harriet

493Your head a little more on one side, ease yourself on your left leg, and play with your right hand.

Young Bellair

494Thus, is it not?

Harriet

495Now set your right leg firm on the ground, adjust your belt, then look about you.

Young Bellair

496A little exercising will make me perfect.

Harriet

497Smile, and turn to me again very sparkish.

Young Bellair

498Will you take your turn and be instructed?

Harriet

499With all my heart.

Young Bellair

500At one motion play your fan, roll your eyes, and then settle a kind look upon me.

Harriet

501So.

Young Bellair

502Now spread your fan, look down upon it, and tell the sticks with a finger.

Harriet

503Very modish!

Young Bellair

504Clap your hand up to your bosom, hold down your gown; shrug a little, draw up your breasts, and let 'em fall again gently, with a sigh or two, etc.

Harriet

505By the good instructions you give, I suspect you for one of those malicious observers who watch people's eyes and from innocent looks make scandalous conclusions.

Young Bellair

506I know some, indeed, who, out of mere love to mischief, are as vigilant as jealousy itself, and will give you an account of every glance that passes at a play and i' th' circle.

Harriet

507'Twill not be amiss now to seem a little pleasant.

Young Bellair

508Clap your fan then in both your hands, snatch it to your mouth, smile, and with a lively motion fling your body a little forwards. So, –now spread it; fall back on the sudden, cover your face with it, and break out in to a loud laughter – take up! look grave, and fall a-fanning of yourself –admirably well acted.

Harriet

509I think I am pretty apt at these matters.

Old Bellair

510Adod, I like this well.

Lady Woodvil

511This promises something.

Old Bellair

512Come! there is love i' th' case, adod there is, or will be; what say you, young lady?

Harriet

513All in good time, sir; you expect we should fall to and love, as gamecocks fight, as soon as we are set together; adod, you're unreasonable!

Old Bellair

514Adod, sirrah, I like thy wit well.

Enter a Servant.

Servant

515The coach is at the door, madam.

Old Bellair

516Go, get you and take the air together.

Lady Woodvil

517Will not you go with us?

Old Bellair

518Out a pise. Adod, I ha' business and cannot. We shall meet at night at my sister Townley's.

Young Bellair

519[aside] He's going to Emilia. I overheard him talk of a collation.

[Exeunt.

SCENE II

Enter Lady TOWNLEY, EMILIA, and Mr. MEDLEY.

Lady Townley

520I pity the young lovers we last talked of; though, to say truth, their conduct has been so indiscreet they deserve to be unfortunate.

Medley

521You've had an exact account, from the great lady i' th' box down to the little orange-wench.

Emilia

522You're a living libel, a breathing lampoon; I wonder you are not torn in pieces.

Medley

523What think you of setting up an office of intelligence for these matters? The project may get money.

Lady Townley

524You would have great dealings with country ladies.

Medley

525More than Muddiman has with their husbands.

Enter BELINDA.

Lady Townley

526Belinda, what has been become of you? we have not seen you here of late with your friend Mrs. Loveit.

Belinda

527Dear creature, I left her but no so sadly afflicted.

Lady Townley

528With her old distemper, jealousy?

Medley

529Dorimant has played her some new prank.

Belinda

530Well, that Dorimant is certainly the worst man breathing.

Emilia

531I once thought so.

Belinda

532And do you not think so still?

Emilia

533No, indeed!

Belinda

534Oh, Jesu!

Emilia

535The town does him a great deal of injury, and I will never believe what it says of a man I do not know again, for his sake.

Belinda

536You make me wonder!

Lady Townley

537He's a very well-bred man.

Belinda

538But strangely ill-natured.

Emilia

539Then he's a very witty man.

Belinda

540But a man of no principles.

Medley

541Your man of principles is a very fine thing indeed!

Belinda

542To be preferred to men of parts by women who have regard to their reputation and quiet. Well, were I minded to play the fool, he should be the last man I'd think of.

Medley

543He has been the first in many lady's favours, though you are so severe, madam.

Lady Townley

544What he may be for a lover I know not, but he's a very pleasant acquaintance, I am sure.

Belinda

545Had you seen him use Mrs. Loveit as I have done, you would never endure him more.

Emilia

546What, he has quarrelled with her again?

Belinda

547Upon the slightest occasion; he's jealous of Sir Fopling.

Lady Townley

548She never saw him in her life but yesterday, and that was there.

Emilia

549On my conscience, he's the only man in town that's her aversion; how horribly out of humour she was all the while he talked to her!

Belinda

550And somebody has wickedly told him –

Emilia

551Here he comes.

Enter DORIMANT.

Medley

552Dorimant! you are luckily come to justify yourself – here's a lady –

Belinda

553Has a word or two to say to you from a disconsolate person.

Dorimant

554You tender your reputation too much, I know, madam, to whisper with me before this good company.

Belinda

555To serve Mrs. Loveit, I'll make a bold venture.

Dorimant

556Here's Medley, the very spirit of scandal.

Belinda

557No matter!

Emilia

558'Tis something you are unwilling to hear, Mr. Dorimant.

Lady Townley

559Tell him, Belinda, whether he will or no.

Belinda

560[aloud] Mrs. Loveit –

Dorimant

561Softly, these are laughers, you do not know 'em.

Belinda

562[to DORIMANT, apart] In a word, you've made me hate you, which I thought you never could have done.

Dorimant

563In obeying your commands.

Belinda

564'Twas a cruel part you played! how could you act it?

Dorimant

565Nothing is cruel to a man who could kill himself to please you; remember, five o'clock to-morrow morning.

Belinda

566I tremble when you name it.

Dorimant

567Be sure you come.

Belinda

568I sha' not.

Dorimant

569Swear you will.

Belinda

570I dare not.

Dorimant

571Swear, I say.

Belinda

572By my life! by all the happiness I hope for –

Dorimant

573You will.

Belinda

574I will.

Dorimant

575Kind.

Belinda

576I am glad I've sworn, I vow I think I should ha' failed you else!

Dorimant

577Surprisingly kind! In what temper did you leave Loveit?

Belinda

578Her raving was prettily over, and she began to be in a brave way of defying you and all your works. Where have you been since you went from thence?

Dorimant

579I looked in at the play.

Belinda

580I have promised, and must return to her again.

Dorimant

581Persuade her to walk in the Mall this evening.

Belinda

582She hates the place, and will not come.

Dorimant

583Do all you can to prevail with her.

Belinda

584For what purpose?

Dorimant

585Sir Fopling will be here anon; I'll prepare him to set upon her there before me.

Belinda

586You persecute her too much; but I'll do all you'll ha' me.

Dorimant

587[aloud] Tell her plainly, 'tis grown so dull a business I can drudge on no longer.

Emilia

588There are afflictions in love, Mr. Dorimant.

Dorimant

589You women make 'em, who are commonly as unreasonable in that as you are at play; without the advantage be on your side a man can never quietly give over when he's weary.

Medley

590If you would play without being obliged to complaisance, Dorimant, you should play in public places.

Dorimant

591Ordinaries were a very good thing for that, but gentlemen do not of late frequent 'em; the deep play is now in private houses.

[BELINDA offering to steal away.

Lady Townley

592Belinda, are you leaving us so soon?

Belinda

593I am to go to the Park with Mrs. Loveit, madam.

[Exit BELINDA.

Lady Townley

594This confidence will go nigh to spoil this young creature.

Medley

595'Twill do her good, madam. Young men who are brought up under practising lawyers prove the abler counsel when they come to be called to the Bar themselves.

Dorimant

596The town has been very favourable to you this afternoon, my Lady Townley; you use to have an embarrass of chairs and coaches at your door, an uproar of footmen in your hall, and a noise of fools above here.

Lady Townley

597Indeed my house is the general rendezvous, and, next to the playhouse, is the common refuge of all the young idle people.

Emilia

598Company is a very good thing, madam, but I wonder you do not love it a little more chosen.

Lady Townley

599'Tis good to have an universal taste; we should love wit, but for variety be able to divert ourselves with the extravagancies of those who want it.

Medley

600Fools will make you laugh.

Emilia

601For once or twice; but the repetition of their folly after a visit or two grows tedious and unsufferable.

Lady Townley

602You are a little too delicate, Emilia.

Enter a Page.

Page

603Sir Fopling Flutter, madam, desires to know if you are to be seen.

Lady Townley

604Here's the freshest fool in town, and one who has not cloyed you yet. Page!

Page

605Madam!

Lady Townley

606Desire him to walk up.

[Exit Page.

Dorimant

607Do not you fall on him, Medley, and snub him. Soothe him up in his extravagance; he will show the better.

Medley

608You know I have a natural indulgence for fools, and need not his caution, sir.

Enter SIR FOPLING FLUTTER, with his Page after him.

Sir Fopling

609Page, wait without. Madam, [To LADY TOWNLEY.] I kiss your hands. I see yesterday was nothing of chance; the belles assemblées form themselves here every day. [To EMILIA.], Lady, your servant. Dorimant, let me embrace thee; without lying, I have not met with any of my acquaintance who retain so much of Paris as thou dost –the very air thou hadst when the marquis mistook thee i' th' Tuileries, and cried, Hey! Chevalier! and then begged thy pardon.

Dorimant

610I would fain wear in fashion as long as I can, sir; 'tis a thing to be valued in men as well as baubles.

Sir Fopling

611Thou art a man of wit, and understandest the town; prithee let thee and I be intimate, there is no living without making some good man the confidant of our pleasures.

Dorimant

612'Tis true! but there is no man so improper for such a business as I am.

Sir Fopling

613Prithee, why hast thou so modest an opinion of thyself?

Dorimant

614Why, first, I could never keep a secret in my life, and then there is no charm so infallibly makes me fall in love with a woman as my knowing a friend loves her. I deal honestly with you.

Sir Fopling

615Thy humour's very gallant, or let me perish; I knew a French count so like thee.

Lady Townley

616Wit, I perceive, has more power over you than beauty, Sir Fopling, else you would not have let this lady stand so long neglected.

Sir Fopling

617[to EMILIA] A thousand pardons, madam; some civilities due, of course, upon the meeting a long absent friend. The éclat of so much beauty, I confess, ought to have charmed me sooner.

Emilia

618The brilliant of so much good language, sir, has much more power than the little beauty I can boast.

Sir Fopling

619I never saw anything prettier than this high work on your point d'Espagne. –

Emilia

620'Tis not so rich as point de Venise. –

Sir Fopling

621Not altogether, but looks cooler, and is more proper for the season. Dorimant, is not that Medley?

Dorimant

622The same, sir.

Sir Fopling

623Forgive me, sir; in this embarrass of civilities I could not come to have you in my arms sooner. You understand an equipage the best of any man in town, I hear.

Medley

624By my own you would not guess it.

Sir Fopling

625There are critics who do not write, sir.

Medley

626Our peevish poets will scarce allow it.

Sir Fopling

627Damn 'em, they'll allow no man wit who does not play the fool like themselves, and show it! Have you taken notice of the calèche I brought over?

Medley

628Oh, yes! 'T has quite another air than th' English makes.

Sir Fopling

629'Tis as easily known from an English tumbril as an Inns of Court man is from one of us.

Dorimant

630Truly, there is a bel-air in calèches as well as men.

Medley

631But there are few so delicate to observe it.

Sir Fopling

632The world is generally very grossier here, indeed.

Lady Townley

633Here's very fine.

Emilia

634Extreme proper.

Sir Fopling

635A slight suit I made to appear in at my first arrival, not worthy your consideration, ladies.

Dorimant

636The pantaloon is very well mounted.

Sir Fopling

637The tassels are new and pretty.

Medley

638I never saw a coat better cut.

Sir Fopling

639It makes me show long-waisted, and, I think, slender.

Dorimant

640That's the shape our ladies dote on.

Medley

641Your breech, though, is a handful too high in my eye, Sir Fopling.

Sir Fopling

642Peace, Medley; I have wished it lower a thousand times, but a pox on't, 'twill not be.

Lady Townley

643His gloves are well fringed, large and graceful.

Sir Fopling

644I was always eminent for being bien-gante.

Emilia

645He wears nothing but what are originals of the most famous hands in Paris.

Sir Fopling

646You are in the right, madam.

Lady Townley

647The suit?

Sir Fopling

648Barroy.

Emilia

649The garniture?

Sir Fopling

650Le Gras.

Medley

651The shoes?

Sir Fopling

652Piccat.

Dorimant

653The periwig?

Sir Fopling

654Chedreux.

Lady Town and Emilia

655The gloves?

Sir Fopling

656Orangerie: you know the smell, ladies. Dorimant, I could find in my heart for an amusement to have a gallantry with some of our English ladies.

Dorimant

657'Tis a thing no less necessary to confirm the reputation of your wit than a duel will be to satisfy the town of your courage.

Sir Fopling

658Here was a woman yesterday –

Dorimant

659Mistress Loveit.

Sir Fopling

660You have named her.

Dorimant

661You cannot pitch on a better for your purpose.

Sir Fopling

662Prithee, what is she?

Dorimant

663A person of quality, and one who has a rest of reputation enough to make the conquest considerable. Besides, I hear she likes you too.

Sir Fopling

664Methought she seemed, though, very reserved and uneasy all the time I entertained her.

Dorimant

665Grimace and affectation. You will see her i' th' Mall to-night.

Sir Fopling

666Prithee let thee and I take the air together.

Dorimant

667I am engaged to Medley, but I'll meet you at St. James's and give you some information upon the which you may regulate your proceedings.

Sir Fopling

668All the world will be in the Park to-night: ladies, 'twere pity to keep so much beauty longer within doors and rob the Ring of all those charms that should adorn it. –Hey, page! Enter Page, and goes out again.
[Exit SIR FOPLING. See that all my people be ready. Dorimant, au revoir!

Medley

669A fine mettled coxcomb.

Dorimant

670Brisk and insipid.

Medley

671Pert and dull.

Emilia

672However you despise him, gentlemen, I'll lay my life he passes for a wit with many.

Dorimant

673That may very well be; nature has her cheats, stums a brain, and puts sophisticate dulness often on the tasteless multitude for true wit and good-humour. Medley, come.

Medley

674I must go a little way, I will meet you i' the Mall.

Dorimant

675I'll walk through the garden thither. [To the Women.] We shall meet anon and bow.

Lady Townley

676Not to-night; we are engaged about a business the knowledge of which may make you laugh hereafter.

Medley

677Your servant, ladies.

Dorimant

678Au revoir! as Sir Fopling says.

[Exeunt MEDLEY and DORIMANT.

Lady Townley

679The old man will be here immediately.

Emilia

680Let's expect him i' th' garden.

Lady Townley

681Go, you are a rogue.

Emilia

682I can't abide you.

[Exeunt.

SCENE III. –

The Mall

Enter HARRIET and YOUNG BELLAIR, she pulling him.

Harriet

683Come along.

Young Bellair

684And leave your mother?

Harriet

685Busy will be sent with a hue and cry after us; but that's no matter.

Young Bellair

686'Twill look strangely in me.

Harriet

687She'll believe it a freak of mine and never blame your manners.

Young Bellair

688What reverend acquaintance is that she has met?

Harriet

689A fellow-beauty of the last King's time, though by the ruins you would hardly guess it.

[Exeunt.

Enter DORIMANT, and crosses the stage.

Enter YOUNG BELLAIR and HARRIET.

Young Bellair

690By this time your mother is in a fine taking.

Harriet

691If your friend Mr. Dorimant were but here now, that she might find me talking with him.

Young Bellair

692She does not know him, but dreads him, I hear, of all mankind.

Harriet

693She concludes if he does but speak to a woman she's undone; is on her kneed every day to pray Heav'n defend me from him.

Young Bellair

694You do not apprehend him so much as she does.

Harriet

695I never saw anything in him that was frightful.

Young Bellair

696On the contrary, have you not observed something extreme delightful in his wit and person?

Harriet

697He's agreeable and pleasant I must own, but he does so much affect being so, he displeases me.

Young Bellair

698Lord, madam, all he does and says is so easy and so natural.

Harriet

699Some men's verses seem so to the unskilful, but labour i' the one and affectation in the other to the judicious plainly appear.

Young Bellair

700I never heard him accused of affectation before.

Enter DORIMANT, and stares upon her.

Harriet

701It passes on the easy town, who are favourably pleased in him to call it humour.

[Exeunt YOUNG BELLAIR and HARRIET.

Dorimant

702'Tis she! it must be she, that lovely hair, that easy shape, those wanton eyes, and all those melting charms about her mouth which Medley spoke of; I'll follow the lottery, and put in for a prize with my friend Bellair.

[Exit DORIMANT repeating:

In love the victors from the vanquish'd fly:
They fly that wound, and they pursue that die.

Enter YOUNG BELLAIR and HARRIET, and after them DORIMANT, standing at a distance.

Young Bellair

703Most people prefer High Park to this place.

Harriet

704It has the better reputation, I confess; but I abominate the dull diversions there, the formal bows, the affected smiles, the silly by-words, and amorous tweers in passing; here one meets with a little conversations now and then.

Young Bellair

705These conversations have been fatal to some of your sex, madam.

Harriet

706It may be so; because some who want temper have been undone by gaming, must others who have it wholly deny themselves the pleasure of play?

Dorimant

707Trust me, it were unreasonable, madam.

[Coming up gently, and bowing to her.

Harriet

708Lord! who's this?

[She starts, and looks grave.

Young Bellair

709Dorimant.

Dorimant

710Is this the woman your father would have you marry?

Young Bellair

711It is.

Dorimant

712Her name?

Young Bellair

713Harriet.

Dorimant

714I am not mistaken, she's handsome.

Young Bellair

715Talk to her, her wit is better than her face; we were wishing for you but now.

Dorimant

716[to HARRIET] Overcast with seriousness o' the sudden! A thousand smiles were shining in that face but now; I never saw so quick a change of weather.

Harriet

717[aside] I feel as great a change within; but he shall never know it.

Dorimant

718You were talking of play, madam; pray what may be your stint?

Harriet

719A little harmless discourse in public walks, or at most a appointment in a box barefaced at the playhouse; you are for masks and private meetings where women engage for all they are worth, I hear.

Dorimant

720I have been used to deep play, but I can make one at small game when I like my gamester well.

Harriet

Dorimant

Harriet

Dorimant

Harriet

725To men who have fared in this town like you, 'twould be a great mortification to live on hope; could you keep a Lent for a mistress?

Dorimant

726In expectation of a happy Easter, and though time be very precious, think forty days well lost to gain your favour.

Harriet

727Mr. Bellair! let us walk, 'tis time to leave him; men grow dull when they begin to be particular.

Dorimant

728You're mistaken, flattery will not ensue, though I know you're greedy of the praises of the whole Mall.

Harriet

729You do me wrong.

Dorimant

730I do not; as I followed you I observed how you were pleased when the fops cried: "She's handsome, very handsome, By God she is," and whispered aloud your name, the thousand several forms you put your face into; then, to make yourself more agreeable, how wantonly you played with your head, flung back your locks, and looked smilingly over your shoulder at 'em.

Harriet

731I do not begging the men's, as you do the ladies' good liking, with a sly softness in your looks and a gentle slowness in your bows as you pass by 'em –as thus, sir; –
[Acts him.] Is not this like you?
