George Etherege

The Man of Mode, or Sir Fopling Flutter

← Prologue ACT II →

ACT I

SCENE I. –

A Dressing-room. A table covered with a toilet; clothes laid ready.

Enter DORIMANT in his gown and slippers, with a note in his hand made up, repeating verses.

Dorimant

2Now for some ages had the pride of Spain
Made the sun shine on half the world in vain.

[Then looking on the note.

[For Mrs. LOVEIT.]

What a dull insipid thing is a billet-doux written in cold blood, after the heat of the business is over! It is a tax upon good-nature which I have here been labouring to pay, and have done it, but with as much regret as ever fanatic paid the Royal Aid or Church Duties, 'Twill have the same fate, I know, that all my notes to her have had of late, 'twill not be thought kind enough. Faith, women are i' the right when they jealousy examine our letters, for in them we always first discover our decay of passion. –Hey! Who waits?

Enter HANDY

Handy

3Sir –

Dorimant

4Call a footman.

Handy

5None of 'em are come yet.

Dorimant

6Dogs! Will they ever lie snoring a-bed till noon?

Handy

7'Tis all one, sir: if they 're up, you indulge 'em so they're ever poaching after whores all the morning.

Dorimant

8Take notice henceforward, who's wanting in his duty, the next clap he gets, he shall rot for an example. What vermin are those chattering without?

Handy

9Foggy Nan the orange-woman and swearing Tom the shoemaker.

Dorimant

10Go; call in that overgrown jade with the flasket of guts before her; fruit is refreshing in a morning.

[Exit HANDY.

It is not that I love you less
Than when before your feet I lay.

Enter ORANGE-WOMAN with HANDY.

How now, Double Tripe! what news do you bring?

Orange-Woman

11News! Here's the best fruit has come to town t'year; gad, I was up before four a'clock this morning, and bought all the choice i' the market.

Dorimant

12The nasty refuse of your shop.

Orange-Woman

13You need not make mouths at it; I assure you 'tis all culled ware.

Dorimant

14The citizens buy better on a holiday in their walk to Totnam.

Orange-Woman

15Good or bad, tis all one; I never knew you commend anything. Lord! would the ladies had heard you talk of 'em as I have done. Here, bid you man give me an angel.

[Sets down the fruit.

Dorimant

16Give the bawd her fruit again.

Orange-Woman

17Well, on my conscience, there never was the like of you. God's my life, I had almost forgot to tell you there is a young gentlewoman lately come to town with her mother, that is so taken with you.

Dorimant

18Is she handsome?

Orange-Woman

19Nay, gad, there are few finer women, I tell you but so, and a hugeous fortune, they say. Here, eat this peach, it comes from the stone; 'tis better than any Newington y' have tasted.

Dorimant

20This fine woman, I'll lay my life,
[Taking the peach.] is some awkward, ill-fashioned, country toad, who, not having above four dozen of black hairs on her head, has adorned her baldness with a large white fruz, that she may look sparkishly in the forefront of the King's box at an old play.

Orange-Woman

21Gad, you'd change your note quickly if you did but see her.

Dorimant

22How came she to know me?

Orange-Woman

23She saw you yesterday at the Change; she told me you came and fooled with the woman at the next shop.

Dorimant

24I remember there was a mask observed me indeed. Fooled, did she say?

Orange-Woman

25Ay, I vow she told me twenty things you said too; and acted with her head and with her body so like you –

Enter MEDLEY.

Medley

26Dorimant, my life, my joy, my darling sin, how dost tou?

Orange-Woman

27Lord! what a filthy trick these men have got of kissing one another!

[She spits.

Medley

28Why do you suffer this cartload of scandal to come near you and make your neighbours think you so improvident to need a bawd?

Orange-Woman

29Good, now we shall have it! you did but want him to help you; come, pay me for my fruit.

Medley

30Make us thankful for it, huswife; bawds are as much out of fashion as gentlemen-ushers: none but old formal ladies use the one, and none but foppish old stagers employ the other – go, you are an insignificant brandy bottle.

Dorimant

31Nay, there are wrong her, three quarts of canary is her business.

Orange-Woman

32What you please, gentlemen.

Dorimant

33To him! give him as good as he brings.

Orange-Woman

34Hang him, there is not such another heathen in the town again, except it be the shoemaker without.

Medley

35I shall see you hold up your hand at the bar next sessions for murder, huswife; that shoemaker can take his oath you are in fee with the doctor's to sell green fruit to the gentry, that the crudities may breed diseases.

Orange-Woman

36Pray give me my money.

Dorimant

37Not a penny; when you bring the gentlewoman hither you spoke of, you shall be paid.

Orange-Woman

38The gentlewoman! the gentlewoman may be as honest as your sisters, for aught as I know. Pray pay me, Mr. Dorimant, and do not abuse me so; I have an honester way of living, you know it.

Medley

39Was there ever such a resty bawd?

Dorimant

40Some jade's tricks she has, but she makes amends when she's in good-humour. Come, tell me the lady's name, and Handy shall pay you.

Orange-Woman

41I must not, she forbid me.

Dorimant

42That's a sure sign she would have you.

Medley

43Where does she live?

Orange-Woman

44They lodge at my house.

Medley

45Nay, then she's in a hopeful way.

Orange-Woman

46Good Mr. Medley, say your pleasure of me, but take heed how you affront my house. God's my life, in a hopeful way!

Dorimant

47Prithee, peace! what kind of woman's the mother?

Orange-Woman

48A goodly grave gentlewoman. Lord! how she talks against the wild young men o' the town! As for your part, she thinks you an arrant devil; should she see you, on my conscience she would look if you had not a cloven foot.

Dorimant

49Does she know me?

Orange-Woman

50Only by hearsay; a thousand horrid stories have been told her of you, and she believes 'em all.

Medley

51By the character, this should be the famous Lady Woodvil and her daughter Harriet.

Orange-Woman

52The devil's in him for guessing, I think.

Dorimant

53Do you know 'em?

Medley

54Both very well; the mother's a great admirer of the forms and civility of the last age.

Dorimant

55An antiquated beauty may be allowed to be out of humour at the freedoms of the present. This is a good account of the mother; pray, what is the daughter?

Medley

56Why, first she's an heiress, vastly rich.

Dorimant

57And handsome?

Medley

58What alteration a twelvemonth may have bred in her I know not, but a year ago she was the beautifullest creature I ever saw; a fine, easy, clean shape; light brown hair in abundance; her features regular; her complexion clear and lively; large wanton eyes; but above all, a mouth that has made me kiss it a thousand times in imagination, teeth white and even, and pretty pouting lips, with a little moisture ever hanging on them, that look like the Provence rose fresh on the bush, ere the morning sun has quite drawn up the dew.

Dorimant

59Rapture, mere rapture!

Orange-Woman

60Nay, gad, he tells you true; she's a delicate creature.

Dorimant

61Has she wit?

Medley

62More than is usual in her sex, and as much malice. Then she's as wild as you would wish her, and has a demureness in her looks that makes it so surprising.

Dorimant

63Flesh and blood cannot hear this, and not long to know her.

Medley

64I wonder what makes her mother bring her up to town; an old doting keeper cannot be more jealous of his mistress.

Orange-Woman

65She made me laugh yesterday; there was a judge came to visit 'em, and the old man, she told me, did so stare upon her, and when he saluted her smacked so heartily; who would think it of 'em?

Medley

66God a mercy, a judge!

Dorimant

67Do 'em right, the gentlemen of the long robe have not been wanting by their good examples to countenance the crying sin o' the nation.

Medley

68Come, on with your trappings; 'tis later than you imagine.

Dorimant

69Call in the shoemaker, Handy.

Orange-Woman

70Good Mr. Dorimant, pay me; gad, I had rather give you my fruit than stay to be abused by that foul-mouthed rogue; what you gentlemen say, it matters not much, but such a dirty fellow does one more disgrace.

Dorimant

71Give her ten shillings, and be sure you tell the young gentlewoman I must be acquainted with her.

Orange-Woman

72Now do you long to be tempting this pretty creature. Well, heavens mend you!

Medley

73Farewell, Boga. [Exeunt ORANGE-WOMAN and HANDY.]
Dorimant, when did you see your pis-aller, as you call her Mrs. Loveit?

Dorimant

74Not these two days.

Medley

75And how stand affairs between you?

Dorimant

76There has been great patching of late, much ado; we make a shift to hang together.

Medley

77I wonder how her mighty spirit bears it.

Dorimant

78I'll enough, on all conscience; I never knew so violent a creature.

Medley

79She's the most passionate in her love, and the most extravagant in her jealousy, of any woman I ever heard of. What note is that?

Dorimant

80An excuse I am going to send her for the neglect I am guilty of.

Medley

81Prithee read it.

Dorimant

82No; but if you will take the pains you may.

Medley

83[reads] "I never was a lover of business, but now I have a just reason to hate it, since it has kept me these two days from seeing you. I intend to wait upon you in the afternoon, and in the pleasure of your conversation forget all I have suffered during this tedious absence." This business of yours, Dorimant, has been with a vizard at the playhouse; I have had an eye on you. If some malicious body should betray you, this kind note would hardly make your peace with her.

Dorimant

84I desire no better.

Medley

85Why, would her knowledge of it oblige you?

Dorimant

86Most infinitely; next to the coming to a good understanding with a new mistress, I love a quarrel with and old one; but the devil's in't, there has been such a calm in my affairs of late, I have not had the pleasure of making a woman so much as break her fan, to be sullen, or forswear herself these three days.

Medley

87A very great misfortune. Let me see, I love mischief well enough to forward this business myself; I'll about it presently and though I know the truth of what you've done will set her a-raving, I'll heighten it a little with invention, leave her in a fit o' the mother, and be here again before you're ready.

Dorimant

88Pray stay; you may spare yourself the labour; the business is undertaken already by one who will manage it with as much address, and I think with a little more malice than you can.

Medley

89Who i' the devil's name can this be?

Dorimant

90Why the vizard –that very vizard you saw me with.

Medley

91Does she love mischief so well as to betray herself to spite another?

Dorimant

92Not so neither, Medley. I will make you comprehend the mystery: this mask, for a farther confirmation of what I have been these two days swearing to her, made me yesterday at the playhouse make her a promise before her face utterly to break off with Loveit; and because she tenders my reputation, and would not have me do a barbarous thing, has contrived a way to give me a handsome occasion.

Medley

93Very good.

Dorimant

94She intends, about an hour before me, this afternoon to make Loveit a visit, and (having the privilege, by reason of a professed friendship between 'em) to talk of her concerns.

Medley

95Is she a friend?

Dorimant

96Oh, an intimate friend!

Medley

97Better and better; pray proceed.

Dorimant

98She means insensibly to insinuate a discourse of me, and artificially raise her jealousy to such a height, that transported with the first motions of her passion, she shall fly upon me with all the fury imaginable as soon as ever I enter; the quarrel being thus happily begun, I am to play my art, confess and justify all my roguery, swear her impertinence and ill-humour makes her intolerable, tax her with the next fop that comes into my head, and in a huff march away; slight her, and leave her to be taken by whosoever thinks it worth his time to lie down before her.

Medley

99This vizard is a spark, and has a genius that makes her worthy of yourself, Dorimant.

Enter HANDY, Shoemaker, and Footman.

Dorimant

100You rogue there, who sneak like a dog that has flung down a dish, if you do not mend your waiting I'll uncase you, and turn you loose to the wheel of fortune. Handy, seal this, and let him run with it presently.

[Exeunt HANDY and Footman.

Medley

101Since you're resolved on a quarrel, why do you send her this kind note?

Dorimant

102To keep her at home in order to the business.
[To the Shoemaker.] How now, you drunken sot?

Shoemaker

103'Zbud, you have no reason to talk; I have not had a bottle of sack of yours in my belly this fortnight.

Medley

104The orange-woman says your neighbours take notice what a heathen you are, and design to inform the bishop and have you burned for an atheist.

Shoemaker

105Damn her, dunghill! if her husband does not remove her, she stinks so the parish intend to indict him for a nuisance.

Medley

106I advise you like a friend, reform your life; you have brought the envy of the world upon you by living above yourself. Whoring and swearing are vices too genteel for a shoemaker.

Shoemaker

107'Zbud, I think you men of quality will grow as unreasonable as the women; you would engross the sins o' the nation; poor folks can no sooner be wicked, but they're railed at by their betters.

Dorimant

108Sirrah, I'll have you stand i' the pillory for this libel.

Shoemaker

109Some of you deserve it, I'm sure; there are so many of 'em, that our journeymen nowadays, instead of harmless ballads, sin nothing but your damned lampoons.

Dorimant

110Our lampoons, you rogue?

Shoemaker

111Nay, good master, why should not you write your own commentaries as well as Cæsar?

Medley

112The rascal's read, I perceive.

Shoemaker

113You know the old proverb –ale and history.

Dorimant

114Draw on my shoes, sirrah.

Shoemaker

115Here's a shoe.

Dorimant

116Sits with more wrinkles than there are in an angry bully's forehead.

Shoemaker

117'Zbud, as smooth as your mistress's skin does upon her; so strike your foot in home. 'Zbud, if e'er a monsieur of 'em all make more fashionable wear, I'll be content to have my ears whipped off with my own paring-knife.

Medley

118And served up in a ragout instead of coxcombs to a company of French shoemakers for a collation.

Shoemaker

119Hold, hold! damn 'em, caterpillars! let 'em feed upon cabbage. Come, master, your health this morning next my heart now.

Dorimant

120Go, get you home, and govern your family better; do not let your wife follow you to the alehouse, beat your whore, and lead you home in triumph.

Shoemaker

121'Zbud, there's never a man i' the town lives more like a gentleman with his wife than I do. I never mind her motions, she never inquires into mine; we speak to one another civilly, hate one another heartily, and because 'tis vulgar to lie and soak together, we have each of us our several settle-bed.

Dorimant

122Give him half-a-crown.

Medley

123Not without he will promise to be bloody drunk.

Shoemaker

124Tope's the word i' the eye of the world, for my master's honour, Robin.

Dorimant

125Do not debauch my servants, sirrah.

Shoemaker

126I only tip him the wink; he knows an alehouse from a hovel.

[Exit Shoemaker.

Dorimant

127My clothes, quickly.

Medley

128Where shall we dine to-day?

Enter BELLAIR.

Dorimant

129Where you will; here comes a good third man.

Bellair

130Your servant, gentlemen.

Medley

131Gentle sir, how will you answer this visit to your honourable mistress? 'Tis not her interest you should keep company with men of sense, who will be talking reason.

Bellair

132I do not fear her pardon, do you but grant me yours for my neglect of late.

Medley

133Though you've made us miserable by the want of your good company, to show you I am free from all resentment, may the beautiful cause of our misfortune give you all the joys happy lovers have shared ever since the world began.

Bellair

134You wish me in Heaven, but you believe me on my journey to Hell.

Medley

135You have a good strong faith, and that may contribute much towards your salvation. I confess I am but of an untoward constitution, apt to have doubts and scruples, and in love they are no less distracting than in religion; were I so near marriage, I should cry out by fits as I ride in my coach, Cuckold, Cuckold, with no less fury than the mad fanatic does Glory in Bethlem.

Bellair

136Because religion makes some run mad, must I live an atheist?

Medley

137Is it not great indiscretion for a man of credit, who may have money enough on his word, to go and deal with Jews who for little sums make men enter into bonds and give judgments?

Bellair

138Preach no more on this text, I am determined, and there is no hope of my conversion.

Dorimant

139[to HANDY, who is fiddling about him]. Leave, your unnecessary fiddling; a wasp that's buzzing about a man's nose at dinner is not more troublesome than thou art.

Handy

140You love to have your clothes hang just, sir.

Dorimant

141I love to be well dressed, sir; and think it no scandal to my understanding.

Handy

142Will you use the essence, or orange-flower water?

Dorimant

143I will smell as I do to-day, no offence to the ladies' noses.

Handy

144Your pleasure, sir.

Dorimant

145That a man's excellency should lie in neatly tying of a ribbon or a cravat! How careful's nature in furnishing the world with necessary coxcombs?

Bellair

146That's a mighty pretty suit of yours, Dorimant.

Dorimant

147I am glad't has your approbation.

Bellair

148No man in town has a better fancy in his clothes than you have.

Dorimant

149You will make me have an opinion of my genius.

Medley

150There is a great critic, I hear, in these matters lately arrived piping hot from Paris.

Bellair

151Sir Fopling Flutter, you mean.

Medley

152The same.

Bellair

153He thinks himself the pattern of modern gallantry.

Dorimant

154He is indeed the pattern of modern foppery.

Medley

155He was yesterday at the play, with a pair of gloves up to his elbows and a periwig more exactly curled than a lady's head newly dressed for a ball.

Bellair

156What a pretty lisp he has!

Dorimant

157Ho! that he affects in imitation of the people of quality of France.

Medley

158His head stands for the most part on one side, and his looks are more languishing than a lady's when she lolls at stretch in her coach or leans her head carelessly against the side of a box i' the playhouse.

Dorimant

159He is a person indeed of great acquired follies.

Medley

160He is like many others, beholding to his education for making him so eminent a coxcomb; many a fool had been lost to the world had their indulgent parents wisely bestowed neither learning nor good breeding on 'em.

Bellair

161He has been, as the sparkish word is, brisk upon the ladies already; he was yesterday at my Aunt Townley's, and gave Mrs. Loveit a catalogue of his good qualities under the character of a complete gentleman, who, according to Sir Fopling, ought to dress well, dance well, fence well, have a genius for love-letters, an agreeable voice for a chamber, be very amorous, something discreet, but not over-constant.

Medley

162Pretty ingredients to make an accomplished person.

Dorimant

163I am glad he pitched upon Loveit.

Bellair

164How so?

Dorimant

165I wanted a fop to lay to her charge, and this is as pat as may be.

Bellair

166I am confident she loves no man but you.

Dorimant

167The good fortune were enough to make me vain, but that I am in my nature modest.

Bellair

168Hark you, Dorimant; with your leave, Mr. Medley, 'tis only a secret concerning a fair lady.

Medley

169Your good breeding, sir, gives you too much trouble; you might have whispered without all this ceremony.

Bellair

170[to DORIMANT]. How stand your affairs with Belinda of late?

Dorimant

171She's a little jilting baggage.

Bellair

172Nay, I believe her false enough, but she's ne'er the worse for your purpose; she was with you yesterday in a disguise at the play.

Dorimant

173There we fell out, and resolved never to speak to one another more.

Bellair

174The occasion?

Dorimant

175Want of courage to meet me at the place appointed. These young women apprehend loving as much as the young men do fighting at first; but once entered, like them too, they all turn bullies straight.

Enter HANDY

Handy

176[to BELLAIR]. Sir, your man without desires to speak with you.

Bellair

177Gentlemen, I'll return immediately.

[Exit BELLAIR.

Medley

178A very pretty fellow this.

Dorimant

179He's handsome, well-bred, and by much the most tolerable of all the young men that do not abound in wit.

Medley

180Ever well-dressed, always complaisant, and seldom impertinent; you and he are grown very intimate, I see.

Dorimant

181It is our mutual interest to be so: it makes the women think the better of his understanding and judge more favourably of my reputation; it makes him pass upon some for a man of very good sense and I upon others for a very civil person.

Medley

182What was that whisper?

Dorimant

183A thing which he would fain have known, but I did not think it fit to tell him; it might have frighted him from his honourable intentions of marrying.

Medley

184Emilia, give her her due, has the best reputation of any young woman about the town who has beauty enough to provoke detraction; her carriage is unaffected, her discourse modest, not at all censorious nor pretending, like the counterfeits of the age.

Dorimant

185She's a discreet maid, and I believe nothing can corrupt her but a husband.

Medley

186A husband?

Dorimant

187Yes, a husband; I have known many women make a difficulty of losing a maidenhead who have afterwards made none of making a cuckold.

Medley

188This prudent consideration, I am apt to think, has made you confirm poor Bellair in the desperate resolution he has taken.

Dorimant

189Indeed, the little hope I found there was of her, in the state she was in, has made him by my advice contribute something towards the changing of her condition. Enter BELLAIR.
Dear Bellair, by heavens I thought we had lost thee; men in love are never to be reckoned on when we would form a company

Bellair

190Dorimant I am undone; my man has brought the most surprising news i' the world.

Dorimant

191Some strange misfortune is befallen your love.

Bellair

192My father came to town last night, and lodges i' the very house where Emilia lies.

Medley

193Does he know it is with her you are in love?

Bellair

194He knows I love, but knows not whom, without some officious sot has betrayed me.

Dorimant

195Your Aunt Townley is your confidante and favours the business.

Bellair

196I do not apprehend any ill office from her; I have received a letter, in which I am commanded by my father to meet him at my aunt's this afternoon; he tells me farther he has made a match for me, and bids me resolve to be obedient to his will or expect to be disinherited.

Medley

197Now's your time, Bellair; never had lover such an opportunity of giving a generous proof of his passion.

Bellair

198As how, I pray?

Medley

199Why, hang an estate, marry Emilia out of hand, and provoke your father to do what he threatens; 'tis but despising a coach, humbling yourself to a pair of goloshes, being out of countenance when you meet your friends, pointed at and pitied wherever you go by all the amorous fops that know you, and your fame will be immortal.

Bellair

200I could find in my heart to resolve not to marry at all.

Dorimant

201Fie, fie! that would spoil a good jest and disappoint the well-natured town of an occasion of laughing at you.

Bellair

202The storm I have so long expected hangs o'er my head and begins to pour down upon me; I am on the rack, and can have no rest till I'm satisfied in what I fear; where do you dine?

Dorimant

203At Long's or Locket's.

Medley

204At Long's let it be.

Bellair

205I'll run and see Emilia, and inform myself how matters stand; if my misfortunes are not so great as to make me unfit for company, I'll be with you.

[Exit BELLAIR.

Enter a Footman with a letter.

Footman

206[to DORIMANT]. Here's a letter, sir.

Dorimant

207The superscription's right: For Mr. Dorimant.

Medley

208Let's see: the very scrawl and spelling of a true-bred whore.

Dorimant

209I know the hand; the style is admirable, I assure you.

Medley

210Prithee read it.

Dorimant

211[reads]. "I told a you you dud not love me, if you dud, you would have seen me again e'er now; I have no money, and am very mallicolly; pray send me a guynie to see the operies. Your servant to command, Molly."

Medley

212Pray let the whore have a favourable answer, that she may spark it in a box and do honour to her profession.

Dorimant

213She shall, and perk up i' the face of quality. Is the coach at door?

Handy

214You did not bid me send for it.

Dorimant

215Eternal blockhead! [HANDY offers to go out.]
Hey, sot.

Handy

216Did you call me, sir?

Dorimant

217I hope you have not just exception to the name, sir?

Handy

218I have sense, sir.

Dorimant

219Not so much as a fly in winter. –How did you come, Medley?

Medley

220In a chair.

Footman

221You may have a hackney coach if you please, sir.

Dorimant

222I may ride the elephant if I please, sir; call another chair, and let my coach follow to Long's.

[Exeunt singing, Be calm, ye great parents, etc.
