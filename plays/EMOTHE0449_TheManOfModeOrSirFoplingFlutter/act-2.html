George Etherege

The Man of Mode, or Sir Fopling Flutter

← ACT I ACT III →

ACT II

SCENE I

Enter my Lady TOWNLEY and EMILIA.

Lady Townley

223I was afraid, Emilia, all had been discovered.

Emilia

224I tremble with the apprehension still.

Lady Townley

225That my brother should take lodgings i' the very house where you lie!

Emilia

226'Twas lucky we had timely notice to warn the people to be secret; he seems to be a mighty good-humoured old man.

Lady Townley

227He ever had a notable smirking way with him.

Emilia

228He calls me rogue, tells me he can't abide me, and does so bepat me.

Lady Townley

229On my word you are much in his favour then.

Emilia

230He has been very inquisitive, I am told, about my family, my reputation, and my fortune.

Lady Townley

231I am confident he does not i' the least suspect you are the woman his son's in love with.

Emilia

232What should make him then inform himself so particularly of me?

Lady Townley

233He was always of a very loving temper himself; it may be he has a doting fit upon him; who knows?

Emilia

234It cannot be.

Enter YOUNG BELLAIR.

Lady Townley

235Here comes my nephew. Where did you leave your father?

Young Bellair

236Writing a note within. Emilia, this early visit looks as if some kind jealousy would not let you rest at home.

Emilia

237The knowledge I have of my rival gives me a little cause to fear your constancy.

Young Bellair

238My constancy! I vow –

Emilia

239Do not vow –Our love is frail as is our life, and full as little in our power; and are you sure you shall outlive this day?

Young Bellair

240I am not; but when we are in perfect health 'twere an idle thing to fright ourselves with the thoughts of sudden death.

Lady Townley

241Pray what has passed between you and your father i' the garden?

Young Bellair

242He's firm in his resolution, tell me I must marry Mrs. Harriet, or swears he'll marry himself and disinherit me; when I saw I could not prevail with him to be more indulgent, I dissembled an obedience to his will which has composed his passion, and will give us time, and I hope opportunity, to deceive him.

Enter OLD BELLAIR with a note in his hand.

Lady Townley

243Peace, here he comes.

Old Bellair

244Harry, take this, and let your man carry it for me to Mr. Fourbes's chamber, my lawyer, i' the Temple. [Exit YOUNG BELLAIR.
[To EMILIA.] Neighbour, adod, I am glad to see thee here; make much of her, sister, she's one of the best of your acquaintance; I like her countenance and her behaviour well, she has a modesty that is not common i' this age, adod, she has.

Lady Townley

245I know her value, brother, and esteem her accordingly.

Old Bellair

246Advise her to wear a little more mirth in her face, adod, she's too serious.

Lady Townley

247The fault is very excusable in a young woman.

Old Bellair

248Nay, adod, I like her ne'er the worse, a melancholy beauty has her charms; I love a pretty sadness in a face which varies now and then, like changeable colours, into a smile.

Lady Townley

249Methinks you speak very feelingly, brother.

Old Bellair

250I am but five-and-fifty, sister, you know, an age not altogether insensible!
[To EMILIA.] Cheer up, sweetheart, I have a secret to tell thee may chance to make thee merry; we three will make collation together anon; i' the meantime mum, I can't abide you; go, I can't abide you. Enter YOUNG BELLAIR.
Harry, come, you must along with me to my Lady Woodvil's. I am going to slip the boy at a mistress.

Young Bellair

251At a wife, sir, you would say.

Old Bellair

252You need not look so glum, sir; a wife is no curse when she brings the blessings of a good estate with her; but an idle town flirt, with a painted face, a rotten reputation, and a crazy fortune, adod, is the devil and all; and such a one I hear you are in league with.

Young Bellair

253I cannot help detraction, sir.

Old Bellair

254Out, a pise o' their breeches, there are keeping fools enough for such flaunting baggages, and they are e'en too good for 'em.
[To EMILIA.] Remember night, go, you're a rogue you're a rogue; fare you well, fare you well; come, come, come along, sir.

[Exeunt OLD and YOUNG BELLAIR.

Lady Townley

255On my word the old man comes on apace; I'll lay my life he's smitten.

Emilia

256This is nothing but the pleasantness of his humour.

Lady Townley

257I know him better than you; let it work, it may prove lucky.

Enter a Page.

Page

258Madam, Mr. Medley has sent to know whether a visit will not be troublesome this afternoon?

Lady Townley

259[Exit Page. Send him word his visits never are so.

Emilia

260He's a very pleasant man.

Lady Townley

261He's a very necessary man among us women; he's not scandalous i' the least, perpetually contriving to bring good company together, and always ready to stop up a gap at ombre; then he knows all the little news o' the town.

Emilia

262I love to hear him talk o' the intrigues; let 'em be never so dull in themselves, he'll make 'em pleasant i' the relation.

Lady Townley

263But he improves things so much one can take no measure of the truth from him. Mr. Dorimant swears a flea or a maggot is not made more monstrous by a magnifying glass than a story is by his telling it.

Emilia

264Hold, here he comes.

Enter MEDLEY.

Lady Townley

265Mr. Medley.

Medley

266Your servant, madam.

Lady Townley

267You have made yourself a stranger of late.

Emilia

268I believe you took a surfeit of ombre last time you were here.

Medley

269Indeed I had my bellyful of that termagant Lady Dealer; there never was so insatiable a carder, an old gleeker never loved to sit to't like her; I have played with her now at least a dozen times till she's worn out all her fine complexion, and her tour would keep in curl no longer.

Lady Townley

270Blame her not, poor woman; she loves nothing so well as a black ace.

Medley

271The pleasure I have seen her in when she has had hope in drawing for a matadore!

Emilia

272'Tis as pretty sport to her as persuading masks off is to you to make discoveries.

Lady Townley

273Pray, where's your friend Mr. Dorimant?

Medley

274Soliciting his affairs; he's a man of great employment has more mistresses now depending than the most eminent lawyer in England has causes.

Emilia

275Here has been Mrs. Loveit, so uneasy and out of humour these two days.

Lady Townley

276How strangely love and jealousy rage in that poor woman!

Medley

277She could not have picked out a devil upon earth so proper to torment her; he has made her break a dozen or two of fans already, tear half a score points in pieces, and destroy hoods and knots without number.

Lady Townley

278We heard of a pleasant serenade he gave her t'other night.

Medley

279A Danish serenade, with kettledrums and trumpets.

Emilia

280Oh, barbarous!

Medley

281What, you are of the number of the ladies whose ears are grown so delicate since our operas, you can be charmed with nothing but flutes douces and French hautboys.

Emilia

282Leave your raillery, and tell us is there any new wit come forth, songs or novels?

Medley

283A very pretty piece of gallantry by an eminent author called The Diversions of Brussels; very necessary to be read by all old ladies who are desirous to improve themselves at questions and commands, blindman's buff, and the like fashionable recreations.

Emilia

284Oh, ridiculous!

Medley

285Then there is The Art of Affectation, written by a late beauty of quality, teaching you how to draw up your breasts, stretch up your neck, to thrust out your breech, to play with your head, to toss up your nose, to bite your lips, to turn up your eyes, to speak in a silly soft tone of a voice, and use all the foolish French words that will infallibly make your person and conversation charming, with a short apology at the latter end, in the behalf of young ladies who notoriously wash and paint, though they have naturally good complexions.

Emilia

286What a deal of stuff you tell us?

Medley

287Such as the town affords, madam. The Russians hearing the great respect we have for foreign dancing have lately sent over some of their best balladines, who are now practising a famous ballet, which will be suddenly danced at the Bear Garden.

Lady Townley

288Pray forbear your idle stories, and give us an account of the state of love as it now stands.

Medley

289Truly there has been some revolutions in those affairs, great chopping and changing among the old, and some new lovers, whom malice, indiscretion, and misfortune have luckily brought into play.

Lady Townley

290What think you of walking into the next room, and sitting down before you engage in this business?

Medley

291I wait upon you, and I hope (though women are commonly unreasonable) by the plenty of scandal I shall discover to give you very good content, ladies.

[Exeunt.

SCENE II

Enter Mrs. LOVEIT and PERT. Mrs. LOVEIT putting up a letter, then pulling out her pocket-glass, and looking in it.

Loveit

292Pert.

Pert

293Madam.

Loveit

294I hate myself. I look so ill to-day.

Pert

295Hate the wicked cause on't, that base man Mr. Dorimant, who makes you torment and vex yourself continually.

Loveit

296He is to blame, indeed.

Pert

297To blame to be two days without sending, writing, or coming near you, contrary to his oath and covenant! 'twas to much purpose to make him swear: I'll lay my life there's not an article but he has broken –talked to the vizards i' the pit; waited upon the ladies from the boxes to their coaches; gone behind the scenes and fawned upon those little insignificant creatures the players; 'tis impossible for a man of his inconstant temper to forbear, I'm sure.

Loveit

298I know he is a devil, but he has something of the angel yet undefaced in him, which makes him so charming and agreeable that I must love him be he never so wicked.

Pert

299I little thought, madam, to see your spirit tamed to this degree, who banished poor Mr. Lackwit but for taking up another lady's fan in your presence.

Loveit

300My knowing of such odious fools contributes to the making of me love Dorimant the better.

Pert

301Your knowing of Mr. Dorimant, in my mind, should rather make you hate all mankind.

Loveit

302So it does, besides himself.

Pert

303Pray, what excuse does he make in his letter?

Loveit

304He has had business.

Pert

305Business in general terms would not have been a current excuse for another; a modish man is always very busy when he is in pursuit of a new mistress.

Loveit

306Some fop has bribed you to rail at him; he had business, I will believe it, and will forgive him.

Pert

307You may forgive him anything, but I shall never forgive him his turning me into ridicule, as I hear he does.

Loveit

308I perceive you are of the number of those fools his wit has made his enemies.

Pert

309I am of the number of those he's pleased to rally, madam; and if we may believe Mr. Wagfan and Mr. Caperwell, he sometimes makes merry with yourself too among his laughing companions.

Loveit

310Blockheads are as malicious to witty men as ugly women are to the handsome; 'tis their interest, and they make it their business to defame 'em.

Pert

311I wish Mr. Dorimant would not make it his business to defame you.

Loveit

312Should he, I had rather be made infamous by him than owe my reputation to the dull discretion of those fops you talk of. Enter BELINDA.
Belinda!

[Running to her.

Belinda

313My dear.

Loveit

314You have been unkind of late.

Belinda

315Do not say unkind, say unhappy!

Loveit

316I could chide you; where have you been these two days?

Belinda

317Pity me rather, my dear, where I have been so tired with two or three country gentlewomen, whose conversation has been more insufferable than a country fiddle.

Loveit

318Are they relations?

Belinda

319No, Welsh acquaintance I made when I was last year at St. Winifred's; they have asked me a thousand questions of the modes and intrigues of the town, and I have told 'em almost as many things for news that hardly were so when their gowns were in fashion.

Loveit

320Provoking creatures, how could you endure 'em?

Belinda

321[aside] Now to carry on my plot; nothing but love could make me capable of so much falsehood; 'tis time to begin, lest Dorimant should come before her jealousy has stung her.
[Laughs, and then speaks on.] I was yesterday at a play with 'em, where I was fain to show 'em the living, as the man at Westminster does the dead; that is Mrs. Such-a-one, admired for her beauty; this is Mr. Such-a-one, cried up for a wit; that is sparkish Mr. Such-a-one, who keeps reverend Mrs. Such-a-one, and there sits fine Mrs. Such-a-one, who was lately cast off by my Lord Such-a-one.

Loveit

322Did you see Dorimant there?

Belinda

323I did, and imagine you were there with him and have no mind to own it.

Loveit

324What should make you think so?

Belinda

325A lady masked in a pretty déshabillé, whom Dorimant entertained with more respect than the gallants do a common vizard.

Loveit

326[aside.] Dorimant at the play entertaining a mask, of heavens!

Belinda

327[aside.] Good.

Loveit

328Did he stay all the while?

Belinda

329Till the play was done, and then led her out, which confirms me it was you.

Loveit

330Traitor!

Pert

331Now you may believe he had business, and you may forgive him too.

Loveit

332Ungrateful, perjured man!

Belinda

333You seem so much concerned, my dear, I fear I have told you unawares what I had better have concealed for your quiet.

Loveit

334What manner of shape had she?

Belinda

335Tall and slender, her motions were very genteel; certainly she must be some person of condition.

Loveit

336Shame and confusion be ever in her face when she shows it!

Belinda

337I should blame your discretion for loving that wild man, my dear; but they say he has a way so bewitching that few can defend their hearts who know him.

Loveit

338I will tear him from mine, or die i' the attempt.

Belinda

339Be more moderate.

Loveit

340Would I had daggers, darts, or poisoned arrows in my breast, so I could but remove the thoughts of him from thence!

Belinda

341Fie, fie! your transports are too violent, my dear. This may be but an accidental gallantry, and 'tis likely ended at her coach.

Pert

342Should it proceed farther, let your comfort be, the conduct Mr. Dorimant affects will quickly make you know your rival, ten to one let you see her ruined, her reputation exposed to the town; a happiness none will envy her but yourself, madam.

Loveit

343Whoe'er she be, all the harm I wish her is, may she love him as well as I do, and may he give her as much cause to hate him!

Pert

344Never doubt the latter end of your curse, madam.

Loveit

345May all the passions that are raised by neglected love, jealousy, indignation, spite, and thirst of revenge, eternally rage in her soul as they do now in mine!

[Walks up and down with a distracted air.

Enter a Page.

Page

346Madam, Mr. Dorimant.

Loveit

347I will not see him.

Page

348I told him you were within, madam.

Loveit

349Say you lied, say I'm busy, shut the door; say anything.

Page

350He's here, madam.

Enter DORIMANT.

Dorimant

351They taste of death who do at Heaven arrive,
But we this paradise approach alive.

[To LOVEIT.] What, dancing the galloping nag without a fiddle? [Offers to catch her by the hand; she flings away and walks on.
I fear this restlessness of the body, madam.
[Pursuing her.] proceeds from an unquietness of the mind. What unlucky accident puts you out of humour; a point ill washed, knots spoiled i' the making up, hair shaded awry, or some other little mistake in setting you in order?

Pert

352A trifle, I my opinion, sir, more inconsiderable than any you mention.

Dorimant

353Oh, Mrs. Pert, I never knew you sullen enough to be silent; come, let me know the business.

Pert

354The business, sir, is the business that has taken you up these two days; how have I seen you laugh at men of business, and now to become a man of business yourself!

Dorimant

355We are not masters of our own affections, our inclinations daily alter; now we love pleasure, and anon we shall dote on business: human frailty will have it so, and who can help it?

Loveit

356Faithless, inhuman, barbarous man! –

Dorimant

357Good, now the alarm strikes. –

Loveit

358Without sense of love, of honour, or of gratitude, tell me –for I will know– what devil, masked she was, you were with at the play yesterday?

Dorimant

359Faith, I resolved as much as you, but the devil was obstinate and would not tell me.

Loveit

360False in this as in your vows to me! you do know.

Dorimant

361The truth is, I did all I could to know.

Loveit

362And dare you own it to my face? Hell and furies!

[Tears her fan in pieces.

Dorimant

363Spare your fan, madam; you are growing hot, and will want it to cool you.

Loveit

364Horror and distraction seize you, sorrow and remorse gnaw your soul, and punish all your perjuries to me! –

[Weeps.

Dorimant

365So thunder breaks the cloud in twain,
And makes a passage for the rain.

[Turning to BELINDA. Belinda, you are the devil that have raised this storm; you were at the play yesterday, and have been making discoveries to your dear.

Belinda

366You're the most mistaken man i' the world.

Dorimant

367It must be so, and here I vow revenge; resolve to pursue and persecute you more impertinently than ever any loving fop did his mistress, hunt you i' the Park, trace you i' the Mall, dog you in every visit you make, haunt you at the plays and i' the drawing room, hang my nose in your neck, and talk to you whether you will or no, and ever look upon you with such dying eyes, till your friends grow jealous of me, send you out of town, and make the world suspect your reputation.
[In a lower voice. At my Lady Townley's when we go from hence.

[He looks kindly on BELINDA.

Belinda

368I'll meet you there.

Dorimant

369Enough.

Loveit

370Stand off, you sha' not stare upon her so.

[Pushing DORIMANT away.

Dorimant

371Good! There's one made jealous already.

Loveit

372Is this the constancy you vowed?

Dorimant

373Constancy at my years! 'tis not a virtue in season; you might as well expect the fruit the autumn ripens i' the spring.

Loveit

374Monstrous principle!

Dorimant

375Youth has a long journey to go, madam: should I have set up my rest at the first inn I lodged at, I should never have arrived at the happiness I now enjoy.

Loveit

376Dissembler, damned dissembler!

Dorimant

377I am so, I confess; good nature and good manners corrupt me. I am honest in my inclinations, and would not, were't not to avoid offence, make a lady a little in years believe I think her young, wilfully mistake art for nature, and seem as fond of a thing I am weary of as when I doted on't in earnest.

Loveit

378False man!

Dorimant

379True woman!

Loveit

380Now you begin to show yourself!

Dorimant

381Love gilds us over and makes us show fine things to one another for a time, but soon the gold wears off, and then again the native brass appears.

Loveit

382Think on your oaths, your vows and protestations, perjured man.

Dorimant

383I made 'em when I was in love.

Loveit

384And therefore ought they not to bind? Oh, impious!

Dorimant

385What we swear at such a time may be a certain proof of a present passion; but to say truth, in love there is no security to be given for the future.

Loveit

386Horrid and ungrateful, begone, and never see me more.

Dorimant

387I am not one of those troublesome coxcombs, who because they were once well received take the privilege to plague a woman with their love ever after; I shall obey you, madam, though I do myself some violence.

[He offers to go, and LOVEIT pulls him back.

Loveit

388Come back, you sha' not go. Could you have the ill-nature to offer it?

Dorimant

389When love grows diseased, the best thing we can do is to put it to a violent death; I cannot endure the torture of a lingering and consumptive passion.

Loveit

390Can you think mine sickly?

Dorimant

391Oh, 'tis desperately ill! What worse symptoms are there than your being always uneasy when I visit you, your picking quarrels with me on slight occasions, and in my absence kindly listening to the impertinences of every fashionable fool that talks to you?

Loveit

392What fashionable fool can you lay to my charge?

Dorimant

393Why, the every cock-fool of all those fools, Sir Fopling Flutter.

Loveit

394I never saw him in my life but once.

Dorimant

395The worse woman you, at first sight to put on all your charms, to entertain him with that softness in your voice and all that wanton kindness in your eyes you so notoriously affect when you design a conquest.

Loveit

396So damned a lie did never malice yet invent. Who told you this?

Dorimant

397No matter; that ever I should love a woman that can dote on a senseless caper, a tawdry French ribbon, and a formal cravat.

Loveit

398You make me mad.

Dorimant

399A guilty conscience may do much; go on, be the game-mistress o' the town, and enter all our young fops as fast as they come from travel.

Loveit

400Base and scurrilous!

Dorimant

401A fine mortifying reputation 'twill be for a woman of your pride, wit, and quality!

Loveit

402This jealousy's a mere pretence, a cursed trick of your own devising; I know you.

Dorimant

403Believe it, and all the ill of me you can: I would not have a woman have the least good thought of me that can think well of Fopling; farewell; fall to, and much good may [it] do you with your coxcomb.

Loveit

404Stay, oh! stay, and I will tell you all.

Dorimant

405I have been told too much already.

[Exit DORIMANT.

Loveit

406Call him again.

Pert

407E'en let him go, a fair riddance.

Loveit

408Run, I say; call him again. I will have him called.

Pert

409The devil should carry him away first, were it my concern.

[Exit PERT.

Belinda

410He's frightened me from the very thoughts of loving men; for heaven's sake, my dear, do not discover what I told you; I dread his tongue as much as you ought to have done his friendship.

Enter PERT.

Pert

411He's gone, madam.

Loveit

412Lightning blast him!

Pert

413When I told him you desired him to come back, he smiled, made a mouth at me, flung into his coach, and said –

Loveit

414What did he say?

Pert

415"Drive away"; and then repeated verses.

Loveit

416Would I had made a contract to be a witch, when first I entertained this greater devil, monster, barbarian; I could tear myself in pieces. Revenge, nothing but revenge can ease me: plague, war, famine, fire, all that can bring universal ruin and misery on mankind; with joy I'd perish to have you in my power but this moment

[Exit LOVEIT.

Pert

417Follow, madam; leave her not in this outrageous passion.

[PERT gathers up the things.

Belinda

418He's given me the proof which I desired of his love:
But 'tis a proof of his ill-nature too;
I wish I had not seen him use her so.
I sigh to think that Dorimant may be
One day as faithless and unkind to me.

[Exeunt.
