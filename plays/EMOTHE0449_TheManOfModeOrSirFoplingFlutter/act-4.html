ACT IV

SCENE I. –

The scene opens with the fiddles playing a country dance.

Enter DORIMANT, Lady WOODVIL, YOUNG BELLAIR, and Mrs. HARRIET, OLD BELLAIR, and EMILIA, Mr. MEDLEY and Lady TOWNLEY, as having just ended the dance.

Old Bellair

869So, so, so, a smart bout, a very smart bout, adod!

Lady Townley

870How do you like Emilia's dancing, brother?

Old Bellair

871Not at all, not at all.

Lady Townley

872You speak not what you think, I am sure.

Old Bellair

873No matter for that; go, bid her dance no more, it don't become her, it don't become her, tell her I say so.
[Aside.] Adod, I love her.

Dorimant

874[to LADY WOODVIL] All people mingle nowadays, madam, and in public places women of quality have the least respect showed 'em.

Lady Woodvil

875I protest you say the truth, Mr. Courtage.

Dorimant

876Forms and ceremonies, the only things that uphold quality and greatness, are now shamefully laid aside and neglected.

Lady Woodvil

877Well! this is not the women's age, let 'em think what they will; lewdness is the business now, love was the business in my time.

Dorimant

878The women indeed are little beholding to the young men of this age; they're generally only dull admirers of themselves, and make their court to nothing but their periwigs and their cravats, and would be more concerned for the disordering of 'em, though on a good occasion, than a young maid would be for the tumbling of her head or handkercher.

Lady Woodvil

879I protest you hit 'em.

Dorimant

880They are very assiduous to show themselves at Court well dressed to the women of quality, but their business is with the stale mistresses of the town, who are prepared to receive their lazy addresses by industrious old lovers who have cast 'em off and made 'em easy.

Harriet

881He fits my mother's humour so well, a little more and she'll dance a kissing dance with him anon.

Medley

882Dutifully observed, madam.

Dorimant

883They pretend to be great critics in beauty; by their talk you would think they liked no face, and yet can dote on an ill one if it belong to a laundress or a tailor' daughter; they cry a woman's past her prime at twenty, decayed at four-and-twenty, old and unsufferable at thirty.

Lady Woodvil

884Unsufferable at thirty! That they are in the wrong, Mr. Courtage, at five-and-thirty there are living proofs enough to convince 'em.

Dorimant

885Ay, madam, there's Mrs. Setlooks, Mrs. Droplip, and my Lady Loud; show me among all our opening buds a face that promises so much beauty as the remains of theirs.

Lady Woodvil

886The depraved appetite of this vicious age tastes nothing but green fruit, and loathes it when 'tis kindly ripened.

Dorimant

887Else so many deserving women, madam, would not be so untimely neglected.

Lady Woodvil

888I protest, Mr. Courtage, a dozen such good men as you would be enough to atone for that wicked Dorimant and all the under-debauchees of the town. [HARRIET, EMILIA, YOUNG BELLAIR, MEDLEY, and Lady TOWNLEY break out into a laughter.
What's the matter there?

Medley

889A pleasant mistake, madam, that a lady has made, occasions a little laughter.

Old Bellair

890Come, come, you keep 'em idle, they are impatient till the fiddles play again.

Dorimant

891You are not weary, madam?

Lady Woodvil

892One dance more; I cannot refuse you, Mr. Courtage.

[They dance.

Emilia

893You are very active, sir.

[After the dance OLD BELLAIR singing and dancing up to EMILIA.

Old Bellair

894Adod, sirrah, when I was a young fellow I could ha' capered up to my woman's gorget.

Dorimant

895You are willing to rest yourself, madam?

Lady Townley

896We'll walk into my chamber and sit down.

Medley

897Leave us Mr. Courtage, he's a dancer, and the young ladies are not weary yet.

Lady Woodvil

898We'll send him out again.

Harriet

899If you do not quickly, I know where to send for Mr. Dorimant.

Lady Woodvil

900This girl's head, Mr. Courtage, is ever running on that wild fellow.

Dorimant

901'Tis well you have got her a good husband, madam; that will settle it.

[Exeunt Lady TOWNLEY, Lady WOODVIL, and DORIMANT.

Old Bellair

902[to EMILIA] Adod, sweetheart, be advised, and do not throw thyself away on a young idle fellow.

Emilia

903I have no such intention, sir.

Old Bellair

904Have a little patience, thou shalt have the man I spake of. Adod, he loves thee, and will make a good husband; but no words.

Emilia

905But, sir. –

Old Bellair

906No answer –out a pise! peace! and think on't.

Enter DORIMANT.

Dorimant

907Your company is desired within, sir.

Old Bellair

908I go, I go, good Mr. Courtage – [To EMILIA.] Fare you well; go, I'll see you no more.

Emilia

909What have I done, sir?

Old Bellair

910You are ugly, you are ugly; is she not, Mr. Courtage?

Emilia

911Better words, or I shan't abide you.

Old Bellair

912Out a pise –adod, what does she say? Hit her a pat for me there.

[Exit OLD BELLAIR.

Medley

913You have charms for the whole family.

Dorimant

914You'll spoil all with some unseasonable jest, Medley.

Medley

915You see I confine my tongue and am content to be a bare spectator, much contrary to my nature.

Emilia

916Methinks, Mr. Dorimant, my Lady Woodvil is a little fond of you.

Dorimant

917Would her daughter were!

Medley

918It may be you may find her so; try her, you have an opportunity.

Dorimant

919And I will not lose it. Bellair, here's a lady has something to say to you.

Young Bellair

920I wait upon her. Mr. Medley, we have both business with you.

Dorimant

921Get you all together then. [To HARRIET.] That demure curtsey is not amiss in jest, but do not think in earnest it becomes you.

Harriet

922Affectation is catching, I find; from your grave bow I got it.

Dorimant

923Where had you all that scorn and coldness in your look?

Harriet

924From nature, sir; pardon my want of art: I have not learnt those softness and languishing which now in faces are so much in fashion.

Dorimant

925You need 'em not; you have a sweetness of your own, if you would but calm your frowns and let it settle.

Harriet

926My eyes are wild and wandering like my passions, and cannot yet be tied to rules of charming.

Dorimant

927Women, indeed, have commonly a method of managing those messengers of love; now they will look as if they would kill, and anon they will look as if they were dying. They point and rebate their glances the better to invite us.

Harriet

928I like this variety well enough, but hate the set face that always looks as it would say, Come, love me –a woman who at plays makes the doux yeux to a whole audience and at home cannot forbear 'em to her monkey.

Dorimant

929Put on a gentle smile, and let me see how well it will become you.

Harriet

930I am sorry my face does not please you as it is, but I shall not be complaisant and change it.

Dorimant

931Though you are obstinate, I know 'tis capable of improvement, and shall do you justice, madam, if I chance to be at Court when the critics of the circle pass their judgment; for thither you must come.

Harriet

932And expect to be taken in pieces, have all my features examined, every motion censured, and on the whole be condemned to be put pretty, or a beauty of the lowest rate. What think you?

Dorimant

933The women, nay, the very lovers who belong to the drawing-room, will maliciously allow you more than that; they always grant what is apparent that they may the better be believed when they name concealed faults they cannot easily be disproved in.

Harriet

934Beauty runs as great a risk exposed at Court as wit does on the stage, where the ugly and the foolish all are free to censure.

Dorimant

935[aside] I love her, and dare not let her know it; I fear she has an ascendant o'er me, and may revenge the wrongs I have done her sex. [To her.] Think of making a party, madam, love will engage.

Harriet

936You make me start! I did not think to have heard of love from you.

Dorimant

937I never knew what 'twas to have a settled ague yet, but now and then have had irregular fits.

Harriet

938Take heed! sickness after long health is commonly more violent and dangerous.

Dorimant

939[aside] I have took the infection from her, and feel the disease now spreading in me –
[To her.] Is the name of love so frightful that you dare not stand it?

Harriet

940'Twill do little execution out of your mouth on me, I am sure.

Dorimant

941It has been fatal –

Harriet

942To some easy women, but we are not all born to one destiny; I was informed you use to laugh at love and not make it.

Dorimant

943The time has been, but now I must speak –

Harriet

944If it be on that idle subject, I will put on my serious look, turn my head carelessly from you, drop my lip, let my eyelids fall and hang half o'er my eyes –thus– while you buzz a speech of an hour long in my ear, and I answer never a word; why do you not begin?

Dorimant

945That the company may take notice how passionately I make advances of love, and how disdainfully you receive 'em.

Harriet

946When your love's grown strong enough to make you bear being laughed at, I'll give you leave to trouble me with it: till when, pray forbear, sir.

Enter Sir FOPLING and others in masks.

Dorimant

947What's here, masquerades?

Harriet

948I thought that foppery had been left off and people might have been in private with a fiddle.

Dorimant

949'Tis endeavoured to be kept on foot still by some who find themselves the more acceptable the less they are known.

Young Bellair

950This must be Sir Fopling.

Medley

951That extraordinary habit shows it.

Young Bellair

952What are the rest?

Medley

953A company of French rascals whom he picked up in Paris and has brought over to be his dancing equipage on these occasions. Make him own himself; a fool is very troublesome when he presumes he is incognito.

Sir Fopling

954[to HARRIET] Do you know me?

Harriet

955Ten to one but I guess at you.

Sir Fopling

956Are you women as fond of a vizard as we men are?

Harriet

957I am very fond of a vizard that covers a face I do not like, sir.

Young Bellair

958Here are no masks, you see, sir, but those which came with you; this was intended a private meeting, but because you look like a gentleman, if you will discover yourself, and we know you to be such, you shall be welcome.

Sir Fopling

959[pulling off his mask] Dear Bellair.

Medley

960Sir Fopling! how came you hither?

Sir Fopling

961Faith, as I was coming late from Whitehall, after the King's couchée, one of my people told me he had heard fiddles at my Lady Townley's, and –

Dorimant

962You need not say any more, sir.

Sir Fopling

963Dorimant, let me kiss thee.

Dorimant

964[whispers] Hark you, Sir Fopling.

Sir Fopling

965Enough, enough –Courtage. A pretty kind of young woman that, Medley; I observed her in the Mall; more éveillée than our English women commonly are; prithee, what is she?

Medley

966The most noted coquette in town; beware of her.

Sir Fopling

967Let her be what she will, I know how to take my measures; in Paris the mode is to flatter the prude, laugh at the faux-prude, make serious love to the demi-prude, and only rally with the coquette. Medley, what think you?

Medley

968That for all this smattering of the mathematics, you may be out in your judgment at tennis.

Sir Fopling

969What a coq-à-l'âne is this? I talk of women, and thou answer'st tennis.

Medley

970Mistakes will be for want of apprehension.

Sir Fopling

971I am very glad of the acquaintance I have with this family.

Medley

972My lady truly is a good woman.

Sir Fopling

973Ah! Dorimant –Courtage I would say –would thou hadst spent the last winter in Paris with me. When thou wert there La Corneus and Sallyes were the only habitudes we had; a comedian would have been a bonne fortune. No stranger ever passed his time so well as I did some months before I came over. I was well received in a dozen families where all the women of quality used to visit; I have intrigues to tell thee more pleasant than ever thou read'st in a novel.

Harriet

974Write 'em, sir, and oblige us women; our language wants such little stories.

Sir Fopling

975Writing, madam, 's a mechanic part of wit; a gentleman should never go beyond a song or a billet.

Harriet

976Bussy was a gentleman.

Sir Fopling

977Who, d'Ambois?

Medley

978Was there ever such a brisk blockhead?

Harriet

979Not d'Ambois, sir, but Rabutin –he who writ The Loves of France.

Sir Fopling

980That may be madam: many gentlemen do things that are below 'em. Damn your authors, Courtage; women are the prettiest things we can fool away our time with.

Harriet

981I hope ye have wearied yourself to-nigh at Court sir, and will not think of fooling with anybody here.

Sir Fopling

982I cannot complain of my fortune there, madam – Dorimant –

Dorimant

983Again!

Sir Fopling

984Courtage, a pox on't! I have something to tell thee. When I had made my court within, I came out and flung myself upon the mat under the state i' th' outward room i' th' midst of half a dozen beauties who were withdrawn to jeer among themselves, as they called it.

Dorimant

985Did you know 'em?

Sir Fopling

986Not one of 'em by heavens! not I. But they were all your friends.

Dorimant

987How are you sure of that?

Sir Fopling

988Why we laughed at all the town; spared nobody but yourself; they found me a man for their purpose.

Dorimant

989I know you are malicious to your power.

Sir Fopling

990And faith I had occasion to show it for I never saw more gaping fools at a ball or on a Birthday.

Dorimant

991You learned who the women were?

Sir Fopling

992No matter; they frequent the drawing-room.

Dorimant

993And entertain themselves pleasantly at the expense of all the fops who come there.

Sir Fopling

994That's their business; faith, I sifted 'em, and find they have a sort of wit among them –Ah! filthy.

[Pinches a tallow candle.

Dorimant

995Look, he has been pinching the tallow candle.

Sir Fopling

996How can you breathe in a room where there's grease frying? Dorimant, thou art intimate with my lady, advise her for her own sake, and the good company that comes hither, to burn wax lights.

Harriet

997What are these masquerades who stand so obsequiously at a distance?

Sir Fopling

998A set of balladins whom I picked out of the best in France, and brought over with a flutes douces or two, my servants; they shall entertain you.

Harriet

999I had rather see you dance yourself, Sir Fopling.

Sir Fopling

1000And I had rather do it –all the company knows it –but, madam –

Medley

1001Come, come, no excuses, Sir Fopling.

Sir Fopling

1002By heavens, Medley!

Medley

1003Like a woman, I find you must be struggled with before one brings you to what you desire.

Harriet

1004[aside] Can he dance?

Emilia

1005And fence and sing too, if you'll believe him.

Dorimant

1006He has no more excellence in his heels than in his head. He went to Paris a plain bashful English blockhead, and is returned a fine undertaking French fop.

Medley

1007I cannot prevail.

Sir Fopling

1008Do not think it want of complaisance, madam.

Harriet

1009You are too well bred to want that, Sir Fopling. I believe it want of power.

Sir Fopling

1010By heavens! and so it is. I have sat up so damned late and drunk so cursed hard since I came to this lewd town, that I am fit for nothing but low dancing now, a corant, bourée, or a menuet; but St. André tells me, if I will but be regular, in one month I shall rise again. Pox on this debauchery!

[Endeavours at a caper.

Emilia

1011I have heard your dancing much commended.

Sir Fopling

1012It had the good fortune to please in Paris. I was judged to rise within an inch as high as the basque, in an entry I danced there.

Harriet

1013I am mightily taken with this fool; let us sit. Here's a seat, Sir Fopling.

Sir Fopling

1014At your feet, madam; I can be nowhere so much at ease: by your leave, gown.

Harriet and Emilia

1015Ah! you'll spoil it.

Sir Fopling

1016No matter, my clothes are my creatures; I make 'em to make my court to you ladies. Hey, – [TROTT dances.
qu'on commence! To an English dancer English motions. I was forced to entertain this fellow, one of my set miscarrying – Oh, horrid! leave your damned manner of dancing, and put on the French air; have you not a pattern before you –pretty well! Imitation in time may bring him to something.

After the dance enter OLD BELLAIR, Lady WOODVIL, and Lady TOWNLEY.

Old Bellair

1017Hey, adod! what have we here, a mumming?

Lady Woodvil

1018Where's my daughter –Harriet?

Dorimant

1019Here, here, madam. I know not but under these disguises there may be dangerous sparks; I gave the young lady warning.

Lady Woodvil

1020Lord! I am so obliged to you, Mr. Courtage.

Harriet

1021Lord! how you admire this man.

Lady Woodvil

1022What have you to except against him?

Harriet

1023He's a fop.

Lady Woodvil

1024He's not a Dorimant, a wild extravagant fellow of the times.

Harriet

1025He's a man made up of forms and commonplaces sucked out of the remaining lees of the last age.

Lady Woodvil

1026He's so good a man, that were you not engaged –

Lady Townley

1027You'll have but little night to sleep in.

Lady Woodvil

1028Lord! 'tis perfect day –

Dorimant

1029[aside] The hour is almost come I appointed Belinda, and I am not so foppishly in love here to forget: I am flesh and blood yet.

Lady Townley

1030I am very sensible, madam.

Lady Woodvil

1031Lord, madam!

Harriet

1032Look, in what a struggle is my poor mother yonder?

Young Bellair

1033She has much ado to bring out the compliment.

Dorimant

1034She strains hard for it.

Harriet

1035See, see! her head tottering, her eyes staring, and her under lip trembling.

Dorimant

1036[aside] Now, now she's in the very convulsions of her civility. 'Sdeath, I shall lose Belinda. I must fright her hence; she'll be an hour in this fit of good manners else.
[To Lady WOODVIL] Do you not know Sir Fopling, madam?

Lady Woodvil

1037I have seen that face. Oh, Heav'n! 'tis the same we met in the Mall; how came he here?

Dorimant

1038A fiddle in this town is a kind of fop-call; no sooner it strikes up but the house is besieged with an army of masquerades straight.

Lady Woodvil

1039Lord! I tremble, Mr. Courtage; for certain Dorimant is in the company.

Dorimant

1040I cannot confidently say he is not; you had best begone. I will wait upon you; your daughter is in the hands of Mr. Bellair.

Lady Woodvil

1041I'll see her before me. Harriet, come away.

Young Bellair

1042Lights! lights!

Lady Townley

1043Light down there.

Old Bellair

1044Adod, it needs not –

Dorimant

1045Call my Lady Woodvil's coach to the door quickly.

[Exeunt DORIMANT and YOUNG BELLAIR, with the Ladies.

Old Bellair

1046Stay, Mr. Medley, let the young fellows do that duty; we will drink a glass of wine together. 'Tis good after dancing; what mumming spark is that?

Medley

1047He is not to be comprehended in few words.

Sir Fopling

1048Hey! La Tour.

Medley

1049Whither away, Sir Fopling?

Sir Fopling

1050I have business with Courtage –

Medley

1051He'll but put the ladies into their coach, and come up again.

Old Bellair

1052In the meantime I'll call for a bottle.

[Exit OLD BELLAIR.

Enter YOUNG BELLAIR.

Medley

1053Where's Dorimant?

Young Bellair

1054Stolen home; he has had business waiting for him there all this night, I believe, by an impatience I observed in him.

Medley

1055Very likely; 'tis but dissembling drunkenness, railing at his friends, and the kind soul will embrace the blessing and forget the tedious expectation.

Sir Fopling

1056I must speak with him before I sleep.

Young Bellair

1057Emilia and I are resolved on that business.

Medley

1058Peace, here's your father.

Enter OLD BELLAIR and Butler, with a bottle of wine.

Old Bellair

1059The women are all gone to bed. Fill, boy; Mr. Medley, begin a health.

Medley

1060[whispers] To Emilia.

Old Bellair

1061Out, a pise! she's a rogue, and I'll not pledge you.

Medley

1062I know you will.

Old Bellair

1063Adod, drink it then.

Sir Fopling

1064Let us have the new bachique.

Old Bellair

1065Adod, that is a hard word; what does it mean, sir'

Medley

1066A catch or drinking song.

Old Bellair

1067Let us have it then.

Sir Fopling

1068Fill the glasses round, and draw up in a body. Hey! music!

[All]

1069(They sing) The pleasures of love and the joys of good wine
To perfect our happiness wisely we join.
We to beauty all day
Give the sovereign sway,
And her favourite nymphs devoutly obey.
At the plays we are constantly making our court,
And when they are ended we follow the sport,
To the Mall and the Park,
Where we love till 'tis dark;
Then sparkling champagne
Puts an end to their reign;
It quickly recovers
Poor languishing lovers,
Makes us frolic and gay, and drowns all our sorrow;
But, alas! we relapse again on the morrow.
Let ev'ry man stand
With his glass in his hand,
And briskly discharge at the word of command.
Here's a health to all those
Whom to-night we depose:
Wine and beauty by turns great souls should inspire.
Present altogether, and now, boys, give fire!

Old Bellair

1070Adod, a pretty business, and very merry.

Sir Fopling

1071Hark you, Medley, let you and I take the fiddles, and go waken Dorimant.

Medley

1072We shall do him a courtesy, if it be as I guess. For after the fatigue of this night, he'll quickly have his bellyful, and be glad of an occasion to cry: Take away, Handy.

Young Bellair

1073I'll go with you, and there we'll consult about affairs, Medley.

Old Bellair

1074[looks at his watch] Adod, 'tis six o'clock.

Sir Fopling

1075Let's away then.

Old Bellair

1076Mr. Medley, my sister tells me you are an honest man, and, adod, I love you. Few words and hearty –that's the way with old Harry, old Harry.

Sir Fopling

1077Light your flambeaux. Hey!

Old Bellair

1078What does the man mean?

Medley

1079'Tis day, Sir Fopling.

Sir Fopling

1080No matter. Our serenade will look the greater.

[Exeunt omnes.

SCENE II. –

DORIMANT'S Lodging. A table, a candle, a toilett, etc.

HANDY tying up linen

Enter DORIMANT in his gown, and BELINDA.

Dorimant

1081Why will you be gone so soon?

Belinda

1082Why did you stay out so late?

Dorimant

1083Call a chair, Handy. [Exit HANDY.
What makes you tremble so?

Belinda

1084I have a thousand fears about me. Have I not been seen, think you?

Dorimant

1085By nobody but myself and trusty Handy.

Belinda

1086Where are all your people?

Dorimant

1087I have dispersed 'em on sleeveless errands. What does that sigh mean?

Belinda

1088Can you be so unkind to ask me? –Well– [Sighs.] were it to do again –

Dorimant

1089We should do it, should we not?

Belinda

1090I think we should; the wickeder man you to make me love so well. Will you be discreet now?

Dorimant

1091I will.

Belinda

1092You cannot.

Dorimant

1093Never doubt it.

Belinda

1094I will not expect it.

Dorimant

1095You do me wrong.

Belinda

1096You have no more power to keep the secret than I had not to trust you with it.

Dorimant

1097By all the joys I have had, and those you keep in store –

Belinda

1098You'll do for my sake what you never did before –

Dorimant

1099By that truth thou hast spoken, a wife shall sooner betray herself to her husband –

Belinda

1100Yet I had rather you should be false in this, than in another thing you promised me.

Dorimant

1101What's that?

Belinda

1102That you would never see Loveit more but in public places, in the Park, at Court, and plays.

Dorimant

1103'Tis not likely a man should be fond of seeing a damned old play when there is a new one acted.

Belinda

1104I dare not trust your promise.

Dorimant

1105You may.

Belinda

1106This does not satisfy me. You shall swear you never will see her more.

Dorimant

1107I will! A thousand oaths –By all–

Belinda

1108Hold –You shall not, now I think on't better.

Dorimant

1109I will swear.

Belinda

1110I shall grow jealous of the oath, and think I owe your truth to that, not to your love.

Dorimant

1111Then, by my love, no other oath I'll swear.

Enter HANDY.

Handy

1112Here's a chair.

Belinda

1113Let me go.

Dorimant

1114I cannot.

Belinda

1115Too willingly, I fear.

Dorimant

1116Too unkindly feared. When will you promise me again?

Belinda

1117Not this fortnight.

Dorimant

1118You will be better than your word.

Belinda

1119I think I shall. Will it not make you love me less? [Starting.] Hark! what fiddles are these?

[Fiddles without.

Dorimant

1120Look out Handy.

[Exit HANDY and returns.

Handy

1121Mr. Medley, Mr. Bellair, and Sir Fopling; they are coming up.

Dorimant

1122How got they in?

Handy

1123The door was open for the chair.

Belinda

1124Lord! let me fly –

Dorimant

1125Here, here, down the back stairs. I'll see you into your chair.

Belinda

1126No, no, stay and receive 'em, and be sure you keep your word and never see Loveit more: let it be a proof of your kindness.

Dorimant

1127It shall –Handy, direct, her. Everlasting love go along with thee.

[Kissing her hand.

[Exeunt BELINDA and HANDY.

Enter YOUNG BELLAIR, MEDLEY, and Sir FOPLING.

Young Bellair

1128Not a-bed yet!

Medley

1129You have had an irregular fit, Dorimant.

1130I have.

1132Nature has done her part, gentlemen; when she falls kindly to work, great cures are effected in little time, you know.

1144The shadow of himself indeed.

1145Correct the errors of his motions and his dress.

1147'Tis the best diversion in our retirements. Dorimant, thou art a pretty fellow, and wear'st thy clothes well, but I never saw thee have a handsome cravat. Dorimant

1148They are something clumsy-fisted.

1149I have brought over the prettiest fellow that ever spread a toilet; he served some time under Merille, the greatest genie in the world for a valet-de-chambre.

Dorimant

1150What, he who formerly belonged to the Duke of Candale?

1152You've a very fine brandenburgh on, Sir Fopling.

1156Pray how goes your business with Loveit?

1157You might have answered yourself in the Mall last night. Dorimant! did you not see the advances she made me? I have been endeavouring at a song.

Dorimant

1158Already!

1159'Tis my coup d'essai in English; I would fain have thy opinion of it.

Dorimant

1160Let's see it.

1161Hey, page! give me my song –Bellair, here, thou hast a pretty voice, sing it.

1162Sing it yourself, Sir Fopling.

1163Excuse me.

1164You learnt to sing in Paris.

1165I did, of Lambert, the greatest master in the world; but I have his own fault, a weak voice, and care not to sing out of a ruelle.

Dorimant

1166A ruelle is a pretty cage for a singing fop, indeed.

1167(reads the song) How charming Phyllis is! how fair!
Ah, that she were as willing
To ease my wounded heart of care,
And make her eyes less killing!
I sigh! I sigh! I languish now,
And love will not let me rest;
I drive about the Park, and bow
Still as I meet my dearest.

1168Sing it, sing it, man; it goes to a pretty new tune, which I am confident was made by Baptiste.

1169Sing it yourself, Sir Fopling; he does not know the tune.

1170I'll venture.

1172After the French way.

1174It does indeed. I perceive, Sir Fopling, you'll be the very head of the sparks who are lucky in compositions of this nature.

Dorimant

1183What's your business?

1184Ma-tri-mony, an't like you.

Dorimant

1185It does not, sir.

1186It may in time, Dorimant; what think you of Mrs. Harriet?

Dorimant

1187What does she think of me?

Young Bellair

1188I am confident she loves you.

Dorimant

1189How does it appear?

Young Bellair

1190Why, she's never well but when she's talking of you; but then she finds all the faults in you she can. She laughs at all who commend you; but then she speaks ill of all who do not.

Dorimant

1191Women of her temper betray themselves by their over-cunning. I had once a growing love with a lady who would always quarrel with me when I came to see her, and yet was never quiet if I stayed a day from her.

1192My father is in love with Emilia.

Dorimant

1197I may fall into the snare too. But
