Act III

SCENE I

The PEDANT, FABRIZIO and STRAGUALCIA

PEDANT:

483This city seems completely changed since I was last here. Actually I was here only briefly with the delegation from Ancona, and we stayed at the Guicciardino. Still, we were here about six days. [To FABRIZIO] Do you recognize anything?

FABRIZIO:

484It's like I've never seen it before.

PEDANT:

485That makes sense. After all, you left when you were so young that it's no wonder. Wait, I recognize this street. That's the palace of the Rangoni, over there's the Grand Canal, and that church you see there at the head of the street is the cathedral. Have you ever heard the saying "Were you ever the pussy of Modena" or "You think you're the pussy of Modena"?

FABRIZIO:

486A thousand times. Show it to me, please.

PEDANT:

487See that statue above the door of the cathedral?

FABRIZIO:

488That one?

PEDANT:

489That's it.

FABRIZIO:

490Wow! That's a scream!

PEDANT:

491Now you understand.

FABRIZIO:

492I've also heard the saying "You're trying to lead the bear of Modena." What does that mean, and where's this bear?

PEDANT:

493There are sayings of the antiqui de quibus nescitur oigo.

FABRIZIO:

494I really have the feeling, Professor, that this place is going to bring me good things.

STRAGUALCIA:

495And it's going to bring me even better things, because I smell the sweet pefume that comes from a roast, and it's making me die of hunger.

PEDANT:

496Oh! Don't you know what Cantalicius said? "Dulcis amor patriae". And Cato: "Pugna pro patria" Hoc, in summa, there is not a sweeter thing than one's fatherland.

STRAGUALCIA:

497I believe that Trebbiano wine is much sweeter, Tutor. Boy, could I use a cup of that now! My back's broken from carrying this baggage.

PEDANT:

498These streets seem newly paved. When I was here before they were all dirty and muddy.

STRAGUALCIA:

499Are we going to stand around and count the cobblestones? Let's get going! Let's hurry up and find a place where we can eat, that's what I want.

PEDANT:

500Landundun animus est in patinis.

FABRIZIO:

501Whose coat of arms is that over there with the auger on it?

PEDANT:

502That's the coat of arms this community, and it's called the Trivella. And just as in Florence they cry "Marzocco, Marzocco!" and in Venice "San Marco, San Marco!" and in Siena "Lupa, Lupa!" here they cry "Trivella, Trivella!"

STRAGUALCIA:

503I would rather we yell "Frying pan, frying pan!"

FABRIZIO:

504That one I recognize: it's the arms of the duke.

STRAGUALCIA:

505Tutor, why don't you carry this baggage yourself for a bit? My lips are so parched with thirst that I can barely talk.

PEDANT:

506Buck up. You can quench your first later!

STRAGUALCIA:

507When I'm dead, then you'll make me a heavenly broth to drink.

FABRIZIO:

508I'm glad that I really like this place, event at first sight. What about you, Stragualcia?

STRAGUALCIA:

509It seems like heaven to me: here too one doesn't eat or drink. Let's get going and not waste any more time looking at the place. We can look at it at our leisure after we eat.

PEDANT:

510There you see the most egregious bell tower that exists in the whole structure of the universe.

STRAGUALCIA:

511Is that the one the people of Modena wanted to put a sheath over and whose shadow they claim makes a man go crazy?

PEDANT:

512Yes, that's it.

STRAGUALCIA:

513I know that as far as I'm concerned I'm not going to leave the kitchen. Whoever wants to wander around can do so. Now let's find a place to stay.

PEDANT:

514You're in a great rush.

STRAGUALCIA:

515A pox on you! Here I am dying of hunger, and I haven't had a thing to eat this morning besides that half a hen that you left me on the boat.

FABRIZIO:

516Whom can we find to show us to my father's house?

PEDANT:

517Actually, it seems to me that first we should find a place to stay at an inn and rest a bit there. Then we can look for him at our leisure,

FABRIZIO:

518I like that idea. These seem to be inns.

SCENE II

AGIATO, FRULLA, the PEDANT, FABRIZIO, and STRAGUALCIA

AGIATO:

519Oh, kind sirs! This is the inn for you, if you want lodging. Come to the Mirror, to the Mirror!

FRULLA:

520Oh, you're most welcome here! I've been your host other times. Don't remember your Frulla? Come in here, where all people of your quality put up.

AGIATO:

521Come stay with me. You'll have good rooms, a good fire, excellent beds, freshly washed sheets, and you'll not lack for anything that's yours.

STRAGUALCIA:

522I should certainly hope so!

AGIATO:

523I meant to say you won't lack for anything that's your desire.

FRULLA:

524I'll give you the best wine in Lombardy, big fat birds, sausages like this, pigeons, large chickens, and whatever you ask for. And you'll really enjoy everything.

STRAGUALCIA:

525This is what I want most of all.

PEDANT:

526And you, what do you say?

AGIATO:

527I'll give you sweetbreads, mortadella, wine from the mountains, and above all you'll enjoy fine, delicate things.

FRULLA:

528I'll give you lots of food, and you can forget delicate things. If you stay with me I'll treat you like lords, and you can pay me as you wish. At the Mirror, however, you'll pay for everything, even the candles. You decide.

STRAGUALCIA:

529Master, let's stay here, it's clearly better.

AGIATO:

530If you want to lodge well, it would be better for you to stay with me. Do you want people saying that you're staying at the Joker?

FRULLA:

531My Joker is a hundred times better than your Mirror.

PEDANT:

532Speculum prudentia significat iuxia illud Catonis, "Nosce teipsum. " Do you understand, Fabrizio?

FABRIZIO:

533I understand.

FRULLA:

534See for yourself who has more guests, [tauntingly to AGIATO] you or me.

AGIATO:

535See for yourself where the important people stay.

FRULLA:

536See where they are better treated.

AGIATO:

537See who has more fine things.

STRAGUALCIA:

538Enough of these fine things, fine things, fine things. I want just one thing, to fill my whole body, and you can keep your fine things, as far as I'm concerned. Too many fine things are fine for the Florentines.

AGIATO:

539They all stay with me.

FRULLA:

540They used to stay with you, but over the last three years they've all come over to my sign.

AGIATO:

541Young man, pit that baggage down here, for I see that it's tiring you out.

STRAGUALCIA:

542Don't worry about that, I've no desire to lighten the load on my shoulders until I'm sure I'm going to load up my belly.

FRULLA:

543Would a couple of fat capons do the trick? Bring the bag over here, and these are all yours.

STRAGUALCIA:

544Well, why not! But they'll only be an appetizer!

AGIATO:

545Look at this prosciutto! Doesn't it look like crimson silk?

PEDANT:

546That's not bad.

FRULLA:

547Who knows about wine?

STRAGUALCIA:

548Me, me! Why, I know more than the French.

FRULLA:

549See if you like this, then. If not, I can give you at least ten others.

STRAGUALCIA:

550Frulla, for my money you are a much better innkeeper than he is. He's not it backwards: he shows you something that will make you want to drink before he lets you see whether the wine is any good. [Tasting the wine] Oh, Master, this is good. Go ahead, take the bag.

PEDANT:

551Wait a moment. You, what do you have to say?

AGIATO:

552I say that gentlemen don't worry about stuffing their stomachs with a lot of food but rather eat small helpings of food that is fine and delicate.

STRAGUALCIA:

553He must be running a hospital or an inn for the ill.

PEDANT:

554[To AGIATO] You're making sense. What will you give us?

AGIATO:

555Ask for what you wish.

FRULLA:

556I'm amazed at you gentlemen. When there is plenty of food, a man may eat as much or as little as he likes, which is not the case when there is little. Moreover, when a man begins to eat, his appetite grows and he needs to fill up with bread.

STRAGUALCIA:

557"You are wiser than the law," as they say. I've never met a man who understands my needs better than you. Let's go, because I think I love you.

FRULLA:

558Go on into the kitchen, brother, and take a look around.

PEDANT:

559Omnis repletio mala, panis autem pessima.

STRAGUALCIA:

560Learned fool! One of these days I'll rearrange your teeth, if I live long enough.

[He enters the JOKER]

AGIATO:

561Come with me, gentlemen, for it's not a prudent thing to stand around outside in the cold.

FABRIZIO:

562Oh, come now, we're not that fine and delicate!

FRULLA:

563Listen, my lords, than inn in Lombardy. But ever since I opened the Joker, he's lucky if ten people stay there in a year. And my inn is better known than any other in the whole world. Lots of Frenchmen come here, and all the Germans who come through town.

AGIATO:

564You're lying. The Germans go to the Pig.

FRULLA:

565Visitors from Milan, Parma, and Piacenza stay with me.

AGIATO:

566The Venetians, Genoese, and Florentines stay with me.

PEDANT:

567Where do the Neapolitans stay?

FRULLA:

568With me.

AGIATO:

569Are you kidding? Most of them stay at the Love.

FRULLA:

570And how many of them stay with me?

FABRIZIO:

571The duke of Malfi, where does he stay?

AGIATO:

572Sometimes with me, sometimes with him, sometimes at the Sword, sometimes at the Love, depending on what he wants.

PEDANT:

573Where do the Romans stay? Because we have come from Rome.

AGIATO:

574With me.

FRULLA:

575That's a lie. You won't find one who has stayed there all year. It's true that some old cardinals out of habit have stayed there, but all the new ones belong at the Joker.

STRAGUALCIA:

576[Coming out of the JOKER] I won't leave this place unless I'm dragged out. The others can go where they like. Master, there are so many big pots around the fire, so many soups, so many savories, so many sauces, such great spits of roasted pigeon, partridge, thrush, and goat, capons, boiled meats, roasts, and wonderful macaroni, lasagna, pies - if he were getting ready for carnival or the entire court of Rome, he would still have enough to satisfy everyone.

FRULLA:

577Did you try the wine?

STRAGUALCIA:

578And that wine!

PEDANT:

579Variorum ciborum commistio pessima generat digestionem.

STRAGUALCIA:

580Bus asinorum, buorum castronorum, tatte, battate, pecoronibus! What the devil are you up to? May you catch the pox, you and all the other pedants in the world! You're a scoundrel, as far as I'm concerned. Let's stay here, Master.

FABRIZIO:

581Where do the Spanish stay?

FRULLA:

582I avoid having any dealings with them. They stay at the Bandit. But what else could you want? There isn't anyone who comes through here who doesn't stay with me, except for the Sienese, who are so friendly with the Modenese that when they visit they have a hundred friends who put them up in their homes. Lords and great professors, poor and rich, soldiers and good men, they all run to the Joker.

AGIATO:

583All the doctors, judges, friars, and learned men come to my inn.

FRULLA:

584And I'll tell that there hardly passes a day that someone doesn't leave the Mirror to come and stay with me.

FABRIZIO:

585Tutor, what should we do?

PEDANT:

586Etiam atque etiam cogitandum.

STRAGUALCIA:

587Body of mine, it's time to fatten up. For once I'm going to eat my fill and rally pig out.

PEDANT:

588I am thinking, Fabrizio, that we are rather low on funds.

STRAGUALCIA:

589Tutor, inside there I saw the innkeeper's young son, handsome as an angel.

PEDANT:

590Come on, then! Let's stay at the Joker. In any case, your father, if we find him, will pay the innkeeper.

STRAGUALCIA:

591[Aside] I thought that bit about the boy would be just the bait to catch that old bird! I've already had three glasses of wine, although I'm claiming only one. And I'm not going to leave the kitchen until I've tasted everything. Then I'll have a nap by that good fire. And to heel with anyone who wants to hold back!

AGIATO:

592Watch out, Frulla. You've cheated me once too often, and one of these days we're going to have it out once and for all.

FRULLA:

593Whenever you like. I'm ready and waiting.

SCENE III

VIRGINIO and CLEMENZIA

VIRGINIO:

594Is this the way you've taught her to behave? Is this the honor she pays me? Oh, how unlucky I am! I've worked so hard to overcome my evil fortune, and for this? To see my patrimony without heirs; to see my house ruined; to see my daughter a whore; to become the subject of common gossip; to be unable to show my face in public; to be pointed out by children in the streets; to be help up as a warning by the old; to be put in a comedy by the Intronati; to be made an example of in tales; and to be made the subject of the gossip of every woman in this city? Maybe these women aren't gossips, eh? Maybe they don't like to malign others, eh? But I imagine it doesn't really matter, because everyone knows everything already. Actually, I'm certain, for if even one woman knows it, in three hours the whole town knows it. I'm a disgraced father, a miserable and sad old man who has lived too long! What can I do? What should I think?

CLEMENZIA:

595You'd be better off making less noise so you can concentrate on making sure she returns home before the whole town finds out. But I think Sister Blabbermouth is as full of hot air as is the idea that Lelia is really running around town dressed as a man! Be sure that the nuns aren't just saying this because they hope you'll make her a nun and leave everything to the convent.

VIRGINIO:

596How could she be lying? She even told me that Lelia is a servant of a Modenese gentleman and that he hasn't realized she's a woman.

CLEMENZIA:

597Anything is possible, but me, I can't believe it.

VIRGINIO:

598And I certainly can't believe he hasn't figured out that she's a woman.

CLEMENZIA:

599I didn't mean that.

VIRGINIO:

600I have to say it, because my honor's on the line. Actually, I created the problem myself by giving the girl on to you to bring up even though I knew what you were.

CLEMENZIA:

601Virginio, enough of such talk. If I've been a sinner, it's because you made me one. You know very well that before you, there was no one but my husband. And young girls need to be treated differently than you have treated Lelia. Aren't you ashamed to be trying to marry her off to a croaking old fool who could be her grandfather?

VIRGINIO:

602What do you have against old men, you slut? They're a thousand times better than young ones.

CLEMENZIA:

603You've lost your senses, and that's why everyone is deceiving you and telling you such foolish gossip.

VIRGINIO:

604If I find her, I'll drag her home by the hair.

CLEMENZIA:

605That way you'll belike those men who flaunt the horns on their own heads instead of sensibility hiding them.

VIRGINIO:

606I don't care. People will fins out anyway. The best that I can do is chop them off.

CLEMENZIA:

607Do what think best, just be sure you don't chop off your head along with the horns.

VIRGINIO:

608I've been told how she's dressed, so I'm going to find her, and when I do there'll be hell to pay.

CLEMENZIA:

609Do as you please. For myself, I'm off; no sense preaching to the deaf. These men!

SCENE IV

FABRIZIO and FRULLA

FABRIZIO:

610While my two servants are sleeping, I'm going to visit the town. When they wake up, tell them to head towards the main square.

FRULLA:

611You know, my lord, if I hadn't seen you dressed in those clothes, I'd swear that you were the young servant of a local gentleman who dressed like you, in white, and is the spitting image of you.

FABRIZIO:

612Might he be some brother of mine?

FRULLA:

613He could.

FABRIZIO:

614Tell the Professor to find the person we've come to see me.

FRULLA:

615I'll take care of it.

SCENE V

PASQUELLA and FABRIZIO

PASQUELLA:

616My goodness, here he is. I was afraid I was going to have to search the whole city for him. Fabio, how good to see you! I was looking for you, so you've saved me the time and effort of finding you. My dear, my mistress wants you to come to see her right now about something of importance to you and her - I don't know what.

FABRIZIO:

617Who is your mistress?

PASQUELLA:

618You know very well who she is, you. My goodness, you two make a fine couple!

FABRIZIO:

619Actually, we're not a couple! But if she wants to couple, I'm ready.

PASQUELLA:

620I mean that you two aren't very quick. I'd like to be still young like you and able to enjoy such a feast! And I know that if I were you, I'd have already put aside my suspicions and scruples. But you'll get down to it soon enough, right?

FABRIZIO:

621Please, madam! You don't know me. You've mistaken me for someone else, so leave me alone.

PASQUELLA:

622Oh, don't get me wrong, dear Fabio, I'm saying this to help you out.

FABRIZIO:

623I haven't gotten anything wrong. But my name isn't Fabio, and I'm not who you think.

PASQUELLA:

624Well, you two will have to work things out to your own satisfaction. But you know, there are very few young women her equal in wealth or beauty in this city. And I'd rather see you get down to it instead of running back and forth all the time, giving others reason to talk without gaining anything for yourself and with little honor for her.

FABRIZIO:

625[Aside] What's all this about? I don't understand. Either she's mad or she has mistaken me for someone else. But I want to see where she's leading me. [To PASQUELLA] Let's go.

PASQUELLA:

626Oh! I think I hear people in the house. Wait here a bit until I see if Isabella is alone. I'll call you if no one's there.

[She enters the house]

FABRIZIO:

627I want to see how this story ends. Maybe this woman is the servant of some courtesan and she thinks she'll get some money out of me. If that's the case, she's mistaken. I'm virtually a studen of the Spanish, and in the end I'll be more likely to have a scudo of hers before she has a carlin of mine. One of us will be deceived, and it won't be me. I'm going to move away a bit from this house so that I can see the type of people who go in and out and judge what type of person this woman is.

SCENE VI

GHERARDO, VIRGINIO, and PASQUELLA

GHERARDO:

628Please forgive me, but if that's what's happened, I'm going to turn her down. I'm afraid that your daughter did this because she didn't want me, and worse yet I'm convinced that she did it because she has had other lovers.

VIRGINIO:

629Don't think that, Gherardo. Do you think I would have told you any of this if that were the case? Please don't ruin our arrangements.

GHERARDO:

630Please don't talk to me about it anymore.

VIRGINIO:

631Oh! Are you going to betray your word?

GHERARDO:

632Yes, if someone's deeds betray me, I see no reason to keep my word. In the end you don't even know if you can get her back. You're trying to sell me the proverbial bird in the bush. Look, I heard everything you said to Clemenzia.

VIRGINIO:

633As long as I don't have her, I won't try to give her to you, but if I get her back, wouldn't you be willing to marry her immediately?

GHERARDO:

634Virginio, I had the most honorable wife who ever lived in this city, and I have a young daughter who's a pure dove. How can you ask me to take into my home a woman who has fled from her father and goes from house to house like a whore dressed as a man? Don't you understand that if I do that I won't be able to marry my daughter off?

VIRGINIO:

635After a few days no one will be talking about it anymore. What does it really matter? And no one else besides us knows about it.

GHERARDO:

636Soon everyone in town will be talking.

VIRGINIO:

637That's no true.

GHERARDO:

638How long has she been gone?

VIRGINIO:

639Since yesterday or this morning.

GHERARDO:

640I wish to God it were true. But are you sure she's in Modena?

VIRGINIO:

641Absolutely.

GHERARDO:

642All right. Find her, and then we can speak again.

VIRGINIO:

643Promise that you'll take her?

GHERARDO:

644I'll think about it.

VIRGINIO:

645Go ahead, say yes.

GHERARDO:

646I can't say that, but...

VIRGINIO:

647Go ahead, speak up.

[PASQUELLA enters]

GHERARDO:

648[Aside to VIRGINIO] Quiet! [To PASQUELLA] What are you doing here, Pasquella? What is Isabella doing?

PASQUELLA:

649What do you think? She's busy praying before her little altar.

GHERARDO:

650May she be blessed! I have a daughter who's always praying. Who could ask for more?

PASQUELLA:

651Oh, you've described her perfectly. She goes without meat every day that God requires it, and she says her prayers like a little saint.

GHERARDO:

652She's just like her blessed mother.

PASQUELLA:

653Exactly. Oh, the good that wretched woman did! She punished herself more and wore more hair-shirts than anyone else in recent history, and she made charity her life; and if it weren't for her love of you there wouldn't have been a friar, priest, or poor man who came to her door who wouldn't have received everything she had to offer.

VIRGINIO:

654Now those were good deeds!

PASQUELLA:

655Why, many times she'd get up an hour or two before dawn to go to the first mass of the Friars of Saint Francis, for she didn't want to be seen or thought to be a paud like certain saint sniffers I know.

GHERARDO:

656What? "Badw"? What do you mean?

PASQUELLA:

657That's right, "bawd." How do you say it?

VIRGINIO:

658That's an insult!

PASQUELLA:

659I know I've heard her say it.

GHERARDO:

660You meant to say "fraud".

PASQUELLA:

661Maybe. But what I really wanted to say is that her daughter is even more like that than she was.

GHERARDO:

662Good willing.

VIRGINIO:

663[Seeing GHERARDO] Oh, Gherardo, Gherardo! Here's the person we were talking about! Oh, unhappy father that I am. She may hide or run, now that she's seen me. Grab her!

GHERARDO:

664Be sure you're not making a mistake. Maybe it's not her.

VIRGINIO:

665Who wouldn't know her? Don't I see all the signs that Sister Blabbermouth told me to look for?

PASQUELLA:

666[Aside] Things are looking bad. I'm afraid I'm in trouble.

SCENE VII

VIRGINIO, GHERARDO, and FABRIZIO

VIRGINIO:

667[To FABRIZIO] Good day, young lady. Is this any way for a young lady to dress? Is this the way you honor your family? Is this the happiness you give to this poor old man? If only I'd been dead when I fathered you! It seems that the only reason you were born was to dishonor me and bury me alive! Oh, Gherardo! What do you think of your bride? Do you think she is bringing honor to us?

GHERARDO:

668I wouldn't say so. My bride, hardly!

VIRGINIO:

669Tramp! Disgrace! It would serve you right if this man didn't want you as his wife any longer and no one else would take you! But he won't hold your foolishness against you; he wants to have you.

GHERARDO:

670Not so fast!

VIRGINIO:

671[Pointing to GHERARDO’s house] Get in that house over there, you disgrace! It's clear that the milk your mother gave you the day that I sired you was damned.

FABRIZIO:

672Old man, don't you have sons, relatives, or friends in this city who can take care of you?

VIRGINIO:

673What a reply! What are you talking about?

FABRIZIO:

674Well, in view of your need of a doctor, I'm amazed that they've let you out of the house. In any other city they'd keep someone like you tied down.

VIRGINIO:

675I ought to have kept you tied up! Why, I'd like to cut your throat! Bring me a knife.

FABRIZIO:

676Old man, you don't know mw very well. And perhaps you're insulting me like this because you think I'm a foreigner. But I'm as much from Modena as you and the son of a good a father and family as you yourself.

GHERARDO:

677[Aside] She really is pretty. If there's no other problem than what one can see, I'll take her.

VIRGINIO:

678Well, then, why did you run off from your father and the place where I sent you?

FABRIZIO:

679You never sent me anywhere that I know, but I was forced to leave.

VIRGINIO:

680Forced, eh? Who forced you?

FABRIZIO:

681The Spaniards.

VIRGINIO:

682And now where have you come from?

FABRIZIO:

683From the military camp.

VIRGINIO:

684From the military camp?

FABRIZIO:

685Yes, from the military camp.

GHERARDO:

686[Aside] If that's how it is, the marriage is off!

VIRGINIO:

687You're ruined!

FABRIZIO:

688And you too, old man!

VIRGINIO:

689Gherardo, please put her in your house so that she won't be seen in this state.

GHERARDO:

690I will not. Send her to your own house!

VIRGINIO:

691In the name of our friendship, let her in!

GHERARDO:

692I said no.

VIRGINIO:

693Listen a second. [He and GHERARDO move off to talk privately] This way you'll be sure she won't run off.

FABRIZIO:

694[Alone] I've known many crazies from Modena, whom I'll not name, but I've never seen any like this old man who weren't tied down or locked up. What a strange humor he's in! He's out of his mind, at least from what I can tell, because he seems to think that young men are young women. Oh, this is even more intriguingly strange than the story Molza told about a Sienese woman who thought she had become a Cretan vase, especially since women are usually held to be more foolish than old men, who for a host of reasons ought to be very wise. And not for a hundred scudi would I want to miss the chance to tell the tale of this madness during the carnival! But they're coming back. Let's see what they have to say.

GHERARDO:

695I'll tell you the truth, on the one hand I think I will, on the other I think I won't. Perhaps you could question her a little more closely.

VIRGINIO:

696[To FABRIZIO] Come here.
