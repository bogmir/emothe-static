EPILOGUE

2583Poets, like disputants, when reasons fail,
2584Have one sure refuge left – and that's to rail.
2585Fop, coxcomb, fool, are thundered through the pit;
2586And this is all their equipage of wit.
2587We wonder how the devil this difference grows,
2588Betwixt our fools in verse, and yours in prose:
2589For, 'faith, the quarrel rightly understood,
2590'Tis civil war with their own flesh and blood.
2591The threadbare author hates the gaudy coat;
2592And swears at the gilt coach, but swears afoot:
2593For 'tis observed of every scribbling man,
2594He grows a fop as fast as e'er he can;
2595Prunes up, and asks his oracle, the glass,
2596If pink or purple best become his face.
2597For our poor wretch, he neither rails nor prays;
2598Nor likes your with just as you like his plays;
2599He has not yet so much of Mr. Bayes.
2600He does his best; and if he cannot please,
2601Would quietly sue out his writ of ease .
2602Yet, if he might his own grand jury call,
2603By the fair sex he begs to stand or fall.
2604Let Cæsar's power the men's ambition move,
2605But grace you him who lost the world for love!
2606Yet if some antiquated lady say,
2607The last age is not copied in his play;
2608Heaven help the man who for that face must drudge,
2609Which only has the wrinkles of a judge.
2610Let not the young and beauteous join with those;
2611For should you raise such numerous hosts of foes,
2612Young wits and sparks he to his aid must call;
2613'Tis more than one man's work to please you all.
