Tis Pity She's a Whore

[II.i]

Enter Giovanni and Annabella, as from their chamber.

GIOVANNI.

1Come, Annabella: no more sister now,
2But love, a name more gracious; do not blush,
3Beauty’s sweet wonder, but be proud to know
4That yielding thou hast conquer’d, and inflam’d
5A heart whose tribute is thy brother’s life.

ANNABELLA.

6And mine is his. O, how these stol’n contents
7Would print a modest crimson on my cheeks,
8Had any but my heart’s delight prevail’d!

GIOVANNI.

9I marvel why the chaster of your sex
10Should think this pretty toy call’d maidenhead
11So strange a loss, when, being lost, ’tis nothing,
12And you are still the same.

ANNABELLA.

And you are still the same. ’Tis well for you;
13Now you can talk.

GIOVANNI.

Now you can talk. Music as well consists
14In th’ ear, as in the playing.

ANNABELLA.

In th’ ear, as in the playing. O, y’are wanton;
15Tell on’t, y’are best: do.

GIOVANNI.

Tell on’t, y’are best: do. Thou wilt chide me then.
16Kiss me: so; thus hung Jove on Leda’s neck,
17And suck’d divine ambrosia from her lips.
18I envy not the mightiest man alive,
19But hold myself in being king of thee
20More great than were I king of all the world.
21But I shall lose you, sweetheart.

ANNABELLA.

But I shall lose you, sweetheart. But you shall not.

GIOVANNI.

22You must be married, mistress.

ANNABELLA.

You must be married, mistress. Yes? To whom?

GIOVANNI.

23Someone must have you.

ANNABELLA.

Someone must have you. You must.

GIOVANNI.

Someone must have you. You must. Nay, some other.

ANNABELLA.

24Now prithee do not speak so: without jesting,
25You’ll make me weep in earnest.

GIOVANNI.

You’ll make me weep in earnest. What, you will not!
26But tell me, sweet, canst thou be dar’d to swear
27That thou wilt live to me, and to no other?

ANNABELLA.

28By both our loves I dare, for didst thou know,
29My Giovanni, how all suitors seen
30To my eyes hateful, thou wouldst trust me then.

GIOVANNI.

31Enough, I take thy word. Sweet, we must part:
32Remember what thou vow’st, keep well my heart.

ANNABELLA.

Will you be gone?

GIOVANNI.

I must.

ANNABELLA.

When to return?

GIOVANNI.

Soon.

ANNABELLA.

Look you do.

GIOVANNI.

Farewell.

Exit.

ANNABELLA.

39Go where thou wilt, in mind I’ll keep thee here,
40And where thou art, I know I shall be there.

Guardian!

Enter Putana.

PUTANA.

Child, how is’t, child? Well, thank Heaven, ha?

ANNABELLA.

43O guardian, what a paradise of joy
44Have I pass’d over!

PUTANA.

Nay, what a paradise of joy have you pass’d under! Why, now I commend thee, charge: fear nothing, sweetheart; what though he be your brother? Your brother’s a man, I hope, and I say still, if a young wench feel the fit upon her, let her take anybody, father or brother, all is one.

ANNABELLA.

50I would not have it known for all the world.

PUTANA.

Nor I, indeed, for the speech of the people; else ’twere nothing.

FLORIO.

(within). Daughter Annabella!

ANNABELLA.

54O me, my father! –Here, sir! –Reach my work.

FLORIO.

55(within). What are you doing?

ANNABELLA.

(within). What are you doing? So: let him come now.

Enter Florio, Richardetto like a doctor of physic, and Philotis with a lute in her hand.

FLORIO.

56So hard at work? That’s well, you lose no time.
57Look, I have brought you company: here’s one,
58A learned doctor lately come from Padua,
59Much skill’d in physic, and for that I see
60You have of late been sickly, I entreated
61This reverend man to visit you some time.

ANNABELLA.

62Y’are very welcome, sir.

RICHARDETTO.

Y’are very welcome, sir. I thank you, mistress.
63Loud fame in large report hath spoke your praise
64As well for virtue as perfection:
65For which I have been bold to bring with me
66A kinswoman of mine, a maid, for song
67And music one perhaps will give content;
68Please you to know her.

ANNABELLA.

Please you to know her. They are parts I love,
69And she for them most welcome.

PHILOTIS.

And she for them most welcome. Thank you, lady.

FLORIO.

70Sir, now you know my house, pray make not strange,
71And if you find my daughter need your art,
72I’ll be your paymaster.

RICHARDETTO.

I’ll be your paymaster. Sir, what I am
73She shall command.

FLORIO.

She shall command. You shall bind me to you.
74Daughter, I must have conference with you
75About some matters that concerns us both.
76Good master doctor, please you but walk in,
77We’ll crave a little of your cousin’s cunning.
78I think my girl hath not quite forgot
79To touch an instrument: she could have done’t;
80We’ll hear them both.

RICHARDETTO.

We’ll hear them both. I’ll wait upon you, sir.

Exeunt.

[II.ii]

Enter Soranzo in his study reading a book.

SORANZO.

1“Love’s measure is extreme, the comfort, pain,
2The life unrest, and the reward disdain.”
3What’s here? Look’t o’er again; ’tis so, so writes
4This smooth licentious poet in his rhymes.
5But Sannazar, thou liest, for had thy bosom
6Felt such oppression as is laid on mine,
7Thou wouldst have kiss’d the rod that made thee smart.
8To work then, happy muse, and contradict
9What Sannazar hath in his envy writ.
10“Love’s measure is the mean, sweet his annoys,
11His pleasure’s life, and his reward all joys.”
12Had Annabella liv’d when Sannazar
13Did in his brief encomium celebrate
14Venice, that queen of cities, he had left
15That verse which gain’d him such a sum of gold,
16And for one only look from Annabel
17Had writ of her and her diviner cheeks.
18O how my thoughts are–

VASQUES.

(within). Pray forbear; in rules of civility, let me give notice on’t: I shall be tax’d of my neglect of duty and service.

SORANZO.

21What rude intrusion interrupts my peace?
22Can I be nowhere private?

VASQUES.

(within). Troth you wrong your modesty.

SORANZO.

24What’s the matter, Vasques, who is’t?

Enter Hippolita and Vasques.

HIPPOLITA.

25’Tis I:
26Do you know me now? Look, perjur’d man, on her
27Whom thou and thy distracted lust have wrong’d.
28Thy sensual rage of blood hath made my youth
29A scorn to men and angels, and shall I
30Be now a foil to thy unsated change?
31Thou know’st, false wanton, when my modest fame
32Stood free from stain or scandal, all the charms
33Of hell or sorcery could not prevail
34Against the honor of my chaster bosom.
35Thine eyes did plead in tears, thy tongue in oaths
36Such and so many, that a heart of steel
37Would have been wrought to pity, as was mine:
38And shall the conquest of my lawful bed,
39My husband’s death urg’d on by his disgrace,
40My loss of womanhood, be ill rewarded
41With hatred and contempt? No, know Soranzo,
42I have spirit doth as much distaste
43The slavery of fearing thee, as thou
44Dost loathe the memory of what hath pass’d.

SORANZO.

45Nay, dear Hippolita–

HIPPOLITA.

Nay, dear Hippolita– Call me not dear,
46Nor think with supple words to smooth the grossness
47Of my abuses; ’tis not your new mistress,
48Your goodly madam-merchant, shall triumph
49On my dejection: tell her thus from me,
50My birth was nobler and by much more free.

SORANZO.

51You are too violent.

HIPPOLITA.

You are too violent. You are too double
52In your dissimulation. Seest thou this,
53This habit, these black mourning-weeds of care?
54’Tis thou art cause of this, and hast divorc’d
55My husband from his life and me from him,
56And made me widow in my widowhood.

SORANZO.

57Will you yet hear?

HIPPOLITA.

Will you yet hear? More of thy perjuries?
58Thy soul is drown’d too deeply in those sins;
59Thou need’st not add to th’ number.

SORANZO.

Thou need’st not add to th’ number. Then I’ll leave you;
60You are past all rules of sense.

HIPPOLITA.

You are past all rules of sense. And thou of grace.

VASQUES.

Fie, mistress, you are not near the limits of reason: if my lord had a resolution as noble as virtue itself, you take the course to unedge it all. Sir, I beseech you, do not perplex her; griefs, alas, will have a vent. I dare undertake Madam Hippolita will now freely hear you.

SORANZO.

Talk to a woman frantic! Are these the fruits of your love?

HIPPOLITA.

67They are the fruits of thy untruth, false man:
68Didst thou not swear, whilst yet my husband liv’d,
69That thou wouldst wish no happiness on earth
70More than to call me wife? Didst thou not vow,
71When he should die, to marry me? For which,
72The devil in my blood, and thy protests,
73Caus’d me to counsel him to undertake
74A voyage to Ligorn, for that we heard
75His brother there was dead, and left a daughter
76Young and unfriended, who, with much ado,
77I wish’d him to bring hither: he did so,
78And went; and as thou know’st died on the way.
79Unhappy man, to buy his death so dear
80With my advice! Yet thou for whom I did it
81Forget’st thy vows, and leav’st me to my shame.

SORANZO.

82Who could help this?

HIPPOLITA.

Who could help this? Who? Perjur’d man, thou couldst,
83If thou hadst faith or love.

SORANZO.

If thou hadst faith or love. You are deceiv’d.
84The vows I made, if you remember well,
85Were wicked and unlawful: ’twere more sin
86To keep them than to break them; as for me,
87I cannot mask my penitence. Think thou
88How much thou hast digress’d from honest shame
89In bringing of a gentleman to death
90Who was thy husband, such a one as he,
91So noble in his quality, condition,
92Learning, behaviour, entertainment, love,
93As Parma could not show a braver man.

VASQUES.

You do not well, this was not your promise.

SORANZO.

95I care not; let her know her monstrous life.
96Ere I’ll be servile to so black a sin,
97I’ll be accurs’d. Woman, come here no more:
98Learn to repent and die, for by my honor
99I hate thee and thy lust: you have been too foul.

[Exit.]

VASQUES.

This part has been scurvily play’d.

HIPPOLITA.

101How foolishly this beast contemns his fate,
102And shuns the use of that which I more scorn
103Than I once lov’d, his love; but let him go.
104My vengeance shall give comfort to this woe.

She offers to go away.

VASQUES.

Mistress, mistress, Madam Hippolita, pray, a word or two!

HIPPOLITA.

With me, sir?

VASQUES.

With you, if you please.

HIPPOLITA.

What is’t?

VASQUES.

I know you are infinitely mov’d now, and you think you have cause: some I confess you have, but sure not so much as you imagine.

HIPPOLITA.

Indeed?

VASQUES.

O, you were miserably bitter, which you followed even to the last syllable. Faith, you were somewhat too shrewd; by my life you could not have took my lord in a worse time, since I first knew him: tomorrow you shall find him a new man.

HIPPOLITA.

Well, I shall wait his leisure.

VASQUES.

Fie, this is not a hearty patience, it comes sourly from you; troth, let me persuade you for once.

HIPPOLITA.

I have it, and it shall be so; thanks, opportunity!

VASQUES.

Visit him in some milder temper. O if you could but master a little your female spleen, how might you win him!

HIPPOLITA.

He will never love me. Vasques, thou hast been a too trusty servant to such a master, and I believe thy reward in the end will fall out like mine.

VASQUES.

So perhaps too.

HIPPOLITA.

Resolve thyself it will. Had I one so true, so truly honest, so secret to my counsels, as thou hast been to him and his, I should think it a slight acquittance, not only to make him master of all I have, but even of myself.

VASQUES.

O you are a noble gentlewoman!

HIPPOLITA.

Wilt thou feed always upon hopes? Well, I know thou art wise, and seest the reward of an old servant daily, what it is.

VASQUES.

Beggary and neglect.

HIPPOLITA.

True: but Vasques, wert thou mine, and wouldst be private to me and my designs, I here protest myself and all what I can else call mine should be at thy dispose.

VASQUES.

Work you that way, old mole? Then I have the wind of you.

HIPPOLITA.

What then?

VASQUES.

I should then hope to live in these my old years with rest and security.

HIPPOLITA.

145Give me thy hand: now promise but thy silence,
146And help to bring to pass a plot I have;
147And here in sight of Heaven, that being done,
148I make thee lord of me and mine estate.

VASQUES.

Come, you are merry; this is such a happiness that I can neither think or believe.

HIPPOLITA.

151Promise thy secrecy, and ’tis confirm’d.

VASQUES.

Then here I call our good genii for witnesses, whatsoever you designs are, or against whomsoever, I will not only be a special actor therein, but never disclose it till it be effected.

HIPPOLITA.

156I take thy word, and with that, thee for mine;
157Come then, let’s more confer of this anon.
158On this delicious bane my thoughts shall banquet:
159Revenge shall sweeten what my griefs have tasted.

Exeunt.

[II.iii]

Enter Richardetto and Philotis.

RICHARDETTO.

1Thou seest, my lovely niece, these strange mishaps,
2How all my fortunes turn to my disgrace,
3Wherein I am but as a looker-on,
4Whiles others act my shame and I am silent.

PHILOTIS.

5But uncle, wherein can this borrowed shape
6Give you content?

RICHARDETTO.

Give you content? I’ll tell thee, gentle niece.
7Thy wanton aunt in her lascivious riots
8Lives now secure, thinks I am surely dead
9In my late journey to Ligorn for you,
10As I have caus’d it to be rumor’d out;
11Now would I see with what an impudence
12She gives scope to her loose adultery,
13And how the common voice allows hereof:
14Thus far I have prevail’d.

PHILOTIS.

Thus far I have prevail’d. Alas, I fear
15You mean some strange revenge.

RICHARDETTO.

You mean some strange revenge. O, be not troubled;
16Your ignorance shall plead for you in all.
17But to our business: what, you learn’d for certain
18How Signor Florio means to give his daughter
19In marriage to Soranzo?

PHILOTIS.

In marriage to Soranzo? Yes, for certain.

RICHARDETTO.

20But how find you young Annabella’s love
21Inclin’d to him?

PHILOTIS.

Inclin’d to him? For aught I could perceive,
22She neither fancies him or any else.

RICHARDETTO.

23There’s mystery in that which time must show.
24She us’d you kindly?

PHILOTIS.

She us’d you kindly? Yes.

RICHARDETTO.

She us’d you kindly? Yes. And crav’d your company?

PHILOTIS.

25Often.

RICHARDETTO.

Often. ’Tis well: it goes as I could wish.
26I am the doctor now, and as for you,
27None knows you; if all fail not, we shall thrive.
28But who comes here?

Enter Grimaldi.

But who comes here? I know him: ’tis Grimaldi,
29A Roman and a soldier, near allies
30Unto the duke of Montferrato, one
31Attending on the nuncio of the pope
32That now resides in Parma, by which means
33He hopes to get the love of Annabella.

GRIMALDI.

34Save you, sir.

RICHARDETTO.

Save you, sir. And you, sir.

GRIMALDI.

Save you, sir. And you, sir. I have heard
35Of your approv’d skill, which through the city
36Is freely talk’d of, and would crave your aid.

RICHARDETTO.

37For what, sir?

GRIMALDI.

For what, sir? Marry, sir, for this–
38But I would speak in private.

RICHARDETTO.

But I would speak in private. Leave us, cousin.

Exit Philotis.

GRIMALDI.

39I love fair Annabella, and would know
40Whether in art there may not be receipts
41To move affection.

RICHARDETTO.

To move affection. Sir, perhaps there may,
42But these will nothing profit you.

GRIMALDI.

But these will nothing profit you. Not me?

RICHARDETTO.

43Unless I be mistook, you are a man
44Greatly in favour with the cardinal.

GRIMALDI.

45What of that?

RICHARDETTO.

What of that? In duty to his grace,
46I will be bold to tell you, if you seek
47To marry Florio’s daughter, you must first
48Remove a bar ’twixt you and her.

GRIMALDI.

Remove a bar ’twixt you and her. Who’s that?

RICHARDETTO.

49Soranzo is the man that hath her heart,
50And while he lives, be sure you cannot speed.

GRIMALDI.

51Soranzo! What, mine enemy! Is’t he?

RICHARDETTO.

52Is he your enemy?

GRIMALDI.

Is he your enemy? The man I hate
53Worse than confusion–
54I’ll kill him straight.

RICHARDETTO.

I’ll kill him straight. Nay then, take mine advice,
55Even for his grace’s sake, the cardinal:
56I’ll find a time when he and she do meet,
57Of which I’ll give you notice, and to be sure
58He shall not ’scape, I’ll provide a poison
59To dip your rapier’s point in; if he had
60As many heads as Hydra had, he dies.

GRIMALDI.

61But shall I trust thee, doctor?

RICHARDETTO.

But shall I trust thee, doctor? As yourself;
62Thus shall the fates decree:aparte
63By me Soranzo falls, that ruin’d me.aparte

Exeunt.

[II.iv]

Enter Donado, Bergetto, and Poggio.

DONADO.

Well, sir, I must be content to be both you secretary and your messenger myself. I cannot tell what this letter may work, but as sure as I am alive, if thou come once to talk with her, I fear thou wilt mar whatsoever I make.

BERGETTO.

You make, uncle? Why, am not I big enough to carry mine own letter, I pray?

DONADO.

Ay, ay, carry a fool’s head o’ thy own! Why, thou dunce, wouldst thou write a letter and carry it thyself?

BERGETTO.

Yes, that I would, and read it to her with my own mouth; for you must think, if she will not believe me myself when she hears me speak, she will not believe another’s hand- writing. O, you think I am a blockhead, uncle! No, sir, Poggio knows I have indited a letter myself, so I have.

POGGIO.

Yes, truly, sir; I have it in my pocket.

DONADO.

A sweet one, no doubt; pray let’s see’t.

BERGETTO.

I cannot read my own hand very well, Poggio: read it, Poggio.

DONADO.

Begin.

POGGIO.

(reads). “Most dainty and honey-sweet mistress, I could call you fair, and lie as fast as any that loves you, but my uncle being the elder man, I leave it to him, as more fit for his age and the color of his beard. I am wise enough to tell you I can bourd where I see occasion; or if you like my uncle’s wit better than mine, you shall marry me; if you like mine better than this, I will marry you in spite of your teeth. So commending my best parts to you, I rest– Yours upwards and downwards, or you may choose, Bergetto.”

BERGETTO.

Aha, here’s stuff, uncle.

DONADO.

Here’s stuff indeed to shame us all. Pray whose advice did you take in this learned letter?

POGGIO.

None, upon my word, but mine own.

BERGETTO.

And mine, uncle, believe it, nobody’s else; ’twas mine own brain, I thank a good wit for’t.

DONADO.

Get you home, sir, and look keep within doors till I return.

BERGETTO.

How! That were a jest indeed; I scorn it i’faith.

DONADO.

What! You do not?

BERGETTO.

Judge me, but I do now.

POGGIO.

Indeed, sir, ’tis very unhealthy.

DONADO.

Well, sir, if I hear any of your apish running to motions and fopperies, till I come back, you were as good not; look to’t.

Exit Donado.

BERGETTO.

Poggio, shall’s steal to see this horse with the head in’s tail?

POGGIO.

Ay, but you must take heed of whipping.

BERGETTO.

Dost take me for a child, Poggio? Come, honest Poogio.

Exeunt.

[II.v]

Enter Friar and Giovanni.

FRIAR.

1Peace! Thou hast told a tale, whose every word
2Threatens eternal slaughter to the soul.
3I’m sorry I have heard it; would mine ears
4Had been one minute deaf, before the hour
5That thou cam’st to me. O young man castaway,
6By the religious number of mine order,
7I day and night have wak’d my aged eyes,
8Above my strength, to weep on thy behalf:
9But Heaven is angry, and be thou resolv’d,
10Thou art a man remark’d to taste a mischief:
11Look for’t; though it come late, it will come sure.

GIOVANNI.

12Father, in this you are uncharitable;
13What I have done, I’ll prove both fit and good.
14It is a principle, which you have taught
15When I was yet your scholar, that the frame
16And composition of the mind doth follow
17The frame and composition of the body:
18So where the body’s furniture is beauty,
19The mind’s must needs be virtue; which allowed,
20Virtue itself is reason but refin’d,
21And love the quintessence of that. This proves
22My sister’s beauty being rarely fair
23Is rarely virtuous; chiefly in her love,
24And chiefly in that love, her love to me.
25If hers to me, then so is mine to her;
26Since in like causes are effects alike.

FRIAR.

27O ignorance in knowledge! Long ago,
28How often have I warn’d thee this before?
29Indeed, if we were sure there were no deity,
30Nor Heaven nor hell, then to be led alone
31By nature’s light, as were philosophers
32Of elders times, might instance some defense.
33But ’tis not so; then, madmam, thou wilt find
34That nature is in Heaven’s positions blind.

GIOVANNI.

35Your age o’errules you; had you youth like mine,
36You’d make her love your Heaven, and her divine.

FRIAR.

37Nay then, I see thou’rt too far sold to hell,
38It lies not in the compass of my prayers
39To call thee back; yet let me counsel thee:
40Persuade thy sister to some marriage.

GIOVANNI.

41Marriage? Why, that’s to damn her! That’s to prove
42Her greedy of variety of lust.

FRIAR.

43O fearful! If thou wilt not, give me leave
44To shrive her, lest she should die unabsolv’d.

GIOVANNI.

45At your best leisure, father: then she’ll tell you
46How dearly she doth prize my matchless love.
47Then you will know what pity ’twere we two
48Should have been sunder’d from each other’s arms.
49View well her face, and in that little round
50You may observe a world of variety:
51For color, lips: for sweet perfumes, her breath;
52For jewels, eyes; for threads of purest gold,
53Hair; for delicious choice of flowers, cheeks;
54Wonder in every portion of that throne:
55Hear her but speak, and you will swear the spheres
56Make music to the citizens in Heaven.
57But, father, what is else for pleasure fram’d,
58Lest I offend your ears, shall go unnam’d.

FRIAR.

59The more I hear, I pity thee the more,
60That one so excellent should give those parts
61All to a second death; what I can do
62Is but to pray: and yet I could advise thee,
63Wouldst thou be rul’d.

GIOVANNI.

Wouldst thou be rul’d. In what?

FRIAR.

Wouldst thou be rul’d. In what? Why, leave her yet;
64The throne of mercy is above your trespass,
65Yet time is left you both –

GIOVANNI.

Yet time is left you both – To embrace each other,
66Else let all time be struck quite out of number.
67She is like me, and I like her, resolv’d.

FRIAR.

68No more! I’ll visit her; this grieves me most,
69Things being thus, a pair of souls are lost.

Exeunt.

[II.vi]

Enter Florio, Donado, Annabella, Putana.

FLORIO.

1Where’s Giovanni?

ANNABELLA.

Where’s Giovanni? Newly walk’d abroad,
2And, as I heard him say, gone to the friar,
3His reverend tutor.

FLORIO.

His reverend tutor. That’s a blessed man,
4A man made up of holiness; I hope
5He’ll teach him how to gain another world.

DONADO.

6Fair gentlewoman, here’s a letter sent
7To you from my young cousin; I dare swear
8He loves you in his soul: would you could hear
9Sometimes what I see daily, sighs and tears,
10As if his breast were prison to his heart.

FLORIO.

11Receive it, Annabella.

ANNABELLA.

12Alas, good man.

DONADO.

What’s that she said?

PUTANA.

An’t please you, sir, she said, “Alas, good man.” Truly I do commend him to her every night before her first sleep, because I would have her dream of him, and she hear- kens to that most religiously.

DONADO.

Say’st so? God a-mercy, Putana, there’s something for thee [gives her money] , and prithee do what thou canst on his behalf; sha’ not be lost labor, take my word for’t.

PUTANA.

Thank you most heartily, sir; now I have a feeling of your mind, let me alone to work.

ANNABELLA.

Guardian!

PUTANA.

Did you call?

ANNABELLA.

Keep this letter.

DONADO.

Signor Florio, in any case bid her read it instantly.

FLORIO.

26Keep it for what? Pray read it me hereright.

ANNABELLA.

27I shall, sir.

She reads.

DONADO.

How d’ee find her inclin’d, signor?

FLORIO.

29Troth, sir, I know not how; not all so well
30As I could wish.

ANNABELLA.

31Sir, I am bound to rest your cousin’s debtor.
32The jewel I’ll return; for if he love,
33I’ll count that love a jewel.

DONADO.

I’ll count that love a jewel. Mark you that?
34Nay, keep them both, sweet maid.

ANNABELLA.

Nay, keep them both, sweet maid. You must excuse me;
35Indeed I will not keep it.

FLORIO.

Indeed I will not keep it. Where’s the ring,
36That which you mother in her will bequeath’d,
37And charg’d you on her blessing not to give’t
38To any but your husband? Send back that.

ANNABELLA.

39I have it not.

FLORIO.

I have it not. Ha, have it not! Where is’t?

ANNABELLA.

40My brother in the morning took it from me,
41Said he would wear’t today.

FLORIO.

Said he would wear’t today. Well, what do you say
42To young Bergetto’s love? Are you content
43To match with him? Speak.

DONADO.

To match with him? Speak. There’s the point indeed.

ANNABELLA.

44What shall I do? I must say something now.aparte

FLORIO.

45What say? Why d’ee not speak?

ANNABELLA.

What say? Why d’ee not speak? Sir, with your leave,
46Please you to give me freedom?

FLORIO.

Please you to give me freedom? Yes, you have it.

ANNABELLA.

47Signor Donado, if your nephew mean
48To raise his better fortunes in his match,
49The hope of me will hinder such a hope;
50Sir, if you love him, as I know you do,
51Find one more worthy of his choice than me.
52In short, I’m sure I sha’ not be his wife.

DONADO.

53Why, here’s plain dealing, I commend thee for’t,
54And all the worst I wish thee is, Heaven bless thee!
55Your father yet and I will still friends,
56Shall we not, Signor Florio?

FLORIO.

Shall we not, Signor Florio? Yes, why not?
57Look, here your cousin comes.

Enter Bergetto and Poggio.

DONADO.

O coxcomb, what doth he make here?

BERGETTO.

Where’s my uncle, sirs?

DONADO.

What’s the news now?

BERGETTO.

Save you, uncle, save you! You must not think I come for nothing, masters: and how, and how is’t? What, you have read my letter? Ah, there I – tickled you i’faith!

POGGIO.

But ’twere better you had tickled her in another place.

BERGETTO.

Sirrah sweetheart, I’ll tell thee a good jest; and riddle what ’tis.

ANNABELLA.

You say you’d tell me.

BERGETTO.

As I was walking just now in the street, I met a swaggering fellow would needs take the wall of me, and because he did thrust me, I very valiantly call’d him rogue. He here- upon bade me draw: I told him I had more wit than so, but when he saw that I would not, he did so maul me with the hilts of his rapier that my head sung whilst my feet caper’d in the kennel.

DONADO.

Was ever the like ass seen?

ANNABELLA.

And what did you all this while?

BERGETTO.

Laugh at him for a gull, till I see the blood run about mine ears, and then I could not choose but find in my heart to cry; till a fellow with a broad beard – they say he is a new-come doctor – call’d me into his house, and gave me a plaster – look you, here ’tis – and, sir, there was a young wench wash’d my face and hands most excellently, i’faith, I shall love her as long as I live for’t, did she not, Poggio?

POGGIO.

Yes, and kiss’d him too.

BERGETTO.

Why, la now, you think I tell a lie, uncle, I warrant.

DONADO.

Would he that beat thy blood out of thy head had beaten some wit into it; for I fear thou never wilt have any.

BERGETTO.

O, uncle, but there was a wench would have done a man’s heart good to have look’d on her –by this light she had a face methinks worth twenty of you, Mistress Annabella.

DONADO.

Was ever such a fool born?

ANNABELLA.

I am glad she lik’d you, sir.

BERGETTO.

Are you so? By my troth I thank you, forsooth.

FLORIO.

Sure ’twas the doctor’s niece, that was last day with us here.

BERGETTO.

’Twas she, ’twas she.

DONADO.

How do you know that, simplicity?

BERGETTO.

Why, does not he say so? If I should have said no, I should have given him the lie, uncle, and so have deserv’d a dry beating again; I’ll none of that.

FLORIO.

101A very modest well-behav’d young maid
102As I have seen.

DONADO.

As I have seen. Is she indeed?

FLORIO.

As I have seen. Is she indeed? Indeed
103She is, if I have any judgment.

DONADO.

Well, sir, now you are free, you need not care for sending letters: now you are dismiss’d, your mistress here will none of you.

BERGETTO.

No? Why, what care I for that? I can have wenches enough in Parma for a half-a-crown apiece, cannot I, Poggio?

POGGIO.

I’ll warrant you, sir.

DONADO.

Signor Florio,

111I thank you for your free recourse you gave
112For my admittance; and to you, fair maid,
113That jewel I will give you ’gainst your marriage.

Come, will you go, sir?

BERGETTO.

Ay, marry will I. Mistress, farewell, mistress: I’ll come again tomorrow. Farewell, mistress.

Exit Donado, Bergetto, and Poggio.

Enter Giovanni.

FLORIO.

117Son, where have you been? What, alone, alone still?
118I would not have it so, you must forsake
119This over-bookish humor. Well, you sister
120Hath shook the fool off.

GIOVANNI.

Hath shook the fool off. ’Twas no match for her.

FLORIO.

121’Twas not indeed, I meant it nothing less;
122Soranzo is the man I only like–
123Look on him, Annabella. Come, ’tis supper-time,
124And it grows late.

Exit Florio.

GIOVANNI.

125Whose jewel’s that?

ANNABELLA.

Whose jewel’s that? Some sweetheart’s.

GIOVANNI.

Whose jewel’s that? Some sweetheart’s. So I think.

ANNABELLA.

126A lusty youth,
127Signor Donado, gave it me to wear
128Against my marriage.

GIOVANNI.

Against my marriage. But you shall not wear it:
129Send it him back again.

ANNABELLA.

Send it him back again. What, you are jealous?

GIOVANNI.

130That you shall know anon, at better leisure.
131Welcome, sweet night! The evening crowns the day.

Exeunt.
