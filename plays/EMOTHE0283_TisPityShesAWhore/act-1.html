[I.i]

Enter Friar and Giovanni.

FRIAR.

1Dispute no more in this, for know, young man,
2These are no school-points; nice philosophy
3May tolerate unlikely arguments,
4But Heaven admits no jest: wits that presum’d
5On wit too much, by striving how to prove
6There was no God, with foolish grounds of art,
7Discover’d first the nearest way to hell,
8And fill’d the world with devilish atheism.
9Such questions, youth, are fond; for better ’tis
10To bless the sun than reason why it shines,
11Yet He thou talk’st of is above the sun.
12No more; I may not hear it.

GIOVANNI.

No more; I may not hear it. Gentle father,
13To you I have unclasp’d my burdened soul,
14Emptied the storehouse of my thoughts and heart,
15Made myself poor of secrets; have not left
16Another word untold, which hath not spoke
17All what I ever durst or think or know;
18And yet is here the comfort I shall have,
19Must I not do what all men else may, love?

FRIAR.

20Yes, you may love, fair son.

GIOVANNI.

Yes, you may love, fair son. Must I not praise
21That beauty which, if fram’d anew, the gods
22Would make a god of, if they had it there,
23And kneel to it, as I do kneel to them?

FRIAR.

24Why, foolish madman–

GIOVANNI.

Why, foolish madman– Shall a peevish sound,
25A customary form, from man to man,
26Of brother and of sister, be a bar
27’Twixt my perpetual happiness and me?
28Say that we had one father, say one womb
29(Curse to my joys) gave both us life and birth;
30Are we not therefore each to other bound
31So much the more by nature, by the links
32Of blood, of reason- nay, if you will have’t,
33Even of religion –to be ever one,
34One soul, one flesh, one love, one heart, one all?

FRIAR.

35Have done, unhappy youth, for thou art lost.

GIOVANNI.

36Shall then, for that I am her brother born,
37My joys be ever banish’d from her bed?
38No, father; in your eyes I see the change
39Of pity and compassion; from your age,
40As from a sacred oracle, distils
41The life of counsel: tell me, holy man
42What cure shall give me ease in these extremes.

FRIAR.

43Repentance, son, and sorrow for this sin:
44For thou hast mov’d a Majesty above
45With thy unranged-almost blasphemy.

GIOVANNI.

46O do not speak of that, dear confessor!

FRIAR.

47Art thou, my son, that miracle of wit
48Who once, within these three months, wert esteem’d
49A wonder of thine age, throughout Bononia?
50How did the University applaud
51Thy government, behaviour, learning, speech,
52Sweetness, and all that could make up a man!
53I was proud of my tutelage, and chose
54Rather to leave my books than part with thee.
55I did so: but the fruits of all my hopes
56Are lost in thee, as thou art in thyself.
57O, Giovanni, hast thou left the schools
58Of knowledge to converse with lust and death?
59For death waits on thy lust. Look through the world,
60And thou shalt see a thousand faces shine
61More glorious than this idol thou ador’st:
62Leave her, and take thy choice, ’tis much less sin,
63Though in such games as those they lose that win.

GIOVANNI.

64It were more ease to stop the ocean
65From floats and ebbs than to dissuade my vows.

FRIAR.

66Then I have done, and in thy wilful flames
67Already see thy ruin; Heaven is just.
68Yet hear my counsel.

GIOVANNI.

Yet hear my counsel. As a voice of life.

FRIAR.

69Hie to thy father’s house, there lock thee fast
70Alone within thy chamber, then fall down
71On both thy knees, and grovel on the ground:
72Cry to thy heart, wash every word thou utter’st
73In tears, and (if’t be possible) of blood:
74Beg Heaven to cleanse the leprosy of lust
75That rots thy soul, acknowledge what thou art,
76A wretch, a worm, a nothing: weep, sigh, pray
77Three times a day, and three times every night.
78For seven days’ space do this, then if thou find’st
79No change in thy desires, return to me:
80I’ll think on remedy. Pray for thyself
81At home, whilst I pray for thee here. –Away,
82My blessing with thee, we have need to pray.

GIOVANNI.

83All this I’ll do, to free me from the rod
84Of vengeance; else I’ll swear my fate’s my god.

Exeunt.

[I.ii]

Enter Grimaldi and Vasques ready to fight.

VASQUES.

Come, sir, stand to your tackling; if you prove craven, I’ll make you run quickly.

GRIMALDI.

Thou art no equal match for me.

VASQUES.

Indeed I never went to the wars to bring home news, nor cannot play the mountebank for a meal’s meat, and swear I got my wounds in the field. See you these grey hairs? They’ll not flinch for a bloody nose. Wilt thou to this gear?

GRIMALDI.

Why, slave, think’st thou I’ll balance my reputation with a cast-suit? Call thy master, he shall know that I dare–

VASQUES.

Scold like a cot-quean, that’s your profession. Thou poor shadow of a soldier, I will make thee know my master keeps servants thy betters in quality and performance. Com’st thou to fight or prate?

GRIMALDI.

Neither, with thee. I am a Roman and a gentleman, one that have got mine honor with expense of blood.

VASQUES.

You are a lying coward and a fool; fight, or by these hilts I’ll kill thee. –Brave my lord! You’ll fight?

GRIMALDI.

Provoke me not, for if thou dost–

VASQUES.

Have at you!

They fight, Grimaldi hath the worst.

Enter Florio, Donado, Soranzo.

FLORIO.

20What mean these sudden broils so near my doors?
21Have you not other places but my house
22To vent the spleen of your disordered bloods?
23Must I be haunted still with such unrest
24As not to eat or sleep in peace at home?
25Is this your love, Grimaldi? Fie, ’tis naught.

DONADO.

26And Vasques, I may tell thee, ’tis not well
27To broach these quarrels; you are ever forward
28In seconding contentions.

Enter above Annabella and Putana.

FLORIO.

In seconding contentions. What’s the ground?

SORANZO.

29That, with your patience, signors, I’ll resolve:
30This gentleman, whom fame reports a soldier,
31(for else I know not) rivals me in love
32To Signor Florio’s daughter, to whose ears
33He still prefers his suit, to my disgrace,
34Thinking the way to recommend himself
35Is to disparage me in his report.
36But now, Grimaldi, though, may be, thou art
37My equal in thy blood, yet this bewrays
38A lowness in thy mind which, wert thou noble,
39Thou wouldst as much disdain as I do thee
40For this unworthiness; and on this ground
41I will’d my servant to correct his tongue,
42Holding a man so base no match for me.

VASQUES.

And had not your sudden coming prevented us, I had let my gentleman blood under the gills; I should have worm’d you, sir, for running mad.

GRIMALDI.

I’ll be reveng’d, Soranzo.

VASQUES.

On a dish of warm broth to stay your stomach –do, honest innocence, do; spoon-meat is a wholesomer diet than a Spanish blade.

GRIMALDI.

Remember this!

SORANZO.

I fear thee not, Grimaldi.

Exit Grimaldi.

FLORIO.

52My Lord Soranzo, this is strange to me,
53Why you should storm, having my word engag’d:
54Owing her heart, what need you doubt her ear?
55Losers may talk by law of any game.

VASQUES.

Yet the villainy of words, Signor Florio, may be such as would make any unspleen’d dove choleric. Blame not my lord in this.

FLORIO.

59Be you more silent.
60I would not for my wealth my daughter’s love,
61Should cause the spilling of one drop of blood.
62Vasques, put up, let’s end this fray in wine.

Exeunt [Florio, Donado, Soranzo and Vasques].

PUTANA.

How like you this, child? Here’s threat’ning, challenging, quarrelling, and fighting, on every side, and all is for your sake; you had need look to yourself, charge, you’ll be stol’n away sleeping else shortly.

ANNABELLA.

67But tut’ress, such a life gives no content
68To me, my thoughts are fix’d on other ends;
69Would you would leave me.

PUTANA.

Leave you? No marvel else. Leave me no leaving, charge; this is love outright. Indeed I blame you not, you have choice fit for the best lady in Italy.

ANNABELLA.

Pray do not talk so much.

PUTANA.

Take the worst with the best, there’s Grimaldi the soldier, a very well-timber’d fellow: they say he is a Roman, nephew to the Duke Montferrato, they say he did good service in the wars against the Milanese, but ’faith, charge, I do not like him, an’t be for nothing but for being a soldier; not one amongst twenty of your skirmishing captains but have some privy maim or other that mars their standing upright. I like him the worse, he crinkles so much in the hams; though he might serve if there were no more men, yet he’s not the man I would choose.

ANNABELLA.

Fie, how thou prat’st.

PUTANA.

As I am a very woman, I like Signor Soranzo well; he is wise, and what is more, rich; and what is more than that, kind, and what is more than all this, a nobleman; such a one, were I the fair Annabella myself, I would wish and pray for. Then he is bountiful; besides, he is handsome, and by my troth, I think wholesome (and that’s news in a gallant of three and twenty); liberal, that I know; loving, that you know; and a man sure, else he could never ha’ purchas’d such a good name with Hippolita, the lusty widow, in her husband’s lifetime: and ’twere but for that report, sweet- heart, would ’a were thine. Commend a man for his qualities, but take a husband as he is a plain-sufficient, naked man: such a one is for your bed, and such a one is Signor Soranzo, my life for’t.

ANNABELLA.

Sure the woman took her morning’s draught too soon.

Enter Bergetto and Poggio.

PUTANA.

But look, sweetheart, look what thing comes now: here’s another of your ciphers to fill up the number. O brave old ape in a silken coat! Observe.

BERGETTO.

Didst thou think, Poggio, that I would spoil my new clothes, and leave my dinner, to fight?

POGGIO.

No, sir, I did not take you for so arrant a baby.

BERGETTO.

I am wiser than so: for I hope, Poggio, thou never heardst of an elder brother that was a coxcomb. Didst, Poggio?

POGGIO.

Never indeed, sir, as long as they had either land or money left them to inherit.

BERGETTO.

Is it possible, Poggio? O monstrous! Why, I’ll undertake with a handful of silver to buy a headful of wit at any time; but sirrah, I have another purchase in hand, I shall have the wench, mine uncle says. I will but wash my face, and shift socks, and then have at her i’faith! Mark my pace, Poggio.

[Walks affectedly.]

POGGIO.

I have seen an ass and a mule trot the Spanish pavin with a better grace, I know not how often.

Exeunt [Bergetto and Poggio].

ANNABELLA.

This idiot haunts me too.

PUTANA.

Ay, ay, he needs no description; the rich magnifico that is below with your father, charge, Signor Donado his uncle, for that he means to make this his cousin a golden calf, thinks that you will be a right Israelite and fall down to him presently: but I hope I have tutor’d you better. They say a fool’s bauble is a lady’s playfellow, yet you having wealth enough, you need not cast upon the dearth of flesh at any rate: hang him, innocent!

Enter Giovanni.

ANNABELLA.

126But see, Putana, see: what blessed shape
127Of some celestial creature now appears?
128What man is he, that with such sad aspect
129Walks careless of himself?

PUTANA.

Walks careless of himself? Where?

ANNABELLA.

Walks careless of himself? Where? Look below.

PUTANA.

130O, ’tis your brother, sweet.

ANNABELLA.

O, ’tis your brother, sweet. Ha!

PUTANA.

O, ’tis your brother, sweet. Ha! ’Tis your brother.

ANNABELLA.

131Sure ’tis not he: this is some woeful thing
132Wrapp’d up in grief, some shadow of a man.
133Alas, he beats his breast, and wipes his eyes
134Drown’d all in tears: methinks I hear him sigh.
135Let’s down, Putana, and partake the cause;
136I know my brother, in the love he bears me,
137Will not deny me partage in his sadness.
138My soul is full of heaviness and fear.aparte

Exit [with Putana].

GIOVANNI.

139Lost, I am lost: my fates have doom’d my death.
140The more I strive, I love; the more I love,
141The less I hope: I see my ruin certain.
142What judgment or endeavors could apply
143To my incurable and restless wounds
144I thoroughly have examin’d, but in vain:
145O that it were not in religion sin
146To make our love a god and worship it!
147I have even wearied Heaven with prayers, dried up
148The spring of my continual tears, even starv’d
149My veins with daily fasts: what wit or art
150Could counsel, I have practic’d; but alas,
151I find all these but dreams and old men’s tales
152To fright unsteady youth; I’m still the same.
153Or I must speak, or burst; ’tis not, I know,
154My lust, but ’tis my fate that leads me on.
155Keep fear and low faint-hearted shame with slaves;
156I’ll tell her that I love her, though my heart
157Were rated at the price of that attempt.
158O me! She comes.

Enter Annabella and Putana.

ANNABELLA.

O me! She comes. Brother!

GIOVANNI.

O me! She comes. Brother! If such a thingaparte
159As courage dwell in men, ye heavenly powers,aparte
160Now double all that virtue in my tongue.aparte

ANNABELLA.

Why, brother, will you not speak to me?

GIOVANNI.

Yes; how d’ee, sister?

ANNABELLA.

Howsoever I am, methinks you are not well.

PUTANA.

Bless us, why are you so sad, sir?

GIOVANNI.

Let me entreat you, leave us a while, Putana. Sister, I would be private with you.

ANNABELLA.

Withdraw, Putana.

PUTANA.

If this were any other company for her, I should think my absence an office of some credit; but I will leave them together.

Exit Putana.

GIOVANNI.

Come, sister, lend your hand, let’s walk together.

173I hope you need not blush to walk with me;
174Here’s none but you and I.

ANNABELLA.

How’s this?

GIOVANNI.

Faith, I mean no harm.

ANNABELLA.

Harm?

GIOVANNI.

No, good faith; how is’t with ’ee?

ANNABELLA.

I trust he be not frantic.

GIOVANNI.

180Trust me, but I am sick, I fear so sick
181’Twill cost my life.

ANNABELLA.

182Mercy forbid it! ’Tis not so, I hope.

GIOVANNI.

I think you love me, sister.

ANNABELLA.

Yes, you know I do.

GIOVANNI.

I know’t indeed. –Y’are very fair.

ANNABELLA.

Nay then, I see you have a merry sickness.

GIOVANNI.

187That’s as it proves. The poets feign, I read,
188That Juno for her forehead did exceed
189All other goddesses: but I durst swear
190Your forehead exceeds hers, as hers did theirs.

ANNABELLA.

191Troth, this is pretty!

GIOVANNI.

Troth, this is pretty! Such a pair of stars
192As are thine eyes would, like Promethean fire,
193If gently glanc’d, give life to senseless stones.

ANNABELLA.

Fie upon ‘ee!

GIOVANNI.

195The lily and the rose, most sweetly strange,
196Upon your dimpled cheeks do strive for change.
197Such lips would tempt a saint; such hands as those
198Would make an anchorite lascivious.

ANNABELLA.

D’ee mock me or flatter me?

GIOVANNI.

200If you would see a beauty more exact
201Than art can counterfeit or nature frame,
202Look in your glass and there behold your own.

ANNABELLA.

O you are a trim youth!

GIOVANNI.

Here.

Offers his dagger to her.

ANNABELLA.

What to do?

GIOVANNI.

–And here’s my breast, strike home.

207Rip up my bosom, there thou shalt behold
208A heart in which is writ the truth I speak.
209Why stand ‘ee?

ANNABELLA.

Why stand ‘ee? Are you earnest?

GIOVANNI.

Why stand ‘ee? Are you earnest? Yes, most earnest.
210You cannot love?

ANNABELLA.

You cannot love? Whom?

GIOVANNI.

You cannot love? Whom? Me. My tortur’d soul
211Hath felt affliction in the heat of death.
212O Annabella, I am quite undone:
213The love of thee, my sister, and the view
214Of thy immortal beauty hath untun’d
215All harmony both of my rest and life.
216Why d’ee not srike?

ANNABELLA.

Why d’ee not srike? Forbid it, my just fears!
217If this be true, ’twere fitter I were dead.

GIOVANNI.

218True, Annabella; ’tis no time to jest.
219I have too long suppress’d the hidden flames
220That almost have consum’d me; I have spent
221Many a silent night in sighs and groans,
222Ran over all my thoughts, despis’d my fate,
223Reason’d against the reasons of my love,
224Done all that smooth-cheek’d virtue could advise,
225But found all bootles; ’tis my destiny
226That you must either love, or I must die.

ANNABELLA.

227Comes this in sadness from you?

GIOVANNI.

Comes this in sadness from you? Let some mischief
228Befall me soon, if I dissemble aught.

ANNABELLA.

229You are my brother Giovanni.

GIOVANNI.

You are my brother Giovanni. You
230My sister Annabella; I know this:
231And could afford you instance why to love
232So much the more for this; to which intent
233Wise nature first in your creation meant
234To make you mine; else’t had been sin and foul
235To share one beauty to a double soul.
236Nearness in birth or blood doth but persuade
237A nearer nearness in affection.
238I have ask’d counsel of the holy church,
239Who tells me I may love you, and ’tis just
240That since I may, I should; and will, yes, will:
241Must I now live, or die?

ANNABELLA.

Must I now live, or die? Live: thou hast won
242The field, and never fought; what thou hast urg’d
243My captive heart had long ago resolv’d.
244I blush to tell thee –but I’ll tell thee now–
245For every sigh that thou hast spent for me
246I have sigh’d ten; for every tear shed twenty:
247And not so much for that I lov’d, as that
248I durst not say I lov’d, nor scarcely think it.

GIOVANNI.

249Let not this music be a dream, ye gods,
250For pity’s sake, I beg ‘ee!

ANNABELLA.

For pity’s sake, I beg ‘ee! On my knees,

She kneels.

251Brother, even by our mother’s dust, I charge you,
252Do not betray me to your mirth or hate,
253Love me or kill me, brother.

GIOVANNI.

Love me or kill me, brother. On my knees.

He kneels.

254Sister, even by my mother’s dust, I charge you,
255Do not betray me to your mirth or hate,
256Love me or kill me, sister.

ANNABELLA.

257You mean good sooth then?

GIOVANNI.

You mean good sooth then? In good troth I do,
258And so do you, I hope: say, I’m in earnest.

ANNABELLA.

259I’ll swear’t, I.

GIOVANNI.

I’ll swear’t, I. And I, and by this kiss,

Kisses her.

260(Once more, yet once more; now let’s rise by this),

[They rise.]

261I would not change this minute for Elysium.
262What must we now do?

ANNABELLA.

What must we now do? What you will.

GIOVANNI.

What must we now do? What you will. Come then,
263After so many tears as we have wept,
264Let’s learn to court in smiles, to kiss, and sleep.

Exeunt.

[I.iii]

Enter Florio and Donado.

FLORIO.

1Signor Donado, you have said enough,
2I understand you; but would have you know
3I will not force my daughter ‘gainst her will.
4You see I have but two, a son and her;
5And he is so devoted to his book,
6As I must tell you true, I doubt his health:
7Should he miscarry, all my hopes rely
8Upon my girl; as for worldly fortune,
9I am, I thank my stars, blest with enough.
10My care is how to match her to her liking:
11I would not have her marry wealth, but love,
12And if she like your nephew, let him have her,
13Here’s all that I can say.

DONADO.

Here’s all that I can say. Sir, you say well,
14Like a true father, and for my part, I,
15If the young folks can like (’twixt you and me),
16Will promise to assure my nephew presently
17Three thousand florins yearly during life,
18And after I am dead, my whole estate.

FLORIO.

19’Tis a fair proffer, sir; meantime your nephew
20Shall have free passage to commence his suit:
21If he can thrive, he shall have my consent.
22So for this time I’ll leave you, signor.

Exit.

DONADO.

So for this time I’ll leave you, signor. Well,
23Here’s hope yet, if my nephew would have wit:
24But he is such another dunce, I fear
25He’ll never win the wench. When I was young
26I could have done’t, i’faith, and so shall he
27If he will learn of me; and in good time
28He comes himself.

Enter Bergetto and Poggio.

How now, Bergetto, whither away so fast?

BERGETTO.

O uncle, I have heard the strangest news that ever came out of the mint, have I not, Poggio?

POGGIO.

Yes indeed, sir.

DONADO.

What news, Bergetto?

BERGETTO.

Why, look ye, uncle, my barber told me just now that there is a fellow come to town who undertakes to make a mill go without the mortal help of any water or wind, only with sand-bags: and this fellow hath a strange horse, a most excellent beast, I’ll assure you, uncle (my barber says), whose head, to the wonder of all Christian people, stands just behind where his tail is; is’t not true, Poggio?

POGGIO.

So the barber swore, forsooth.

DONADO.

And you are running thither?

BERGETTO.

Ay forsooth, uncle.

DONADO.

Wilt thou be a fool still? Come, sir, you shall not go: you have more mind of a puppet-play than on the business I told ye; why, thou great baby, wilt never have wit, wilt make thyself a may-game to all the world?

POGGIO.

Answer for yourself, master.

BERGETTO.

Why, uncle, should I sit at home still, and not go abroad to see fashions like other gallants?

DONADO.

To see hobby-horses! What wise talk, I pray, had you with Annabella, when you were at Signor Florio’s house?

BERGETTO.

O, the wench! Uds sa’ me, uncle, I tickled her with a rare speech, that I make her almost burst her belly with laughing.

DONADO.

Nay, I think so, and what speech was’t?

BERGETTO.

What did I say, Poggio?

POGGIO.

Forsooth, my master said that he loved her almost as well as he loved parmasent, and swore (I’ll be sworn for him) that she wanted but such a nose as his was to be as pretty a young woman as any was in Parma.

DONADO.

O gross!

BERGETTO.

Nay, uncle, then she ask’d me whether my father had any more children than myself: and I said, “No, ’twere better he should have had his brains knock’d out first.”

DONADO.

This is intolerable.

BERGETTO.

Then said she, “Will Signor Donado your uncle leave you all his wealth?”

DONADO.

Ha! that was good, did she harp upon that string?

BERGETTO.

Did she harp upon that string? Ay, that she did. I answered, “Leave me all his wealth? Why, woman, he hath no other wit; if he had, he should hear on’t to his everlasting glory and confusion: I know,” quoth I, “I am his white boy, and will not be gull’d”; and with that she fell into a great smile and went away. Nay, I did fit her.

DONADO.

Ah, sirrah, then I see there is no changing of nature. Well, Bergetto, I fear thou wilt be a very ass still.

BERGETTO.

I should be sorry for that, uncle.

DONADO.

Come, come you home with me; since you are no better a speaker, I’ll have you write to her after some courtly manner, and enclose some rich jewel in the letter.

BERGETTO.

Ay marry, that will be excellent.

DONADO.

Peace, innocent.

83Once in my time I’ll set my wits to school,
84If all fail, ’tis but the fortune of a fool.

BERGETTO.

Poggio, ’twill do, Poggio.

Exeunt.
