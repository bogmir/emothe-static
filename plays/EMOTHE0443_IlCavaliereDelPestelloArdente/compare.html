[Induction]

[Several Gentlemen sitting on stools upon the stage; the Citizen, his Wife, and Rafe sitting below among the audience.]

Enter Prologue.

[Prologue]

1From all that's near the court, from all that's great
2Within the compass of the city walls,
3We now have brought our scene —

Enter Citizen[, climbing to the stage].

Citizen

4Hold your peace, goodman boy.

Prologue

5What do you mean, sir?

Citizen

6That you have no good meaning. This seven years there hath been plays at this house, I have observed it, you have still girds at citizens; and now you call your play The London Merchant. Down with your title, boy, down with your title.

Prologue

7Are you a member of the noble city?

Citizen

8I am.

Prologue

9And a freeman?

Citizen

10Yea, and a grocer.

Prologue

11So, grocer, then, by your sweet favour, we intend no abuse to the city.

Citizen

12No, sir? yes, sir. If you were not resolved to play the jacks, what need you study for new subjects, purposely to abuse your betters? Why could not you be contented, as well as others, with The Legend of Whittington, or The Life and Death of Sir Thomas Gresham, with the Building of the Royal Exchange? or The Story of Queen Eleanor, with the Rearing of London Bridge upon Woolsacks?

Prologue

13You seem to be an understanding man. What would you have us do, sir?

Citizen

14Why, present something notably in honor of the commons of the city.

Prologue

15Why, what do you say to The Life and Death of Fat Drake, or the Repairing of Fleet Privies?

Citizen

16I do not like that, but I will have a citizen, and he shall be of my own trade.

Prologue

17Oh, you should have told us your mind a month since. Our play is ready to begin now.

Citizen

18'Tis all one for that. I will have a grocer, and he shall do admirable things.

Prologue

19What will you have him do?

Citizen

20Marry, I will have him —

Wife

21(below) Husband, husband.

Rafe

22(below) Peace, Mistress.

Wife

23[below] Hold thy peace, Rafe. I know what I do, I warrant thee. — Husband, husband.

Citizen

24What say'st thou, coney?

Wife

25[below] Let him kill a lion with a pestle, husband, let him kill a lion with a pestle.

Citizen

26So he shall. I'll have him kill a lion with a pestle.

Wife

27[below] Husband, shall I come up, husband?

Citizen

28Ay, coney. — Rafe, help your mistress this way. — Pray, gentlemen, make her a little room. — I pray you, sir, lend me your hand to help up my wife. I thank you sir. — So.

[The Wife comes on the stage.]

Wife

29By your leave, gentlemen all, I'm something troublesome. I'm a stranger here. I was near at one of these plays, as they say, before; but I should have seen Jane Shore once, and my husband hath promised me any time this twelve-month to carry me to The Bold Beauchamps, but in truth he did not. I pray you, bear with me.

Citizen

30Boy, let my wife and I have a couple stools, and then begin; and let the grocer do rare things.

Prologue

31But, sir, we have never a boy to play him. Every one hath a part already.

Wife

32Husband, husband, for God's sake, let Rafe play him. Beshrew me if I do not think he will go beyond them all.

Citizen

33Well remembered, wife. — Come up, Rafe. — I'll tell you, gentlemen, let them but lend him a suit of reparel and necessaries, and by Gad, if any of them all blow wind in the tail on him, I'll be hanged.

[Rafe comes on the stage.]

Wife

34I pray you, youth, let him have a suit of reparel. — I'll be sworn, gentlemen, my husband tells you true: he will act you sometimes at our house, that all the neighbors cry out on him; he will fetch you up a couraging part so in the garret, that we are all as feared, I warrant you, that we quake again. We'll fear our children with him: if they be never so unruly, do but cry "Rafe comes, Rafe comes" to them, and they'll be as quiet as lambs. — Hold up thy head, Rafe, show the gentlemen what thou canst do: speak a huffing part; I warrant you the gentlemen will accept of it.

Citizen

35Do, Rafe, do.

Rafe

36"By heaven, methinks it were an easy leap
37To pluck bright honor from the pale-faced moon
38Or dive into the bottom of the sea
39Where never fathom line touched any ground,
40And pluck up drownèd honor from the lake of hell."

Citizen

41How say you, gentlemen, is it not as I told you?

Wife

42Nay, gentlemen, he hath played before, my husband says, Mucedorus, before the wardens of our company.

Citizen

43Ay, and he should have played Jeronimo with a shoemaker for a wager.

Prologue

44He shall have a suit of apparel, if he will go in.

Citizen

45In, Rafe, in, Rafe, and set out the Grocery in their kind, if thou lov'st me.

[Exit Rafe.]

Wife

46I warrant our Rafe will look finely when he's dressed.

Prologue

47But what will you have it called?

Citizen

48The Grocer's Honor.

Prologue

49Methinks The Knight of the Burning Pestle were better.

Wife

50I'll be sworn, husband, that's as good a name as can be.

Citizen

51Let it be so. Begin, begin. My wife and I will sit down.

Prologue

52I pray you, do.

Citizen

53What stately music have you? You have shawms?

Prologue

54Shawms? No.

Citizen

55No? I'm a thief if my mind did not give me so. Rafe plays a stately part, and he must needs have shawms. I'll be at the charge of them myself rather than we'll be without them.

Prologue

56So you are like to be.

Citizen

57Why, and so I will be. There's two shillings. Let's have the waits of Southwark: they are as rare fellows as any are in England; and that will fetch them all o'er the water with a vengeance, as if they were mad.

Prologue

58You shall have them. Will you sit down then?

Citizen

59Ay. — Come, wife.

Wife

60Sit you merry all, gentlemen. I'm bold to sit amongst you for my ease.

[Citizen and Wife sit down.]

Prologue

61From all that's near the court, from all that's great,
62Within the compass of the city walls,
63We now have brought our scene. Fly far from hence
64All private taxes, immodest phrases,
65Whate'er may but show like vicious.
66For wicked mirth never true pleasure brings,
67But honest minds are pleased with honest things. —

68[To Citizen and Wife] Thus much for that we do. But for Rafe's part, you must answer for yourself.

Citizen

69Take you no care for Rafe, he'l discharge himself, I warrant you.

[Exit Prologue.]

Wife

70I'faith, gentlemen, I'll give my word for Rafe.

1.1

Enter Merchant and Jasper his prentice.

Merchant

1Sirrah, I'll make you know you are my prentice
2And whom my charitable love redeemed
3Even from the fall of fortune, gave thee heat
4And growth to be what now thou art, new cast thee,
5Adding the trust of all I have at home,
6In foreign staples or upon the sea,
7To thy direction; tied the good opinions
8Both of myself and friends to thy endeavors.
9So fair were thy beginnings. But with these,
10As I remember, you had never charge
11To love your Masters daughter, and even then
12When I had found a wealthy husband for her.
13I take it, sir, you had not; but, however,
14I'll break the neck of that commission
15And make you know you are but a merchant's factor.

Jasper

16Sir, I do liberally confess I am yours,
17Bound both by love and duty to your service,
18In which my labor hath been all my profit.
19I have not lost in bargain, nor delighted
20To wear your honest gains upon my back,
21Nor have I given a pension to my blood
22Or lavishly in play consumed your stock.
23These, and the miseries that do attend them,
24I dare, with innocence, proclaim are strangers
25To all my temperate actions. For your daughter,
26If there be any love to my deservings
27Borne by her virtuous self, I cannot stop it!
28Nor am I able to refrain her wishes.
29She's private to herself and best of knowledge,
30Whom she'll make so happy as to sigh for.
31Besides, I cannot think you mean to match her
32Unto a fellow of so lame a presence,
33One that hath little left of nature in him.

Merchant

34'Tis very well, sir. I can tell Your Wisdom
35How all this shall be cured.

Jasper

How all this shall be cured. Your care becomes you.

Merchant

36And thus it must be, sir: I here discharge you
37My house and service; take your liberty,
38And, when I want a son, I'll send for you.

Exit.

Jasper

39These be the fair rewards of them that love.
40Oh, you that live in freedom, never prove
41The travail of a mind led by desire!

Enter Luce.

Luce

42Why, how now, friend, struck with my father's thunder?

Jasper

43Struck and struck dead unless the remedy
44Be full of speed and virtue. I am now
45What I expected long: no more your father's.

Luce

46But mine.

Jasper

But mine. But yours, and only yours I am.
47That's all I have to keep me from the statute.
48You dare be constant still?

Luce

You dare be constant still? Oh, fear me not.
49In this I dare be better than a woman.
50Nor shall his anger nor his offers move me,
51Were they both equal to a prince's power.

Jasper

52You know my rival?

Luce

You know my rival? Yes, and love him dearly
53Even as I love an ague or foul weather.
54I prithee, Jasper, fear him not.

Jasper

I prithee, Jasper, fear him not. Oh, no,
55I do not mean to do him so much kindness.
56But to our own desires: you know the plot
57We both agreed on?

Luce

We both agreed on? Yes, and will perform
58My part exactly.

Jasper

My part exactly. I desire no more.
59Farewell. And keep my heart: 'tis yours.

Luce

Farewell. And keep my heart: 'tis yours. I take it.
60He must do miracles makes me forsake it.

Exeunt.

Citizen

61Fie upon 'em, little infidels! What a matter's here now? Well, I'll be hanged for a halfpenny if there be not some abomination knavery in this play. Well, let 'em look to't. Rafe must come, and if there be any tricks a-brewing —

Wife

62Let 'em brew and bake too, husband, o'God's name. Rafe will find all out, I warrant you, an they were older than they are. — [Enter Boy.] 63I pray, my pretty youth, is Rafe ready?

Boy

64He will be presently.

Wife

65Now, I pray you, make my commendations unto him, and withal carry him this stick of licorice; tell him his mistress sent it him; and bid him bite a piece: 'twill open his pipes the better, say.

[Exit Boy.]

[1.2]

Enter Merchant and Master Humphrey.

Merchant

1Come, sir, she's yours, upon my faith, she's yours.
2You have my hand. For other idle lets
3Between your hopes and her, thus with a wind
4They are scattered and no more. My wanton prentice,
5That like a bladder blew himself with love,
6I have let out and sent him to discover
7New masters yet unknown.

Humphrey

New masters yet unknown. I thank you, sir,
8Indeed, I thank you, sir. And ere I stir
9It shall be known, however you do deem,
10I am of gentle blood and gentle seem.

Merchant

11Oh, sir, I know it certain.

Humphrey

Oh, sir, I know it certain. Sir, my friend,
12Although, as writers say, all things have end,
13And that we call a pudding hath his two,
14Oh, let it not seem strange, I pray to you,
15If in this bloody simile I put
16My love, more endless than frail things or gut.

Wife

17Husband, I prithee, sweet lamb, tell me one thing, but tell me truly — Stay, youths, I beseech you, till I question my husband.

Citizen

18What is it, mouse?

Wife

19Sirrah, didst thou ever see a prettier child? How it behaves itself, I warrant ye, and speaks, and looks, and perts up the head? — I pray you, brother, with your favor, were you never none of Master Moncaster's scholars?

Citizen

20Chicken, I prithee heartily, contain thyself. The childer are pretty childer, but when Rafe comes, lamb —

Wife

21Ay, when Rafe comes, coney — Well, my youth, you may proceed.

Merchant

22Well, sir, you know my love, and rest, I hope,
23Assured of my consent. Get but my daughter's
24And wed her when you please. You must be bold
25And clap in close unto her. Come, I know
26You have language good enough to win a wench.

Wife

27A whoreson tyrant! He's been an old stringer in's days, I warrant him.

Humphrey

28I take your gentle offer and withal
29Yield love again for love reciprocal.

Enter Luce.

Merchant

30What, Luce, within there!

Enter Luce.

Luce

What, Luce, within there! Called you, sir?

Merchant

What, Luce, within there! Called you, sir? I did.
31Give entertainement to this gentleman
32And see you be not froward. — To her, sir,
33My presence will but be an eyesore to you.

Exit.

Humphrey

34Fair Mistress Luce, how do you? Are you well?
35Give me your hand and then, I pray you, tell
36How doth your little sister, and your brother;
37And whether you love me or any other.

Luce

38Sir, these are quickly answered.

Humphrey

Sir, these are quickly answered. So they are,
39Where women are not cruel. But how far
40Is it now distant from this place we are in
41Unto that blessèd place, your father's warren?

Luce

42What makes you think of that, sir?

Humphrey

43Even that face.
44For, stealing rabbits whilom in that place,
45God Cupid, or the keeper, I know not whether,
46Unto my cost and charges brought you thither,
47And there began —

Luce

And there began — Your game, sir.

Humphrey

And there began — Your game, sir. Let no game,
48Or anything that tendeth to the same,
49Be evermore remembered, thou fair killer,
50For whom I sat me downe and brake my tiller.

Wife

51There's a kind gentleman, I warrant you. When will you do as much for me, George?

Luce

52Beshrew me, sir, I am sorry for your losses,
53But, as the proverbe says, I cannot cry.
54I would you had not seen me.

Humphrey

I would you had not seen me. So would I.
55Unless you had more maw to do me good.

Luce

56Why, cannot this strange passion be withstood,?
57Send for a constable and raise the town.

Humphrey

58Oh, no, my valiant love will batter down
59Millions of constables, and put to flight
60Even that great watch of Midsummer Day at night.

Luce

61Beshrew me, sir, 'twere good I yielded, then.
62Weak women cannot hope where valiant men
63Have no resistance.

Humphrey

Have no resistance. Yield, then. I am full
64Of pity, though I say it, and can pull
65Out of my pocket, thus, a pair of gloves.
66Look, Lucy, look, the dog's tooth nor the dove's
67Are not so white as these; and sweet they be,
68And whipped about with silk, as you may see.
69If you desire the price, shoot from your eye
70A beam to this place, and you shall espy
71F. S., which is to say, my sweetest honey,
72They cost me three and two pence, or no money.

Luce

73Well, sir, I take them kindly, and I thank you.
74What would you more?

Humphrey

What would you more? Nothing.

Luce

What would you more? Nothing. Why, then, farewell.

Humphrey

75Nor so, nor so. For, lady, I must tell,
76Before we part, for what we met together.
77God grant me time, and patience, and fair weather.

Luce

78Speak and declare your mind in terms so brief.

Humphrey

79I shall. Then first and foremost, for relief
80I call to you, if that you can afford it.
81I care not at what price, for, on my word, it
82Shall be repaid again, although it cost me
83More than I'll speak of now; for love hath tossed me
84In furious blanket like a tennis ball,
85And now I rise aloft, and now I fall.

Luce

86Alas, good gentleman, alas, the day!

Humphrey

87I thank you heartily, and, as I say,
88Thus do I still continue without rest,
89I'th' morning like a man, at night a beast,
90Roaring and bellowing mine own disquiet,
91That much I fear forsaking of my diet
92Will bring me presently to that quandary
93I shall bid all adieu.

Luce

I shall bid all adieu. Now, by Saint Mary,
94That were great pity!

Humphrey

That were great pity! So it were, beshrew me.
95Then ease me, lusty Luce, and pity show me.

Luce

96Why, sir, you know my will is nothing worth
97Without my father's grant. Get his consent,
98And then you may with assurance try me.

Humphrey

99The worshipful your sire will not deny me;
100For I have asked him, and he hath replied
101"Sweet Master Humphrey, Luce shall be thy bride".

Luce

102Sweet Master Humphrey, then I am content.

Humphrey

103And so am I, in truth.

Luce

And so am I, in truth. Yet take me with you.
104There is another clause must be annexed,
105And this it is (I swore and will perform it):
106No man shall ever joy me as his wife
107But he that stole me hence. If you dare venture,
108I am yours. You need not fear; my father loves you.
109If not, farewell forever.

Humphrey

If not, farewell forever. Stay, nymph, stay.
110I have a double gelding, colored bay,
111Sprung by his father from Barbarian kind;
112Another for myself, though somewhat blind,
113Yet true as trusty tree.

Luce

Yet true as trusty tree. I am satisfied,
114And so I give my hand. Our course must lie
115Through Waltham Forest, where I have a friend
116Will entertaine us. So farewell, sir Humphrey,
117And thinke upon your business.

Exit Luce.

Humphrey

118Though I die,
119I am resolved to venture life and limb
120For one so young, so fair, so kind, so trim.

Exit Humphrey.

Wife

121By my faith and troth, George, and as I am virtuous, it is e'en the kindest young man that ever trod on shoe-leather. — Well, go thy ways; if thou hast her not, 'tis not thy fault, 'faith.

Citizen

122I prithee, mouse, be patient. 'A shall have her, or I'll make some of 'em smoke for't.

Wife

123That's my good lamb, George. Fie, this stinking tobacco kills men! Would there were none in England. Now, I pray, gentlemen, what good does this stinking tobacco do you? Nothing, I warrant you make chimneys o'your faces. — Oh, husband, husband, now, now, there's Rafe, there's Rafe!

[1.3]

Enter Rafe, like a grocer in's shop, with two prentices [Tim and George] reading "Palmerin of England".

Citizen

1Peace, fool; let Rafe, alone. — Hark you, Rafe; do not strain yourself too much at the first. — Peace. — Begin, Rafe.

Rafe

2[reading] "Then Palmerin and Trineus, snatching their lances from their dwarfs, and clasping their helmets, galloped amain after the giant, and Palmerin, having gotten a sight of him, came posting amain, saying 'Stay, traitorous thief, for thou mayst not so carry away her, that is worth the greatest lord in the world'; and with these words gave him a blow on the shoulder that he struk him besides his elephant. And Trineus, coming to the knight that had Agricola behind him, set him soon besides his horse, with his neck broken in the fall, so that the princess, getting out of the throng, between joy and grief, said 'All-happy knight, the mirror of all such as follow arms, now may I be well assured of the love thou bearest me'." — I wonder why the kings do not raise an army of fourteen or fifteen hundred thousand men, as big as the army that the Prince of Portigo brought against Rosicleer, and destroy these Giants. They do much hurt to wandering damsels that go in quest of their knights.

Wife

3Faith, husband, and Rafe says true, for they say the King of Portugal cannot sit at his meat but the giants and the elfins will come and snatch it from him —

Citizen

4Hold thy tongue! — On, Rafe.

Rafe

5And certainly those knights are much to be commended, who, neglecting their possessions, wander with a squire and a dwarf through the deserts to relieve poor Ladies.

Wife

6Ay, by my faith, are they, Rafe. Let 'em say what they will, they are indeed. Our knights neglect their possessions well enough, but they do not the rest.

Rafe

7There are no such courteous and fair well-spoken knights in this age. They will call one the son of a whore that Palmerin of England would have called fair sir; and one that Rosicleer would have called right beauteous damsel, they will call damned bitch.

Wife

8I'll be sworn will they, Rafe. They have called me so an hundred times about a scurvy pipe of tobacco.

Rafe

9But what brave spirit could be content to sit in his shop with a flappet of wood and a blue apron before him, selling mithridatum and dragon's water to visited houses, that might pursue feats of arms, and through his noble achievements procure such a famous history to be written of his heroic prowess?

Citizen

10Well said, Rafe; some more of those words, Rafe.

Wife

11They go finely, by my troth.

Rafe

12Why should not I then pursue this course, both for the credit of myself and our company? For amongst all the worthy books of achievements I do not call to mind that I yet read of a grocer-errant. I will be the said knight. Have you heard of any, that hath wandered unfurnished of his squire and dwarf? My elder prentice Tim shall be my trusty squire, and little George my dwarf. Hence, my blue apron! Yet in remembrance of my former trade, upon my shield shall be portrayed a burning pestle, and I will be called the Knight o'th' Burning Pestle.

Wife

13Nay, I dare swear thou wilt not forget thy old trade. Thou wert ever meek.

Rafe

14Tim.

Tim

15Anon.

Rafe

16My beloved squire, and George, my dwarf, I charge you that from henceforth you never call me by any other name but the Right Courteous and Valiant Knight of the Burning Pestle, and that you never call any female by the name of a woman or wench, but fair lady, if she have her desires, if not, distressed damsel, that you call all forests and heaths deserts, and all horses palfreys.

Wife

17This is very fine, faith. — Do the gentlemen like Rafe, think you, husband?

Citizen

18Ay, I warrant thee, the players would give all the shoes in their shop for him.

Rafe

19My beloved squire Tim, stand out; admit this were a desert, and over it a knight-errant pricking, and I should bid you inquire of his intents, what would you say?

Tim

20“Sir, my master sent me to know whither you are riding.”

Rafe

21No, thus: "Fair sir, the Right Courteous and Valiant Knight of the Burning Pestle commanded me to inquire upon what adventure you are bound, whether to relieve some distressed damsels, or otherwise."

Citizen

22Whoreson blockhead cannot remember!

Wife

23I'faith, and Rafe told him on't before. All the gentlemen heard him. — Did he not, gentlemen, did not Rafe tell him on't?

George

24“Right Courteous and Valiant Knight of the Burning Pestle, here is a distressed damsel to have a halfpennyworth of pepper.”

Wife

25That's a good boy. See, the little boy can hit it. By my troth, it's a fine child.

Rafe

26Relieve her with all courteous language. Now shut up shop; no more my prentice but my trusty squire and dwarf. I must bespeak my shield and arming pestle.

[Exenut Tim and George.]

Citizen

27Go thy ways, Rafe. As I'm a true man, thou art the best on 'em all.

Wife

28Rafe, Rafe.

Rafe

29What say you, mistress?

Wife

30I prithee come again quickly, sweet Rafe.

Rafe

31By and by.

Exit Rafe.

[1.4]

Enter Jasper and his mother, mistress Merrythought.

Mistress Merrythought

1Give thee my blessing? No, I'll ne'er give thee my blessing, I'll see thee hanged first; it shall ne'er be said I gave thee my blessing. Thou'rt thy father's own son, of the right blood of the Merrythoughts. I may curse the time that e'er I knew thy father: he hath spent all his own, and mine too, and when I tell him of it, he laughs and dances, and sings, and cries "A merry heart lives long-a." And thou art a wastethrift and art run away from thy master, that loved thee well, and art come to me, and I have laid up a little for my younger son Michael, and thou think'st to bezzle that, but thou shalt never be able to do it. — Come hither Michael. Enter Michael. 2Come, Michael, down on thy knees. Thou shalt have my blessing.

Michael

3I pray you, mother, pray to God to bless me.

Mistress Merrythought

4God bless thee. But Jasper shall never have my blessing; he shall be hanged first, shall he not, Michael? How say'st thou?

Michael

5Yes, forsooth, mother, and grace of God.

Mistress Merrythought

6That's a good boy.

Wife

7Ay, faith, it's a fine-spoken child.

Jasper

8Mother, though you forget a parent's love,
9I must preserve the duty of a child.
10I ran not from my master, nor return
11To have your stock maintain my idleness.

Wife

12Ungracious child, I warrant him. Hark how he chops logic with his mother. — Thou hadst best tell her she lies. Do, tell her she lies.

Citizen

13If he were my son, I would hang him up by the heels, and flay him, and salt him, whoreson halter-sack.

Jasper

14My coming only is to beg your love,
15Which I must ever, though I never gain it;
16And howsoever you esteem of me,
17There is no drop of blood hid in these veins
18But I remember well belongs to you
19That brought me forth, and would be glad for you

119After this action, I preferrèd was,
120And chosen city captain at Mile End,
121With hat and feather and with leading staff,
122And trained my men and brought them all off clear,
123Save one man that berayed him with the noise.
124But all these things I, Rafe, did undertake
125Only for my belovèd Susan's sake.
126Then coming home, and sitting in my shop
127With apron blue, Death came unto my stall
128To cheapen aqua vitae, but ere I
129Could take the bottle down and fill a taste,
130Death caught a pound of pepper in his hand
131And sprinkled all my face and body o'er,
132And in an instant vanishèd away.

Citizen

133'Tis a pretty fiction, i'faith.

Rafe

134Then took I up my bow and shaft in hand
135And walked into Moorefields to cool myself;
136But there grim cruel Death met me again
137And shot this forkèd arrow through my head,
138And now I faint. Therefore, be warned by me,
139My fellows every one, of forkèd heads.
140Farewell, all you good boys in merry London.
141Ne'er shall we more upon Shrove Tuesday meet
142And pluck downe houses of iniquity.
143My pain increaseth. I shall never more
144Hold open, whilst another pumps both legs,
145Nor daub a satin gown with rotten eggs.
146Set up a stake, oh, never more I shall.
147I die. Fly, fly, my soul to Grocer's Hall.
148Oh, oh, oh, etc.

Wife

149Well said, Rafe. Do your obeisance to the gentlemen and go your ways. Well said, Rafe.

Exit Rafe.

Old Merrythought

150Methinks all we, thus kindly and unexpectedly reconciled, should not depart without a song.

Merchant

151A good motion.

Old Merrythought

152Strike up, then.

Song.

153Better music ne'er was known
154Than a choir of hearts in one.
155Let each other that hath been
156Troubled with the gall or spleen
157Learn of us to keep his brow
158Smooth and plain as ours are now.
159Sing, though, before the hour of dying;
160He shall rise and then be crying:
161"Hey ho! 'Tis naught but mirth
162That keeps the body from the earth."

Exeunt omnes.

Epilogus

Citizen

163Come, Nell. Shall we go? The play's done.

Wife

164Nay, by my faith, George, I have more manners than so; I'll speak to these gentlemen first. — I thank you all gentlemen, for your patience and countenance to Rafe, a poor fatherless child. And, if I might see you at my house, it should go hard but I would have a pottle of wine and a pipe of tobacco for you; for truly I hope you do like the youth, but I would be glad to know the truth. I referr it to your own discretions, whether you will applaud him or no, for I will wink, and whilst you shall do what you will, I thank you with all my heart. God give you good night. Come, George.

[Exeunt.]

1"Lontana dalla corte, lontana dai grandi, entro
la cinta delle mura cittadine, portiamo questa volta la nostra

Il Cittadino monta sul palcoscenico.

CITTADINO

2Zitto, bel giovinotto mio!

PROLOGO

3Che volete dire, signore?

CITTADINO

4Che non siete come si deve. Osservo che, da
sette anni che state recitando in questo teatro, continuate
a prendere in giro i cittadini; e stavolta intitolate la vostra
commedia Il mercante di Londra. Giú quel titolo, giovinotto,
giú.

PROLOGO

5Siete membro della nobile città?

CITTADINO

6Sicuro.

PROLOGO

7E membro d'una delle compagnie?

CITTADINO

8Sí, di quella dei droghieri.

PROLOGO

9Ah, siete droghiere; e allora con vostra graziosa
licenza, non vogliamo per niente recare offesa alla città.

CITTADINO

10No, signor mio! Sí, signor mio! Se non foste
decisi a giocar dei tiri, che bisogno avreste di studiar nuove
commedie, per offendere di proposito chi val piú di voi?
Perché non vi dovreste accontentare, come s'accontentano
gli altri, della "Leggenda di Whittington", o della "Vita
e morte di sir Thomas Greshan, con costruzione della Borsa",
o della "Storia della regina Eleonora, con la elevazione del
Ponte di Londra su sacchi di lana"?

PROLOGO

11Mi pare che siate un uomo intelligente; che cosa
vorreste facessimo, signore?

CITTADINO

12Che presentaste qualche cosa di importante,
in onore dei membri della città.

PROLOGO

13E che cosa ne direste della "Vita e morte di
Drake il grasso, o della riparazione dei cessi della prigione di
Fleet"?

CITTADINO

14Non mi piaccion codeste cose; voglio che ci
sia un cittadino, e che sia del mio mestiere.

PROLOGO

15Oh, ma ci avreste dovuto esprimere il vostro
desiderio un mese fa; la nostra commedia adesso è pronta
per la rappresentazione.

CITTADINO

16E che mi fa? Voglio un droghiere, e deve
far cose meravigliose.

PROLOGO

17Cosa volete che faccia?

CITTADINO

18Affemia, voglio che…

MOGLIE

19tra il pubblico Marito mio! Marito mio!

RALPH

20tra il pubblico Zitta, padrona!

MOGLIE

21(c. s.) Smettila, Ralph; ti dico che so io quello
che faccio. – Marito mio, marito mio!

CITTADINO

22Che cosa dici, coniglietta?

MOGLIE

23(c. s.) Fa che ammazzi un leone con un pestello,
marito mio! fa che ammazzi un leone con un pestello!

CITTADINO

24Sicuro! – Voglio che ammazzi un leone con
un pestello.

MOGLIE

25(c. s.) Marito! debbo venir su anch'io, marito?

CITTADINO

26Sí, coniglietta. – Ralph, aiuta la tua padrona
da questa parte. – Prego, signori, fatele un po' di posto. –
Di grazia, signore, datemi una mano ad aiutare mia moglie
a salire. Grazie, signore. – Ecco!

La Moglie sale sul palcoscenico.

MOGLIE

27Signori, col vostro permesso! Lo so, sono piuttosto
importuna. E qui sono un'estranea: in vita mia non
sono mai stata a una di queste commedie, come le chiamano;
ma, una volta dovevo andare a vedere "Jane Shore";
e son dodici mesi che mio marito continua a promettermi di
portarmi a "Beauchamps l'ardito", ma, in verità, non mi
ci ha condotta. Vi prego, abbiate pazienza.

CITTADINO

28Ragazzo, fa portare un paio di scranne per
mia moglie e per me, e poi cominciate; e che il droghiere
faccia qualcosa di straordinario.

Portano le scranne.

PROLOGO

29Ma, signore, non abbiamo nessuno che possa
recitare la sua parte. Ognuno ha già la sua.

MOGLIE

30Marito, marito mio, per amor di Dio, la deve
recitare Ralph! Che il diavolo mi porti se non credo che reciterà
meglio di tutti.

CITTADINO

31Ben detto, moglie. – Vien su, Ralph! – Vi
dico, signori: che gli prestino soltanto un costume e quel
che occorre, e, perdiana, se uno solo di loro gli può mettere
il sale sulla coda, voglio essere impiccato!

Ralph sale sul palcoscenico.

MOGLIE

32Vi prego, quel giovane, dategli un costume. –
Son pronta a giurare, signori, che mio marito ha detto la verità.
A volte Ralph si mette a recitare a casa nostra, in modo
che tutti i vicini restano a bocca aperta; in soffitta, è capace
di fare una parte da bravaccio così bene da metterci paura a
tutti: ve lo garantisco, ne tremiamo ancora. Ci serviamo di
lui per spaventare i bambini: quando fanno i cattivi, basta
gridare: "Viene Ralph, ora viene Ralph!", e diventan tranquilli
come agnellini. – Su la testa, Ralph; mostra a questi
signori quel che sai fare; fa il fanfarone; ti garantisco che
questi signori se la godranno.

CITTADINO

33Su, Ralph, da bravo.

RALPH

34Per il cielo, mi pare che sarebbe facile fare un salto
per portar via l'onor della luce alla pallida luna, o tuffarsi
nel fondo del mare dove mai scandaglio toccò terra, e portar
su dal lago d'inferno l'affogato onore.

CITTADINO

35Che ne dite, signori? non è come vi dicevo?

MOGLIE

36Ma sí, signori, ha recitato, dice mio marito,

CITTADINO

37Sicuro! e per scommessa doveva far da Jeronimo
con un calzolaio.

PROLOGO

38Se vuol venire, gli daremo un costume.

CITTADINO

39Va, Ralph! va! e fa vedere quel che sanno
essere i droghieri, se mi vuoi bene!

Ralph esce.

MOGLIE

40Vi garantisco che il nostro Ralph farà la sua
figura, quando sarà vestito.

PROLOGO

41Ma come volete che si chiami la commedia?

CITTADINO

42"L'onore del droghiere".

PROLOGO

43Mi pare che sarebbe meglio chiamarla "Il Cavaliere

MOGLIE

44Marito, son pronta a giurare che questo è il più

CITTADINO

45E va bene. – Cominciate, cominciate; mia
moglie e io ci metteremo a sedere.

PROLOGO

46Sí, ve ne prego.

CITTADINO

47Avete una musica grandiosa? Cennamelle?

PROLOGO

48Cennamelle? No.

CITTADINO

49No? Datemi del ladro, se non me l'ero immaginato.

PROLOGO

50Cosí dovrete fare, credo.

CITTADINO

51Sí; e cosí sarà. Ecco due scellini; fate venire

PROLOGO

52Li avrete. Volete mettervi a sedere dunque?

CITTADINO

53Sí. – Vieni, moglie.

MOGLIE

54Sedetevi tutti allegramente, signori miei; mi dicono

Una stanza in casa di Venturewell.

Entrano Venturewell e Jasper.

VENTUREWELL

58Caro mio, vi voglio ricordare che siete il
mio apprendista, salvato per mio caritatevole amore dalla
cattiva sorte. Io ti ho scaldato e fatto crescere fino a diventare
quello che sei; io ti ho rifatto. E ho aggiunto a questo
la mia fiducia, e ho affidato tutto quanto ho in casa, in mercati
stranieri o sul mare, alla tua direzione; e ho legato la
mia reputazione e quella dei miei amici al tuo operare; di
meglio non potevi incominciare. Ma, da quanto ricordo, non
v'ho mai dato l'incarico d'amare la figlia del vostro padrone, e
proprio quando le avevo trovato un ricco marito… non ve
l'ho dato, mi pare, signor mio. Ma in ogni modo, ve lo leverò
io quell'incarico, e vi farò capire che non siete che l'amministratore
di un mercante.

JASPER

59Signore, confesso di gran cuore d'esser vostro,
d'esser legato da amore e dovere al vostro servizio nel quale
tutto il mio profitto sono state le fatiche mie. Non ho perduto
nei contratti e non m'è piaciuto portare sul mio dosso i vostri
onesti guadagni; né mi sono abbandonato ai piaceri, e non
ho sperperato le vostre mercanzie al gioco. Queste cose, e i
mali che ne vengono, oso in tutta purità di coscienza proclamarli
lontani dal mio agire onesto. In quanto a vostra figlia,
se nella sua virtuosa coscienza m'ama per quello che valgo,
non la posso trattenere; né so frenare i suoi desideri: è padrona
di sé, e sa meglio di ogni altro chi ha da far felice sospirando
per lui. Per di piú, non so credere che la vogliate sposare
con un uomo cosí debole, al quale rimane tanto poco delle
forze che ci dà la natura.

VENTUREWELL

60Molto bene, signorino mio; saprò dire alla
vostra saggezza come si può curare tutto questo.

JASPER

61Sta a voi di averne cura.

VENTUREWELL

62E cosí sarà, signorino mio; qui vi licenzio
dalla mia casa e dal mio servizio; pigliatevi la vostra libertà;
e quando vorrò avere un figlio vi manderò a cercare.

(Esce).

JASPER

63Ecco il bel premio che hanno quelli che amano.
Oh, voi che vivete liberi, non vi dia mai la vostra esperienza
di conoscere le sofferenze dell'animo in preda al desiderio!

Entra Luce.

LUCE

64Come, amico? colpito dalle folgori scagliate da mio
padre?

JASPER

65Colpito, e colpito a morte, se il rimedio non è
veloce e appieno efficace: è accaduto quello che m'aspettavo
da lungo tempo: no sono piú con vostro padre.

LUCE

66Ma con me.

JASPER

67Ma con voi, e soltanto con voi; ci siete voi sola
a proteggermi. Osate ancora amarmi?

LUCE

68Oh, non temete di me! In questo, oso esser piú
che donna. Né la sua collera né le sue promesse mi commuoveranno,
neppure se fossero pari a quelle di un principe.

JASPER

69Conoscete il mio rivale?

LUCE

70Sí, e gli voglio molto bene… come ne voglio alla
febbre terzana, o al maltempo. Ti prego, Jasper, non temere!

JASPER

71Oh, no, non voglio usargli tanta cortesia. Ma
pensiamo ai nostri desidèri; sapete quello che decidemmo
insieme?

LUCE

72Sí, e farò la mia parte, fedelmente.

JASPER

73Non desidero piú. Addio, e tenete con voi il
mio cuore: è vostro.

LUCE

74Me lo prendo; e dovrà saper far miracoli, chi me
lo farà abbandonare.

(Escono).

CITTADINO

75Vergogna, vergogna, cattivelli! Che bello spettacolo
abbiamo davanti! Voglio essere impiccato per un quattrino
se non c'è qualche odioso tiro in questa commedia. Bé,
bàdino a quello che fanno; deve venire Ralph, e se ci son dei
pasticci in aria…

MOGLIE

76Lascia che ci siano, e anche in terra, marito, in
nome di Dio; Ralph scoprirà ogni cosa, te lo garantisco, anche
se fossero piú furbi di quel che sono. Entra un Ragazzo.
– Dimmi, ti prego, bel giovane, è pronto Ralph?

RAGAZZO

77Lo sarà tra poco.

MOGLIE

78E allora, ti prego, salutalo per me, e portagli
questo bastoncino di liquerizia; digli che glielo manda la sua
padrona, e che ne succhi un pezzettino; gli schiarirà la gola,
diglielo.

(Esce il Ragazzo).

La stessa.

Entrano Venturewell e Mastro Humphrey.

VENTUREWELL

79Andiamo, signore, è vostra; sulla mia fede,
è vostra; vi ho data la mano; e gli altri vani ostacoli fra le
vostre speranze e lei, cosí, con un soffio, sono dispersi, e non
se ne parla piú. Quel briccone del mio apprendista, che s'è
gonfiato d'amore come una vescica, l'ho cacciato via; vada
a cercare nuovi padroni, che ancora non conosce.

HUMPHREY

80Vi ringrazio, signore; davvero vi ringrazio,
signore; e prima ch'io mi muova, dovete sapere, qualunque
cosa ne pensiate, che sono di nobile sangue e di gentile aspetto.

VENTUREWELL

81Oh signore, lo so benissimo.

HUMPHREY

82Signore, amico mio, benché, come dicono gli
scrittori, tutte le cose abbiano fine e ogni migliaccio abbia il
suo eguale (oh, non vi paia strano, ve ne prego, ch'io ve lo
dica con questa metafora sanguinolenta), il mio amore è piú
eterno degli oggetti frali o delle budella.

MOGLIE

83Marito, ti prego, agnellino mio, dimmi un cosa,
ma dimmi la verità. – Aspettate, ragazzi, per favore, fin
che faccio una domanda a mio marito.

CITTADINO

84Che cosa vuoi, topolino mio?

MOGLIE

85Caro, hai mai veduto un piú bel ragazzo? Come
si muove, non lo vedi, perdinci, e parla e tien su la testolina!
– Ditemi, amico, per favore, non siete mai stato scolaro di
mastro Moncaster?

CITTADINO

86Pollastrina mia, ti prego vivamente, frènati;
i bambini son bei bambini; ma quando verrà Ralph, agnellino
mio…

MOGLIE

87Ah sí, quando verrà Ralph, coniglietto mio! –
Sí, giovinotto, potete continuare.

VENTUREWELL

88Bene, signore; conoscete il mio affetto,
e vi fiderete, spero, del mio consenso; solo che adesso voi
l'otteniate da mia figlia, sposatela quando vi aggrada. Dovete
farvi ardito e stringerla da vicino; andiamo, so che avete
eloquenza abbastanza per innamorare una ragazza.

MOGLIE

89Bel tiranno, figlio di baldracca! Dev'essere stato
un famoso libertino, ai suoi tempi, ve lo garantisco!

HUMPHREY

90Accetto la vostra gentile offerta; e cosí, possa
l'amore far nascere reciproco amore.

VENTUREWELL

91Ehi, Luce, sei là?

Entra Luce.

LUCE

92Avete chiamato, signore?

VENTUREWELL

93Sí, intrattenete questo gentiluomo, e badate
di non essere bisbetica. – Vi lascio con lei, signore! La
mia presenza sarebbe per voi un pruno.

(Esce).

HUMPHREY

94Bella signora Luce, come state? State bene?
Datemi la mano; e poi, ve ne prego, ditemi come stanno la
vostra sorellina e vostro fratello; e se amate me, o qualche
altro.

LUCE

95Signore, a questo si fa presto a rispondere.

HUMPHREY

96Cosí è, quando le donne non son crudeli.
Ma ditemi, qual è la distanza che separa il luogo dove siamo
da quel luogo benedetto che è la conigliera di vostro padre?

LUCE

97Cos'è che vi fa pensare a questo, signore?

HUMPHREY

98Proprio il vostro volto; ché, rubando conigli
in quel luogo, il dio Cupido o il guardiano, non so quale dei
due, a mio costo e spese vi porto là, e là cominciò…

LUCE

99La vostra caccia, signore?

HUMPHREY

100Non ricordiamo mai più la caccia, né nulla
che le si avvicini, o bella crudele per la quale iniziai l'assedio,
e ruppi la balestra.

MOGLIE

101Ecco un gentiluomo cortese, in fede mia; quando
mai farai altrettanto per me, George?

LUCE

102Ahimè, signore, sono dolente della vostra perdita;
ma, come dice il proverbio, "non posso piangere". Cosí non
mi avreste veduta!

HUMPHREY

103È quel che dico anch'io, a meno che non
aveste piú buona voglia di farmi contento.

LUCE

104Ma come, non potete resistere all'assalto di questa
strana passione? Mandate a chiamare le guardie, e mettete
l'allarme in città.

HUMPHREY

105Oh, no, il mio amor coraggioso sconfiggerà
milioni di guardie, e metterà in fuga anche la gran rivista
della notte di mezza estate.

LUCE

106Ahimè, signore, allora sarebbe meglio ch'io cedessi;
le donne deboli non possono aver speranza là dove gli uomini
coraggiosi non sanno far resistenza.

HUMPHREY

107Cedete, allora; son pieno di pietà, anche se
son io a dirlo; e posso cosí tirar fuori di tasca un paio di guanti.
Guardate, Luce, guardate; né i denti dei cani, né quelli delle
colombe sono bianchi com'essi; e graziosi sono, e ornati di
seta, come potete vedere. Se volete sapere il prezzo, lanciate
un raggio dei vostri occhi su questo punto, e scorgerete F S,
vale a dire, mio dolcissimo miele, che mi son costati tre scellini
e due denari o niente.

LUCE

108Bene, signore, li prenderò volentieri, e vi ringrazio.
Che cosa volete ancora?

HUMPHREY

109Nulla.

LUCE

110E allora, addio!

HUMPHREY

111No, non cosí, non cosí; perché, signora, prima
che ci lasciamo debbo dirvi lo scopo del nostro incontro...
(Dio mi conceda pazienza, tempo e cielo sereno).

LUCE

112Parlate, e dite quel che pensate senza tante parole.

HUMPHREY

113Lo farò. Dunque, in prima e anzi tutto vengo
da voi per aver sollievo, se potete concedermelo; non importa
a qual prezzo, poiché, parola mia, sarà ripagato, dovesse costarmi
piú ch’io non dica adesso. Perché l’amore m’ha sballottato
furiosamente contro cortina, come una palla da tennis,
e ora salgo in aria, ora casco.

LUCE

114Ahimè, poveretto, che disgrazia!

HUMPHREY

115Vi ringrazio di tutto cuore; e, come dico,
sempre rimango senza riposo, cosí: la mattina, come un uomo,
la notte come una bestia, ruggendo e dando fiato alla mia
inquietudine; sí che temo assai l’abbandono della mia dieta
mi porterà fra poco a quel bivio, dal quale dovrò dire a tutti
addio.

LUCE

116Vergine santa, sarebbe un gran peccato!

HUMPHREY

117Sí, che lo sarebbe, ahimè! E allora, aiutatemi,
generosa Luce, e mostrate di aver pietà di me.

LUCE

118Ma voi sapete, signore, che la mia volontà non è
niente senza il permesso di mio padre; ottenete il suo consenso,
e poi potrete fiducioso provar con me.

HUMPHREY

119Il vostro onorando padre non mi darà un
diniego; perché gli ho chiesto, e mi ha risposto: «Caro mastro
Humphrey, Luce sarà la tua sposa».

LUCE

120Allora, caro mastro Humphrey, son contenta.

HUMPHREY

121E anch’io lo sono, davvero.

LUCE

possa piú crescere.

TIM

238Vado, coraggioso cavaliere.

(Esce).

(Esce).

GEORGE

240Il cielo benedica il cavaliere che cosí libera le
povere gentildonne erranti!

(Esce).

a combattere davanti a me, e che ci sieno tamburi e trombe,

242Sta zitta un momento, uccellino mio! Li ammazzerà
tutti, anche se fossero venti piú di quelli che sono.

245E ne avrà ben ragione, amor mio.

MICHAEL

310Oh sí, mamma, sí, e ho una gran fame.

311Confortatevi, dama graziosa, e voi, bello scudiero!

RALPH

320Bussate alle porte, mio scudiero, con regal lancia.

e a tutti i cavalieri erranti, pensai di bussare, e ora
mi faccio ardito d'entrare.

GARZONE

324Se desiderate vedere una stanza, siete assai
benvenuto.

(Escono).

325Vorrei che qualcosa si facesse, e non so dire quale.

CITTADINO

326Cosa, Nell?

MOGLIE

Entra il Vecchio Merrythought.

MERRYTHOUGHT

342(canta) Quando venne l'oscura mezzanotte,

Ecco entrò il triste spettro di Margaret,

cui parlo, di questa nobil città, e levate il morbido capo,

detto, per nostra vergogna, che noi, giovanetti di Londra,

prolungare la gioia, Dio salvi il re, e mandi pace al paese, e
sradichi il tradimento dalla nostra terra! E cosí, amici, vi
lascio.
