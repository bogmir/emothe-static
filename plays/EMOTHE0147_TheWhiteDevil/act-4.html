[Act IV, Scene I]

Enter FRANCISCO and MONTICELSO

MONTICELSO

1Come, come my lord, untie your folded thoughts,
2And let them dangle loose as a bride's hair.
3Your sister's poisoned.

FRANCISCO

Your sister's poisoned. Far be it from my thoughts
4To seek revenge.

MONTICELSO

To seek revenge. What, are you turn'd all marble?

FRANCISCO

5Shall I defy him, and impose a war
6Most burthensome on my poor subjects' necks,
7Which at my will I have not power to end?
8You know; for all the murders, rapes,, and thefts,
9Committed in the horrid lust of war,
10He that unjustly caus'd it first proceed,
11Shall find it in his grave and in his seed.

MONTICELSO

12That's not the course I'd wish you: pray, observe me.
13We see that undermining more prevails
14That doth the cannon. Bear you wrongs conceal'd,
15And, patient as the tortoise, let this camel
16Stalk o'er your back unbruis'd: sleep with the lion,
17And let this brood of secure foolish mice
18Play with your nostrils; till the time be ripe
19For th' bloody audit, and the fatal gripe:
20Aim like a cunning fowler, close one eye,
21That you the better may your game espy.

FRANCISCO

22Free me my innocence, from treacherous acts:
23I know there's thunder yonder: and I'll stand,
24Like a safe valley, which low bends the knee
25To some aspiring mountain: since I know
26Treason, like spiders weaving nets for flies,
27By her foul work is found, and in it dies.
28To pass away these thoughts, my honour'd lord,
29It is reported you possess a book
30Wherein you have quoted, by intelligence,
31The names of all notorious offenders
32Lurking about the city.

MONTICELSO

Lurking about the city. Sir I do;
33And some there are which call it my black book:
34Well may the title hold: for thought it teach not
35The art of conjuring, yet in it lurk
36The names of many devils.

FRANCISCO

The names of many devils. Pray let's see it.

MONTICELSO

37I'll fetch it to your lordship.

Exit MONTICELSO

FRANCISCO

I'll fetch it to your lordship. Monticelso,
38I will not trust thee, but in all my plots
39I'll rest as jealous as a town besieg'd.
40Thou canst not reach what I intend to act;
41Your flax soon kindles, soon is out again,
42But gold slow heats, and long will hot remain.

Enter MONT[ICELSO] presents FRAN[CISCO] with a book

MONTICELSO

43'Tis here my lord.

FRANCISCO

44First your intelligencers, pray let's see.

MONTICELSO

45Their number rises strangely,
46And some of them
47You'd take for honest men.
48Next are panders.
49These are your pirates: and these following leaves,
50For base rogues that undo young gentlemen
51By taking up commodities: for politic bankrupts:
52For fellows that are bawds to their own wives,
53Only to put off horses and slight jewels,
54Clocks, defac'd plate, and such commodities,
55At birth of their first children.

FRANCISCO

At birth of their first children. Are there such?

MONTICELSO

56These are for imprudent bawds,
57That go in men's apparel; for users
58That share with scriveners for their good reportage:
59For lawyers that will antedate their writs:
60And some divines you might find folded there,
61But that I slip them o'er for conscience' sake.
62Here is a general catalogue of knaves.
63A man might study all the prisons o'er,
64Yet never attain this knowledge.

FRANCISCO

Yet never attain this knowledge. Murderers.
65Fold down the leaf I pray.
66Good my lord let me borrow this strange doctrine.

MONTICELSO

67Pray us't my lord.

FRANCISCO

Pray us't my lord. I do assure your lordship,
68You are a worthy member of the state,
69And have done inginite good in your discovery
70Of these offenders.

MONTICELSO

Of these offenders. Somewhat sir.

FRANCISCO

Of these offenders. Somewhat sir. O God!
71Better than tribute of wolves paid in England,
72'Twill hang their skins o'th' hedge.

MONTICELSO

'Twill hang their skins o'th' hedge. I must make bold
73To leave your lordship.

FRANCISCO

To leave your lordship. Dearly sir, I thank you;
74If any ask for me at court, report
75You have left me in the company of knaves.

Exit MONT[ICELSO]

76I gather now by this, some cunning fellow
77That's my lord's officer, one that lately skipp'd
78From a clerk's desk up to a justice' chair,
79Hath made this knavish summons; and intends,
80As th'Irish rebels wont were to sell heads,
81So to make prize of these. And thus it happens,
82Your poor rogues pay for't, which have not the means
83To present bribe in fist: the rest o'th' band
84Are raz'd out of the knaves' record; or else
85My lord he winks at them with easy will,
86His man grows rich, the knaves are the knaves still.
87But to the use I'll make of it; it shall serve
88To point me out a list of murderers,
89Agents for any villainy. Did I want
90Ten leash of courtezans, it would furnish me;
91Nay laundress three armies. That in so little paper
92Should lie th'undoing of so many men!
93'Tis not so big as twenty declarations.
94See the corrupted use some make of books:
95Divinity, wrested by some factious blood,
96Draws swords, swells battles, and o'erthrows all good.
97To fashion my revenge more seriously,
98Let me remember my dead sister's face:
99Call for her picture: no; I'll close mine eyes,
100And in a melancholic thought I'll frame

Enter ISABEL[L]A'S Ghost

101Her figure 'fore me. Now I ha't - - - how strong
102Imagination works! How she can frame
103Things which are not! Methinks she stands afore me;
104And by the quick idea of my mind,
105Were my skill pregnant, I could draw her picture.
106Thought, as a subtle juggler, makes us deem
107Things supernatural, which have cause
108Common as sickness. 'Tis my melancholy.
109How cam'st thou by thy death? - - - How idle am I
110To question my own idleness - - - Did ever
111Man dream awake till now? - - - Remove this object,
112Out of my brain with't: what have I to do
113With tombs, or death-beds, funerals, or tears,
114That have to meditate upon revenge?

[Exit Ghost]

115So now 'tis ended, like an old wives' story.
116Statesmen think often they see stranger sights
117Than madmen. Come, to this weighty business.
118My tragedy must have some idle mirth in't,
119Else it will never pass. I am in love,
120In love with Corombona, and my suit
121He writes. Thus halts yo her in verse. –
122I have done it rarely: O the fate of princes!
123I am so us'd to frequent flattery,
124That being alone I now flatter myself;
125But it will serve; 'tis seal'd. [calls offstage] Bear this

Enter SERVANT

126To th'house of convertites; and watch your leisure
127To give it to the hands of Corombona,
128Or to the matron, when some followers
129Exit SERVANT Of Brachiano may be by. Away!
130He that deals all by strength, his wit is shallow:
131When a man's head goes through, each limb will follow.
132The engine for my business, bold Count Lodowick;
133'Tis gold must such an instrument procure,
134With empty fist no man doth falcons lure.
135Brachiano, I am now fit for thy encounter.
136Like the wild Irish I'll ne'er think thee dead,
137Till I can play at football with thy head.
138Flectere si nequeo superos, Acheronta movebo.

Exit

[Act IV, Scene ii]

Enter the MATRON, and FLAMINEO

MATRON

1Should it be known the Duke hath such recourse
2To your imprison'd sister, I were like
3T'incur much damage by it.

FLAMINEO

T'incur much damage by it. Not a scruple.
4The pope lies on his death-bed, and their heads
5Are troubled now with other business
6Enter SERVANT Than guarding of a lady.

SERVANT

7Yonder's Flamineo in conferenceaparte
8With the Matrona.aparte
9[To the MATRON] Let me speak with you.
10I would entreat you yo deliver for me
11This letter to the fair Vittoria.

MATRON

12I shall sir.

Enter BRACHIANO

SERVANT

I shall sir. With all care and secrecy;
13Hereafter you shall know me, and receive
14Thanks for this courtesy.

[Exit]

FLAMINEO

Thanks for this courtesy. How now? What's that?

MATRON

15A letter.

FLAMINEO

A letter. To my sister: I'll see't delivered.

[Exit MATRON]

BRACHIANO

16What's that you read Flamineo?

FLAMINEO

What's that you read Flamineo? Look.

BRACHIANO

17[reads] Ha? To the most unfortunate his best respected Vittoria –
18Who was the messenger?

FLAMINEO

Who was the messenger? I know not.

BRACHIANO

19I'll open't, were't her heart. What's here subscribed –
20Florence? This juggling is gross and palpable.
21I have found out the conveyance; read it, read it.

FLAMINEO

22(Reads the letter) Your tears I'll turn to triumphs, be but mine.
23Your prop is fall'n; I pity that a vine
24Which princes heretofore have long'd to gather,
25Wanting supporters, now should fade and wither.
26Wine i'faith, my lord, with lees would serve his turn.
27Your sad imprisonment I'll soon uncharm,
28And with a princely uncontrolled arm
29Lead you to Florence, where my love and care
30Shall hang your wishes in my silver hair.
31A halter on his strange equivocation.
32Nor for my years return me the sad willow:
33Who prefer blossoms before fruit that's mellow?
34Rotten on my knowledge with lying too long i'th' bed-straw.
35And all the lines of age this line convinces:
36The gods neves wax old, no more do princes.
37A pox on't, rear it, let's have no more atheists for God's sake.

BRACHIANO

38Ud's death, I'll cut her into atomies
39And let th'irregular north-wind sweep her up
40And blow her int' his nostrils. Where's this whore?

FLAMINEO

41That –? what do you call her?

BRACHIANO

That –? what do you call her? O, I could be mad,
42Prevent the curs'd disease she'll bring me to,
43And tear my hair off. Where's this changeable stuff?

FLAMINEO

44O'er head and ears in water, I assure you,
45She is not for your wearing.

BRACHIANO

She is not for your wearing. In your pander!

FLAMINEO

46What me, my lord, am I your dog?

BRACHIANO

47A blood-hound: do you brave? do you stand me?

FLAMINEO

48Stand you? Let those that have diseases run;
49I need no plasters.

BRACHIANO

I need no plasters. Would you be kick'd?

FLAMINEO

50Would you have your neck broke?
51I tell you Duke, I am not in Russia;
52My shins must be kept whole.

BRACHIANO

My shins must be kept whole. Do you know me?

FLAMINEO

53O my lord! Methodically.
54As in this world there are degrees of evils:
55So in this world there are degrees of devils.
56You're a great Duke; I your poor secretary.
57I look now for a Spanish fig, or an Italian sallet daily.

BRACHIANO

58Pander, ply your convoy, and leave your prating.

FLAMINEO

All your kindness to me is like that miserable courtesy of Polyphemus to Ulysses; you reserve me to be devour'd last; you would dig turves out of my grave to feed your larks; that would be music to you. Come, I'll lead you to her.

BRACHIANO

Do you face me?

FLAMINEO

O sir I would not go before a politic enemy with my back towards him, though there were behind me a whirlpool.

Enter VITTORIA to BRACHIANO and FLAMINEO

BRACHIANO

62Can you read mistress? Look upon that letter;
63There are no characters nor hieroglyphics.
64You need no comment, I am grown your reciever;
65God's precious, you shall be a brave great lady,
66A stately and advanced whore.

VITTORIA

A stately and advanced whore. Say sir?

BRACHIANO

67Come, come, let's see your cabinet, discover
68Your treasury of love-letters. Death and furies,
69I'll see them all.

VITTORIA

I'll see them all. Sir, upon my soul,
70I have not any. Whence was this directed?

BRACHIANO

71Confusion on your politic ignorance!

[Gives her the letter]

72You are reclaimed, are you? I'll give you the bells
73And let you fly to the devil.

FLAMINEO

And let you fly to the devil. Ware hawk, my lord.

VITTORIA

74[reads] Florence! This is some treacherous plot, my lord.
75To me, he ne'er was lovely I protest,
76So much as in my sleep.

BRACHIANO

So much as in my sleep. Right: they are plots.
77Your beauty! O, ten thousand curses on't.
78How long have I beheld the devil in crystal?
79Thou hast led me, like an heathen sacrifice,
80With music, and with fatal yokes of flowers
81To my eternal ruin. Woman to man
82Is either a god or a wolf.

VITTORIA

Is either a god or a wolf. My lord.

BRACHIANO

Is either a god or a wolf. My lord. Away.
83We'll be as differing as two adamants;
84The one shall shun the other. What? Dost weep?
85Procure but ten of tht dissembling trade,
86Ye'ld furnish all the Irish funerals
87With howling, past wild Irish.

FLAMINEO

With howling, past wild Irish. Fie, my lord.

BRACHIANO

88That hand, that crused hand, which I have wearied
89With doting kisses! O my sweetest Duchess
90[to VITTORIA] Thy loose thoughts How lovely art thou now!
91Scatter like quicksilver; I was bewitch'd;
92For all the world speaks ill of thee.

VITTORIA

For all the world speaks ill of thee. No matter.
93I'll live so now I'll make that world recant
94And change her speeches. You did name your Duchess.

BRACHIANO

95Whose death God pardon.

VITTORIA

Whose death God pardon. Whose death God revenge
96On thee most godless Duke.

FLAMINEO

On thee most godless Duke. Now for two whirlwinds.

VITTORIA

97What have I gain'd by thee but infamy?
98Thou hast stain'd the spotless honour of my house,
99And frighted thence noble society:
100Like those, which sick o'th' palsy, and retain
101Ill-secnting foxes 'bout them, are still shunn'd
102By those of choicer nostrils. What do you call this house?
103Is this your palace? Did not the judge style it
104A house of penitent whores? Who sent me to it?
105Who hath the honour to advance Vittoria
106To this incontinent college? Is't not you?
107Is't not your high preferment? Go, go brag
108How many ladies you have undone, like me.
109Fare you well sir; let me hear no more of you.
110I had a limb corrupted to an ulcer,
111But I have cut it off: and now I'll go
112Weeping to heaven on crutches. For your gifts,
113I will return them all; and I do wish
114That I could make you full executor
115To all my sins. O that I could toss myself
116Into a grave as quickly: for all thou art worth
117I'll not shed one tear more; – I'll burst first.

She throws herself upon a bed

BRACHIANO

118I have drunk Lethe. Vittoria?
119My dearest happiness! Vittoria!
120What do you ail my love? Why do you weep?

VITTORIA

121Yes, I now weep poniards, do you see.

BRACHIANO

122Are not those matchless eyes mine?

VITTORIA

Are not those matchless eyes mine? I had rather
123They were not matches.

BRACHIANO

They were not matches. Is not this lip mine?

VITTORIA

124Yes: thus to bite it off, rather than give it thee.

FLAMINEO

125Turn to my lord, good sister.

VITTORIA

Turn to my lord, good sister. Hence you pander.

FLAMINEO

126Pander! Am I the author of your sin?

VITTORIA

127Yes. He's a base thief that a thief lets in.

FLAMINEO

128We're blown up, my lord, –

BRACHIANO

We're blown up, my lord, – Wilt thou hear me?
129Once to be jealous of thee is t'express
130That I will love thee everlastingly
131And never more be jealous.

VITTORIA

And never more be jealous. O thou fool,
132Whose greatness hath by much o'ergrown thy wit!
133What dar'st thou do, that I not dare to suffer,
134Excepting to be still thy whore? For that,
135In the sea's bottom sooner thou shalt make
136A bonfire.

FLAMINEO

A bonfire. O, no oaths for God's sake.

BRACHIANO

137Will you hear me?

VITTORIA

Will you hear me? Never.

FLAMINEO

138What a damn'd imposthume is a woman's will?
139[to BRACHIANO, aside] Fie, fie, my lord. Can nothing break it?
140Women are caught as you take tortoises,
141She must be turn'd on her back. -Sister, by this hand
142I am on your side.- Come, come, you have wrong'd her.
143What a strange credulous man were you, my lord,
144To think the Duke of Florence would love her?
145Will any mercer take another's ware
146When once 'tis tows'd and sullied? And, yet sister,
147How scurvily this frowardness becomes you!
148Young leverets stand not long; and women's anger
149Should, like their flight, procure a little sport;
150A full cry for a quarter of an hour;
151And then be put to th' dead quat.

BRACHIANO

And then be put to th' dead quat. Shall these eyes,
152Which have so long time dwelt upon your face,
153Be now put out?

FLAMINEO

Be now put out? No cruel landlady i'th' world,
154Which lends forth groats to broom-men, and takes use for them
155Would do't.
156Hand her, my lord, and kiss her: be not like
157A ferret to let go your hold with blowing.

BRACHIANO

158Let us renew right hands.

VITTORIA

Let us renew right hands. Hence.

BRACHIANO

159Never shall rage, or the forgetful wine,
160Make me commit like fault.

FLAMINEO

161Now you are i'th' way on't, follow't hard.

BRACHIANO

162Be thou at peace with me; let all the world
163Threaten the cannon.

FLAMINEO

Threaten the cannon. Mark his penitence.
164Best natures do commit the grossest fauls,
165When they're giv'n o'er to jealousy; as best wine
166Dying makes strongest vinegar. I'll tell you;
167The sea's more rough and raging than calm rivers,
168But nor so sweet nor wholesome. A quiet woman
169Is a still water under a great bridge.
170A man may shoot her safely.

VITTORIA

171O ye dissembling men!

FLAMINEO

O ye dissembling men! We suck'd that, sister,
172From women's breasts, in our first infancy.

VITTORIA

173To add misery to misery.

BRACHIANO

To add misery to misery. Sweetest.

VITTORIA

174Am I not low enough?
175Ay, ay, your good hearth gathers like a snowball
176Now your affection's cold.

FLAMINEO

Now your affection's cold. Ud's foot, it shall melt
177To a hearth again, or all the wine in Rome
178Shall run o'th' lees for't.

VITTORIA

179You hawk or dog should be rewarded better
180Than I have been. I'll speak not one word more.

FLAMINEO

181Stop her mouth,
182With a sweet kiss, my lord.
183So now the tide's turn'd the vessel's come about.
184He's a sweet armful. O we curl'd-hair'd men
185Are still most kind to women. This is well.

BRACHIANO

186That you should chide thus!

FLAMINEO

That you should chide thus! O, sir, your little chimneys
187Do ever cast most smoke. I sweat for you.
188Couple together with as deep a silence
189As did the Grecians in their wooden horse.
190My lord, supply your promises with deeds.
191You know that painted meat no hunger feeds.

BRACHIANO

192Stay-ingrateful Rome!

FLAMINEO

Stay-ingrateful Rome! Rome! It deserves
193To be call'd Barbary, for our villainous usage.

BRACHIANO

194Soft; the same project which the Duke of Florence,
195(Whether in love or gullery I know not)
196Laid down for her escape, will I pursue.

FLAMINEO

197And no time fitter than this night, my lord;
198The Pope being dead; and all the cardinals ent'red
199The convlave for th'electing a new Pope;
200The city in a great confusion;
201We may attire her in a page's suit,
202Lay her post-horse, take shipping, and amain
203For Padua.

BRACHIANO

204I'll instantly steal forth the Prince Giovanni,
205And make for Padua. You two with your old mother
206And young Marcello that attends on Florence,
207If you can work him to it, follow me.
208I will advance you all: for you Vittoria,
209Think of a duchess' title.

FLAMINEO

Think of a duchess' title. Lo you sister.

Stay, my lord; I'll tell you a tale. The crocodile, which lives in the river Nilus, hath a worm breeds i'th' teeth of't, which puts it to extreme anguish: a little bird, no bigger than a wren, is barber-surgeon to this crocodile; flies into the jaws of't; picks out the worm; and brings present remedy. The fish, glad of ease but ingrateful to her that did it, that the bird may not talk largely of her abroad for non-payment, closeth her chaps intending to swallow her, ando so put her to perpetual silence. But nature loathing such ingratitude, hath arm'd this bird with a quill or prick on the head, top o'th' which wounds the crocodile i'th' mouth; forceth her open her bloody prision; and away flies the pretty tooth-picker from her cruel patient.

BRACHIANO

211Your application is, I have not rewarded
212The service you have done me.

FLAMINEO

The service you have done me. No my lord;

You sister are the crocodile: you are blemish'd in your fame, my lord cures it. And thought the comparison hold not in every particle; yet observe, remember, what good the bird with the prick i'th' head hath done you; and scorn ingratitude.

214It may appear to some ridiculousaparte
215Thus to talk knave and madman; and sometimesaparte
216Come in with a dried sentence, stuff'd with sage.aparte
217But this allows my varying of shapes,aparte
218Knaves do grow great by being great men's apes.aparte

Exeunt

[Act IV, Scene iii]

Enter LODOVICO, GASPARO, and six Ambassadors. At another door [FRANCISCO] the Duke of Florence.

FRANCISCO

1So, my lord, I commented your diligence.
2Guard well the conclave, and, as the order ir,
3Let none have conference with the cardinals.

LODOVICO

4I shall, my lord. Room for the ambassadors!

GASPARO

5They're wondrous brave today: why do they wear
6These several habits?

LODOVICO

These several habits? O sir, they're knights
7Of several orders.
8That lord i'th' black cloak with the silver cross
9Is Knight of Rhodes; the next Knight of S. Michael;
10That of the Golden Fleece; the Frenchman there
11Knight of the Holy Ghost; my lord of Savoy
12Knight of th'Annunciation; the Englishman
13Is Knight of th'honoured Garter, dedicated
14Unto their saint, S. George. I could describe to you
15Their several institutions, with the laws
16Annexed to their orders; but that time
17Permits not such discovery.

FRANCISCO

Permits not such discovery. Where's Count Lodowick?

LODOVICO

18Here my lord.

FRANCISCO

Here my lord. 'Tis o'th' point of dinner time;
19Marshal the cardinals' service.

LODOVICO

Marshal the cardinals' service. Sir, I shall.

Enter SERVANTS with several dishes covered

20Stand, let me search your dish; who's this for?

SERVANT

21For my lord Cardinal Monticelso.

LODOVICO

22Who's this?

SERVANT

Who's this? For my Lord Cardinal of Bourbon.

FRENCH AMBASSADOR

23Why doth he search the dishes? To observe
24What meat is dress'd?

ENGLISH AMBASSADOR

What meat is dress'd? No sir, but to prevent
25Lest any letters should be convey'd in
26To brive or to solicit the advancement
27Of any cardinal. When first they enter
28'Tis lawful for the ambassadors of princes
29To enter with them, and to make their suit
30For any man their prince affecteth best;
31But after, till a general election,
32No man may speak with them.

LODOVICO

33You that attend on the lord cardinals
34Open the window, and receive their viands.

A CARDINAL

35You must return the servide; the lord cardinals
36Are busied 'bout electing of the Pope;
37They have given o'er scrutiny, and are fallen
38To admiration.

LODOVICO

To admiration. Away, away.

[Exeunt SERVANTS with dishes]

FRANCISCO

39I'll lay a thousand ducats you hear news
40Of a Pope presently. Hark; sure he's elected, –
41[The] Cardinal [of ARRAGON appears] on the terrace
42Behold! my lord of Arragon appears
43On the church battlements.

ARRAGON

Denuntio vobis gaudiu, magnum. Reverendissimus Cardinals LORENZO de MONTICELSO electus est in sedem apostolicam, et eligit nomen PAULUM quartum.

OMNES

Vivat Sanctus Pater Paulus Quartus.

[Enter SERVANT]

SERVANT

46Vittoria my lord –

FRANCISCO

Vittoria my lord – Well: what of her?

SERVANT

47Is fled the city –

FRANCISCO

Is fled the city – Ha?

SERVANT

Is fled the city – Ha? With Duke Brachiano.

FRANCISCO

48Fled? Where's the Prince Giovanni?

SERVANT

Fled? Where's the Prince Giovanni? Gone with his father.

FRANCISCO

49Let the Matrona of the convertites
50Be apprehended. Fled? O damnable!

[Exit SERVANT]

51How fortunate are my wishes. Why? 'Twas thisaparte
52I only labour'd. I did send the letteraparte
53T'instruct him what to do. Thy fame, fond Duke,aparte
54I first have poison'd; directed thee the wayaparte
55To marry a whore; what can be worse? This follows:aparte
56The hand must act to drown the passionate tongue,aparte
57I scorn to wear a sword and prate of wrong.aparte

Enter MONTICELSO in state

MONTICELSO

Concedimus vobis apostolicam benedictionem et remissionem peccatorum.

[FRANCISCO whispers to him]

59My lord reports Vittoria Corombona
60Is stol'n from forth the house of convertites
61By Brachiano, and they're fled the city.
62Now, though this be the first day of our seat,
63We cannot better please the divine power,
64That to sequester from the holy church
65These cursed persons. Make it therefore known,
66We do denounce excommunication
67Against them both: all that are theirs in Rome
68We likewise banish. Set on.

Exeunt [all except FRANCISCO and LODOVICO]

FRANCISCO

69Come dear Lodovico
70You have tane the sacrament to prosecute
71Th'intended murder.

LODOVICO

Th'intended murder. With all constancy.
72But, sir, I wonder you'll engage yourself
73In person, being a great prince.

FRANCISCO

In person, being a great prince. Divert me not.
74Most of this court are of my faction,
75And some are of my counsel. Noble friend,
76Our danger shall be 'like in this design;
77Give leave, part of the glory may be mine.

Enter MONTICELSO

MONTICELSO

78Why did the Duke of Florence with such care
79Labour your pardon? Say.

LODOVICO

80Italian beggars will resolve you that
81Who, begging of an alms, bid those they beg of
82Do good for their own sakes; or't may be
83He spreads his bounty with a sowing hand,
84Like kings, who many times give out of measure;
85Not for desert so much as for their pleasure.

MONTICELSO

86I know you're cunning. Come, what devil was that
87That you were raising?

LODOVICO

That you were raising? Devil, my lord?

MONTICELSO

That you were raising? Devil, my lord? I ask you
88How doth such compliment unto his knee,
89When he departed from you?

LODOVICO

When he departed from you? Why, my lord,
90He told me of a resty Barbary horse
91Which he would fain have brought to the career,
92The 'sault, and the ring-galliard. Now, my lord,
93I have a rare French rider.

MONTICELSO

I have a rare French rider. Take you heed:
94Lest the jade break your neck. Do you put me off
95With your wild horse-tricks? Sirrah you do lie.
96O, thou'rt a foul black cloud, and thou dost threat
97A violent storm.

LODOVICO

A violent storm. Storms are i'th' air, my lord;
98I am yoo low to storm.

MONTICELSO

I am yoo low to storm. Wretched creature!
99I know that thou art fashion'd for all ill,
100Like dogs, that once get blood, they'll ever kill.
101About some murder? Was't not?

LODOVICO

About some murder? Was't not? I'll not tell you;
102And yet I care not greatly if I do;
103Marry with this preparation. Holy Father,
104I come not yo you as an intelligencer,
105But as a penitent sinner. What I utter
106Is in confession merely; which you know
107Must never be reveal'd.

MONTICELSO

Must never be reveal'd. You have o'ertane me.

LODOVICO

108Sir I did love Brachiano's Duchess dearly;
109Or rather I pursued her with hot lust,
110Though she ne'er knew on't. She was poison'd;
111Upon my soul she was: for which I have sworn
112T'avenge her murder.

MONTICELSO

T'avenge her murder. To the Duke of Florence?

LODOVICO

113To him I have.

MONTICELSO

To him I have. Miserable creature!
114If thou persist in this, 'tis damnable.
115Dost thou imagine thou canst slide on blood
116And not be tained with a shameful fall?
117Or, like the black and melancholic yew-tree,
118Dost think to root thyself in dead men's graves,
119And yet to prosper? Instruction to thee
120Comes like sweet showers to over-hard'ned ground:
121They wet, but pierce not deep. And so I leave thee
122With all the Furies hanging 'bout thy neck,
123Till by thy penitence thou remove this evil,
124In conjuring from thy breast that cruel devil.

Exit MON[TICELSO]

LODOVICO

125I'll give it o'er. He says 'tis damnable:
126Besides I did expect his suffrage,
127By reason of Camillo's death.

Enter SERVANT and FRANCISCO

FRANCISCO

128Do you know that count?

SERVANT

Do you know that count? Yes, my lord.

FRANCISCO

129Bear him these thousand ducats to his lodging;
130Tell him the Pope hath sent them. Happily
131[Exit] That will confirm more than all the rest.

SERVANT

[Exit] That will confirm more than all the rest. Sir.

[SERVANT delievers purse of money to LODOVICO]

LODOVICO

132To me sir?

SERVANT

133His Holiness hath sent you a thousand crowns,
134And wills you, if you travel, to make him
135Your patron for intelligence.

LUDOVICO

Your patron for intelligence. His creature
136[Exit SERVANT] Ever to be commanded.
137Why now 'tis come about. He rail'd upon me;
138And yet these crowns were told out and laid ready,
139Before he knew my voyage. O the art,
140The modest form of greatness! that do sit
141Like brides at wedding dinners, with their looks turn'd
142From the least wanton jests, their puling stomach
143Sick of the modesty, when their thoughts are loose,
144Even acting of those hot and lustful sports
145Are to ensue about midnight: such his cunning!
146He sounds my depth thus with a golden plummet;
147I am doubty arm'd now. Now to th'act of blood;
148There's but three Furies found in spacious hell;
149But in a great man's breast three thousand dwell.

[Exit]
