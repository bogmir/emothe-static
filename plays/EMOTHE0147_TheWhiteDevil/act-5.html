[Act V, Scene i]

A passage over the stage of BRACHIANO, FLAMINEO, MARCELLO, HORTENSIO, [VITTORIA] COROMBONA, CORNELIA, ZANCHE and others

[Enter FLAMINEO and HORTENSIO]

FLAMINEO

1In all the weary minutes of my life,
2Day ne'er broke up till now. This marriage
3Confirms me happy.

HORTENSIO

Confirms me happy. 'Tis a good assurance.
4Saw you not yet the Moor that's come to court?

FLAMINEO

5Yes, and conferr'd with him i'th' Duke's closet;
6I have not seen a goodlier personage,
7Nor ever talk'd with man better experienc'd
8In state affairs or rudiments of war.
9He hath by report serv'd the Venetian
10In Candy these twice seven years, and been chief
11In many a bold design.

HORTENSIO

In many a bold design. What are those two
12That bear him company?

FLAMINEO

Two noblemen of Hungary, that living in the emperor's service as commanders, eight years since, contrary to the expectation of all the court ent'red into religion, into the strict order of Capuchins: but being not well settled in their undertaking they left their order and returned to court: for which being after troubled in conscience, they vowed their service agaist the enemies of Christ; went to Malta; were there knighted; and in their return back, at this great solemnity, they are resolved for ever to forsake the world, and settle themselves here in a house of Capuchins in Padua.

HORTENSIO

'Tis strange.

FLAMINEO

One thing makes it so. They have vowed for ever to wear next their bare bodies those coats of mail they served in.

HORTENSIO

16Hard penance. Is the Moor a Christian?

FLAMINEO

17He is.

HORTENSIO

18Why proffers he his service to our Duke?

FLAMINEO

19Because he understands there's like to grow
20Some wars between us and the Duke of Florence,
21In which he hopes employment.
22I never saw one in a stern bold look
23Wear more command, nor in a lofty phrase
24Express more knowing, or more deep contempt
25Of our slight airy courtiers. He talks
26As if he had travell'd all the princes' courts
27Of Christendom; in all things strives t'express
28That all that should dispute with him may know,
29Glories, like glow-worms, afar off shine bright
30But look'd to near, have neither heat nor light.
31The Duke!

Enter BRACHIANO, [FRANCISCO, Duke of] Florence disguised like Mulinassar; LODOVICO, ANTONELLI, GASPARO, FERNESE having their swords and helmets.

BRACHIANO

32You are nobly welcome. We have heard at full
33Your honourable service 'gainst the Turk.
34To you, brave Mulinassar, we assign
35A competent pension: and are inly sorrow,
36The vows of those two worthy gentlemen
37Make them incapable of our proffer'd bounty.
38Your wish is you may leave your warlike swords
39For monuments in our chapel. I accept it
40As a great honour done me, and must crave
41Your leave to furnish out our Duchess' revels.
42Only one thing, as the last vanity
43You e'er shall view, deny me not to stay
44To see a barriers prepar'd tonight;
45You shall have private standings. It hath pleas'd
46The great ambassadors of several princes
47In their return from Rome to their own countries
48To grace our marriage, and to honour me
49With such a kind of sport.

FRANCISCO

With such a kind of sport. I shall persuade them
50To stay, my lord.

BRACHIANO

To stay, my lord. Set on there to the presence.

Exeunt BRACHIANO, FLAMINEO and [HORTENSIO]

LODOVICO

51Noble my lord, most fortunately welcome,

The conspirators here embrace

52You have our vows seal'd with the sacrament
53To second your attempts.

GASPARO

To second your attempts. And all things ready.
54He could not have invented his own ruin,
55Had he despair'd, with more propriety.

LODOVICO

56You would not take my way.

FRANCISCO

You would not take my way. 'Tis better ordered.

LODOVICO

57T'have poison'd his prayer book, or a pair of beads,
58The pommel of his saddle, his looking-glass,
59Or th'handle of his racket. O that, that!
60That while he had been bandying at tennis,
61He might have sworn himself to hell, and struck
62His soul into the hazard! O my lord!
63I would have our plot be ingenious,
64And have it hereafter recorded for example
65Rather than borrow example.

FRANCISCO

Rather than borrow example. There's no way
66More speeding than this thought on.

LODOVICO

More speeding than this thought on. On then.

FRANCISCO

67And yet methinks that this revenge is poor,
68Because it steals upon him like a thief;
69To habe tane him by the cask in a pitch'd field,
70Led him to Florence!

LODOVICO

Led him to Florence! It had been rare. -And there
71Have crown'd him with a wreath of stinking garlic,
72T'have shown the sharpness of his government,
73And rankness of his lust. Flamineo comes.

Exeunt LODOVICO, ANTONELLI [and GASPARO]

Enter FLAMINEO, MARCELLO and ZANCHE

MARCELLO

74Why doth this devil haunt you? Say.

FLAMINEO

Why doth this devil haunt you? Say. I know not.
75For by this light I do not conjure for her.
76'Tis not so great a cunning as men think
77To raise the devil: for here's one up already;
78The greatest cunning were to lay him down.

MARCELLO

79She is your shame.

FLAMINEO

She is your shame. I prithee pardon her.
80In faith you see, women are like to burs;
81Where their affection throws them, there they'll stick.

ZANCHE

82That is my countryman, a goodly person;
83When he's at leisure I'll discourse with him
84In our own language.

FLAMINEO

In our own language. Exit ZANCHE I beseech you do.
85How is't brave soldier? O that I had seen
86Some of your iron days! I pray relate
87Some of your service to us.

FRANCISCO

'Tis a ridiculous thing for a man to be his own chronicle; I did never wash my mouth with mine own praise for fear of getting a stinking breath.

MARCELLO

You're too stoical. The Duke will expect other discourse from you.

FRANCISCO

I shall never flatter him, I have studied man too much to do that. What difference is between the Duke and I? No more than between two bricks; all made of one clay. Only 't may be one in plac'd on the top of a turret; the other in the bottom of a well by mere chance; if I were plac'd as high as the Duke, I should stick as fast; make as fair a show; and bear out weater equally.

FLAMINEO

If this soldier had a patent to beg in churches, then he would tell them stories.

MARCELLO

I have been a soldier too.

FRANCISCO

How have you thriv'd?

MARCELLO

Faith, poorly.

FRANCISCO

That's the mistery of peace. Only outsiders are then respected.As ships seem very great upon the river, which show very little upon the seas: so some men i'th' court seem Colossues in a chamber, who if they came into the fiel would appear pitiful pigmes.

FLAMINEO

Give me a fair room yet hung with arras, and some great cardinal to lug me by th'ears as his endeared minion.

FRANCISCO

And thou may'st do, -the devil knows what villainy.

FLAMINEO

And safely.

FRANCISCO

Right; you shall see in the country in harvest time, pigeons, thought they destroy never so much corn, the farmer dare not present the fowling-piece to them! Why? Because they belong to the lord of the manor; whilst your poor sparrows that belong to the Lord of heaven, they go to the pot for't.

FLAMINEO

I will now give you some politic instruction. The Duke says he will give you pension; that's but bare promise: get it under his hand. For I have know men that have come for serving against the Turk; for three or four months they have had pension to boy them new wooden legs and fresh plasters; but after 'twas not to be had. And this miserable courtesy shows, as if a tormentor should give hot cordial drinks to one three-quarters dead o'th' rack, only to fetch the miserable soul again to endure more dog-days.

Enter HORTENSIO, a YOUNG LORD, ZANCHE and two more

101How now, gallants; what, are they ready for the barriers?

[Exit FRANCISCO]

YOUNG LORD

Yes: the lords are putting on their armour.

HORTENSIO

What's he?

FLAMINEO

A new upstart: one that swears like a falc'ner, and will lie in the Duke's ear day by day like a maker of almanacs; and yet I knew him since he came to th'court smell worse of sweat than an under-tennis-court-keeper.

HORTENSIO

Look you, yonder's your sweet mistress.

FLAMINEO

Thou art my sworn brother; I'll tell thee, I do love that Moor, that witch, very constrainedly: she knows some of my villainy; I do love her, just as a man holds a wolf by the ears. But for fear of turning upon me, and pulling out my throat, I would let her go to the devil.

HORTENSIO

I hear she claims marriage of thee.

FLAMINEO

'Faith, I made to her some such dark promise, and in seeking to fly from't I run on, like a frighted dog with a bottle at's tail, that fain would bite it off and yet dares not look behind him. [to ZANCHE] Now my precious gipsy!

ZANCHE

Ay, your love to me rather cools than heats.

FLAMINEO

Marry, I am the sounder lover; we have many wenches about the town heat too fast.

HORTENSIO

What do you think of these perfum'd gallants then?

FLAMINEO

113Their satin cannot save them. I am confident
114They have a certain spice of the disease.
115For they that sleep with dogs, shall rise with fleas.

ZANCHE

116Believe it! A little painting and gay clothes
117Make you loathe me.

FLAMINEO

How? Love a lady for painting or gay apparel? I'll unkennel one example more of thee. Æsop had a foolish dog that let go the flesh to catch the shadow. I would have courtiers be better diners.

ZANCHE

You remember your oaths.

FLAMINEO

Lovers' oaths are like mariners' prayers, uttered in extremity; but when the tempest is o'er, and that the vessel leaves tumbling, they fall from protesting to drinking. And yet amongst gentlemen protesting and drinking go together, and agree as well as shoemakers and Westphalia bacon. They are both drawers on: for drink draws on protestation; and protestation draws on more drink. Is not this discourse better no than the morality of your sunburnt gentleman?

Enter CORNELIA

CORNELIA

121Is this your perch, you haggard? Fly to th'stews.

[Strikes ZANCHE]

FLAMINEO

122You should be clapp'd by th'heels now: strike i'th'court?

[Exit CORNELIA]

ZANCHE

123She's good for nothing but to make her maids
124Catch cold o'nights; they dare not usa a bedstaff,
125For fear f her light fingers.

MARCELLO

For fear f her light fingers. You're a strumpet.
126An impudent one.

FLAMINEO

An impudent one. Why do you kick her? Say,
127Do you think that she's like a walnut-tree?
128Must she be cudgell'd ere she bear good fruit?

MARCELLO

129She brags that you shall marry her.

FLAMINEO

She brags that you shall marry her. What then?

MARCELLO

130I had rather she were pitch'd upon a stake
131In some new-seeded garden, to affright
132Her fellow crows thence.

FLAMINEO

Her fellow crows thence. You're a boy, a fool,
133Be guardian to your hound, I am of age.

MARCELLO

134If I take her near you I'll cut her throat.

FLAMINEO

135With a fan of feathers?

MARCELLO

With a fan of feathers? And for you, I'll whip
136This folly from you.

FLAMINEO

This folly from you. Are you choleric?
137I'll purgue't with rhubarb.

HORTENSIO

I'll purgue't with rhubarb. O your brother!

FLAMINEO

I'll purgue't with rhubarb. O your brother! Hang him.
138He wrongs me most that ought t'offend me least.
139[to MARCELLO] I do suspect my mother play'd foul play
140When she conceiv'd thee.

MARCELLO

When she conceiv'd thee. Now by all my hopes,
141Like the two slaught'red sons of Œdipus,
142The very flames of our affection
143Shall turn two ways. Those words I'll make thee answer
144With thy heart blood.

FLAMINEO

With thy heart blood. Do; like the gesses in the progress,
145You know where you shall find me, –

MARCELLO

You know where you shall find me, – Very good.

[Exit FLAMINEO]

146And thou beest a noble friend, bear him my sword,
147And bid him fit the length on't.

YOUNG LORD

And bid him fit the length on't. Sir I shall

[Exeunt all but ZANCHE]

Enter FRANCISCO the Duke of Florence

ZANCHE

148He comes. Hence petty thought of my disgrace!aparte
149I ne'er lov'd my complexion till now,
150Cause I may boldly say without a blush,
151I love you.

FRANCISCO

I love you. Your love is untimely sown;
152There's a Spring at Michaelmas, but 'tis but a faint one.
153I am sunk in years, and I have vowed never yo marry.

ZANCHE

Alas! poor maids get more lovers than husbands. Yet you may mistake my wealth. For, as when ambassadors are sent to congratulate princes, there's commonly sent along with them a rich present; so that though the prince like not the ambassador's person nor words, yet he likes well of the presentment. So I may come to you in the same manner, and be better loved for my dowry than my virtue.

FRANCISCO

I'll think on the motion.

ZANCHE

Do, I'll now detain you no longer. At your better leisure I'll tell you things shall startle your blood.

157Nor blame me that this passion I reveal;

[Act V, Scene ii]

Enter MARCELLO and CORNELIA [and a PAGE, who remains in the background]

CORNELIA

1I hear a whispering all about the court,
2You are to fight; who is your opposite?
3What is the quarrel?

MARCELLO

What is the quarrel? 'Tis an idle rumour.

CORNELIA

4Will you dissemble? Sure you do not well
5To fright me thus; you never look thus pale,
6But when you are most angry. I do charge you
7Upon my blessing; –nay I'll call the Duke,
8And he shall school you.

MARCELLO

And he shall school you. Publish not a fear
9Which would convert to laughter; 'tis not so.
10Was not this crucifix my father's?

CORNELIA

Was not this crucifix my father's? Yes.

MARCELLO

11I have heard you say, giving my brother suck,
12Enter FLAMINEO He took the crucifix between his hands,
13And broke a limb off.

CORNELIA

And broke a limb off. Yes: but 'tis mended.

FLAMINEO

14I have brought your weapon back.

FLAMINEO runs MARCELLO through

CORNELIA

I have brought your weapon back. Ha, O my horror!

MARCELLO

15You have brought it home indeed.

CORNELIA

You have brought it home indeed. Help! Oh he's murdered.

FLAMINEO

16Do you turn your gall up? I'll to sanctuary,
17And send a surgeon to you

[Exit]

Enter CARL[O,] HORT[ENSIO,] PEDRO

HORTENSIO

And send a surgeon to you How? O'th' ground?

MARCELLO

18O mother now remember what I told
19Of breaking off the crucifix: farewell.
20There are some sins which heaven doth duly punish
21In a whole family. This it is to rise
22By all dishonest means. Let all men know
23That tree shall long time keep a steady foot
24Whose branches spread no wider than the root.

CORNELIA

25O my perpetual sorrow!

HORTENSIO

O my perpetual sorrow! Virtuous Marcello.
26He's dead: pray leave him lady; come, you shall.

CORNELIA

Alas he is not dead: he's in a trance. Why here's nobody shall get anything by his death. Let me call him again for God's sake.

LODOVICO

I would you were deceiv'd.

CORNELIA

O you abuse me, you abuse, me, you abuse me. How many have gone away this for lack of tendance; rear up's head, rear up's head. His bleeding inward will kill him.

HORTENSIO

You see he is departed.

CORNELIA

Let me come to him; give me him as he is; if he be turn'd to earth; let me but give him one hearty kiss, and you shall put us both into one coffin: fetch a looking-glass, see if his breath will not stain it; or pull out some feathers from my pillow, and lay them to his lips, -will you lose him for a little pains-taking?

HORTENSIO

Your kindest office is to pray for him.

CORNELIA

Alas! I would not pray for him yet. He may live lo lay me i'th' ground, and pray for me, if you'll let me come to him.

[Act V, Scene iii]

FLAMINEO

BRACHIANO

FLAMINEO

BRACHIANO

4Enter ARMOURER O my brain's on fire,
5The helmet is poison'd.

ARMOURER

The helmet is poison'd. My lord upon my soul –

BRACHIANO

6[Exit ARMOURER, guarded] Away with him to torture.
7There are some great ones that have hand in this,
8And near about me.

VITTORIA

And near about me. O my loved lord; poison'd?

FLAMINEO

9Remove the bar: here's unfortunate revels,
10Ent[er] 2 PHYSICIANS Call the physicians; a plague upon you;
11We have too much of your cunning here already.

BRACHIANO

13Oh I am gone already: the infection
14Fllies to the brain and hearth. O thou strong heart!
15There's such a covenant 'tween the world and it,
16They're loth to break.

GIOVANNI

They're loth to break. O my most loved father!

BRACHIANO

17Remove the boy away.
18Where's this good woman? Had I infinite worlds
19They were too little for thee. Must I leave thee?
20What say yon screech-owls, is the venom mortal?

PHYSICIANS

21Most deadly.

BRACHIANO

Most deadly. Most corrupted politic hangman!
22You kill without book; but your art to save
23Fails you as a oft as great men's needy friends.
24I that have given life to offending slaves
25And wretched murderers, have I not power
26To lengthen mine own a twelvemonth?
27[to VITTORIA] Do not kiss me, for I shall poison thee.
28This unction is sent from the great Duke of Florence.

FRANCISCO

29Sir be of confort.

BRACHIANO

30O thou soft natural death, that art joint-twin
31To sweetest slumber: no rought-bearded comet
32Stares on thy mild departure; the dull owl
33Beats not against thy casement: the hoarse wolf
34Scents not thy carrion. Pity winds thy corse,
35Whilist horror waits on pinces.

VITTORIA

Whilist horror waits on pinces. I am lost for ever.

BRACHIANO

36How miserable a thing it is to die
37'Mongst women howling!

'Mongst women howling! What are those?

FLAMINEO

'Mongst women howling! What are those? Franciscans.
38They have brought the extreme unction.

BRACHIANO

39On pain of death, let no man name death to me,
40It is a word infinitely terrible.
41Withdraw into our cabinet.

Exeunt omnes praeter FRANCISCO and FLAMINEO

FLAMINEO

To see what solitariness is about dying princes. As heterofore they have unpeopled towns; divorc'd friends, and made great houses unhospitable: so now, O justice! Where are their flatterers now? Flatterers are but the shadows of princes' bodies, the least thrick could makes them invisible.

FRANCISCO

There's great moan made for him.

FLAMINEO

'Faith, for some few hours salt water will run most plentifully in every office o'th'court. But believe it; most of them do but weep over their stepmothers' graves.

FRANCISCO

How mean you?

FLAMINEO

Why? They dissemble, as some men do that live within compass o'th' verge.

FRANCISCO

Come you have thriv'd well under him.

FLAMINEO

'Faith, like a wolf in a woman's breast; I have been fed with poultry: but money, understand me, I had as good a will to cozen him, as e'er an officer of them all. But I had not cunning enough to do it.

FRANCISCO

What did'st thou think of him? 'Faith speak freely.

FLAMINEO

He was a kind of statesman, that would sooner have reckon'd how many cannon-bullets he had discharged against a town, to count his expense that way, than how many of this valiant and deserving subjects he lost before it.

FRANCISCO

O speak well of the Duke.

FLAMINEO

I have done. Wilt hear some of my court wisdom? Enter LODOVICO To reprehend princes is dangerous: and to over-commend dome of them is palpable lying.

FRANCISCO

54How is it with the Duke?

LODOVICO

64Upon your sister, till the Prince arrive
65At mature age.

FLAMINEO

At mature age. There's some good luck in that yet.

FRANCISCO

66See here he comes.

VITTORIA

67O my good lord!

103All my infected blood out.
104Are you ready?

VITTORIA and ZANCHE

Are you ready? Ready.

FLAMINEO

105Whither shall I go now? O Lucian thy ridiculous purgatory!
106to fin Alexander the Great cobbling shoes, Pompey tagging
107points, and Julius Caesar making hair buttons, Hannibal
108selling blacking, and August crying garlic, Charlemagne
109selling lists by the dozen, and King Pippin crying apples in
110a cart drawn with one horse.
111Whether I resolve to fire, earth, water, air,
112Or all the elements by scruples, I know not
113Nor greatly care. — Shoot, shoot,
114Of all deaths the violent death is best,
115For from ourselves it steals ourselves so fast
116The pain once apprehended is quite past.

They shoot and run to him and tread upon him

VITTORIA

117What, are you dropp’d?

FLAMINEO

118I am mix’d with earth already. As you are noble
119Perform you vows, and bravely follow me.

VITTORIA

120Whither? to hell?

ZANCHE

Whither? to hell? To most assured damnation.

VITTORIA

121O thou most cursed devil.

ZANCHE

O thou most cursed devil. Thou art caught —

VITTORIA

122In thine own engine; I thread the fire out
123That would have been my ruin.

FLAMINEO

Will you be perjur’d? What a religious oath was Styx that the gods never durst swear by and violate? O that we had such an oath to minister, and to be so well kept in our courts of justice.

VITTORIA

125Think whither thou art going.

ZANCHE

Think whither thou art going. And remember
126What villainies thou hast acted.

VITTORIA

What villainies thou hast acted. This thy death
127Shall make me like a blazing ominous star, —
128Look up and tremble.

FLAMINEO

Look up and tremble. O I am caught with a springe!

VITTORIA

129You see the fox comes many times short home,
130‘Tis here prov’d true.

FLAMINEO

‘Tis here prov’d true. Kill’d with a couple of braches

VITTORIA

131No fitter offering for the infernal Furies
132Than one in whom they reign’d while he was living.

FLAMINEO

133O the way’s dark and horrid! I cannot see,
134Shall I have no company?

VITTORIA

Shall I have no company? O yes thy sins
135Do rin before thee to fetch fire from hell,
136To light thee thither.

FLAMINEO

To light thee thither. O I smell soot,
137Most stinking soot, the chimney is a-fire,
138My liver’s parboil’d like Scotch holy-bread;
139There’s a plumber, laying pipes in my guts, it scalds;
140Wilt thou outlive me?

ZANCHE

Wilt thou outlive me? Yes, and drive a stake
141Through thy body; for we’ll give it out,
142Thou didst this violence upon thyself.

FLAMINEO

143O cunning devils! now I have try’d your love,
144And doubled all your reaches. I am not wounded:

FLAMINEO riseth

145The pistols held no bullets: ‘Twas a plot
146To prove your kindness to me; and I live
147To punish your ingratitude; I knew
148One time or other you would find a way
149To give me a strong potion. O men
150That lie upon your death-beds, and are haunted
151With howling wives, ne’er trust them; they’ll remarry
152Ere the worm pierce your winding sheet; ere the spider
153Make a thin curtain for your epitaphs.

How cunning you were to discharge! Do you practice at the Artillery Yard? Trust a woman? Never, never; Brachiano be my president: we lay our souls to pawn to the devil for a little pleasure, and a woman makes the bill of sale. That ever man should marry! For one Hypermnestra that sav’d her lord and husband, forty-nine of her sisters cut their throats all in one night. There was a shoal of virtuous horseleeches.

155Here are two other instruments.

Enter LOD[OVICO], GASP[ARO, disguised as] CARLO, PEDRO [with others]

VITTORIA

Here are two other instruments. Help, help!

FLAMINEO

156What noise is that? hah? False keys I’th’ court.

LODOVICO

157We have brought you a masque.

FLAMINEO

We have brought you a masque. A matachin it seems,
158By your drawn swords. Churchmen turn’d revellers.

CONSPIRATORS

159Isabella, Isabella!

[They throw off their disguises]

LODOVICO

160Do you know us now?

FLAMINEO

Do you know us now? Lodovico and Gasparo.

LODOVICO

161Yes, and that Moor the Duke gave pension to
162Was the great Duke of Florence.

VITTORIA

Was the great Duke of Florence. O we are lost.

FLAMINEO

163You shall not take justice from forth my hands;
164O let me kill her. ——— I’ll cut my safety
165Through your coats of steel. Fate’s a spaniel,
166We cannot beat it from us. What remains now?
167Let all that do ill take this president:
168Man may his fate foresee, but not prevent.
169And od all axioms this shall win the prize,
170‘Tis better to be fortunate than wise.

GASPARO

171Bind him to the pillar.

VITTORIA

Bind him to the pillar. O your gentle pity!
172I have seen a blackbird that would sooner fly
173To a man’s bosom, than to stay the gripe
174Of the fierece sparrow-hawk.

GASPARO

Of the fierece sparrow-hawk. Your hope deceives you.

VITTORIA

175If Florence be I’th’ court, would he would kill me.

GASPARO

176Fool! Princes give rewards with their own hands,
177But death or punishment by the hands of others.

LODOVICO

178Sirrah you once did strike me; I’ll strike you
179Into the centre.

FLAMINEO

180Thou’lt do it like a hangman; a base hangman;
181Not like a noble fellow, for thou seest
182I cannot strike again.

LODOVICO

I cannot strike again. Dost laugh?

FLAMINEO

183Wouldst have me die, as I was born, in whining?

GASPARO

184Recommend yourself to heaven.

FLAMINEO

185No I will carry mine won commendations thither.

LODOVICO

186Oh could I kill you forty times a day
187And use’t four tear together; ‘twere too little:
188Nought grieve’s but that you are too few to feed
189The famine of our vengeance. What dost think on?

FLAMINEO

190Nothing; of nothing: leave thy idle questions;
191I am I’th’way to study a long silence
192To prate were idle; I remember nothing.
193There’s nothing of so infinite vexation
194As man’s own thoughts.

LODOVICO

As man’s own thoughts. O thou glorious strumpet,
195Could I divide thy breath from this pure air
196When’t leaves thy body, I would suck it up
197And breathe’t upon some dunghill.

VITTORIA

And breathe’t upon some dunghill. You, my death’s-man;
198Methinks thou dost not look horrid enough,
199Thou hast too good a face to be a hangman;
200If thou be, do thy office in right form;
201Fall down upon thy knees and ask forgiveness.

LODOVICO

202O thou hast been a most prodigious comet,
203But I’ll cut off your train: kill the Moor first.

VITTORIA

204You shall not kill her first. Behold my breast,
205I will be waited on in death; my servant
206Shall never go before me.

GASPARO

207Are you so brave?

VITTORIA

Are you so brave? Yes I shall welvome death
208As princes do some great ambassadors;
209I’ll meet thy weapon half way.

LODOVICO

I’ll meet thy weapon half way. Thou dost tremble,
210Methinks fear should dissolve thee into air.

VITTORIA

211O thou art deceiv’d, I am too true a woman:
212Conceit can never kill me: I’ll tell thee what;
213I will not in my death shed one base tear,
214Or if look pale, for want of blood, not fear.

GASPARO

215Thou art my task, black fury.

ZANCHE

Thou art my task, black fury. I have blood
216As red as either of theirs: wilt drink some?
217‘Tis good for the falling sickness. I am proud
218Death cannot alter my complexion,
219For I shall ne’er look pale.

LODOVICO

For I shall ne’er look pale. Strike, strike,
220[They strike] With a joint motion.

VITTORIA

[They strike] With a joint motion. ‘Twas a manly blow.
221The next thou giv’st, murder some sicking infant,
222And then thou wilt be famous.

FLAMINEO

And then thou wilt be famous. O what blade is’t?
223A Toledo, or an English fox?
224I ever thought a cutler should distinguish
225The cause of my death, rather than a doctor.
226Search my wound deeper: tent it with the steel
227That made it.

VITTORIA

228O my greatest sin lay in my blood.
229Now my blood pays for’t.

FLAMINEO

Now my blood pays for’t. Th’art a noble sister,
230I love thee now; if woman do breed man
231She ought to teach him manhood. Fare thee well.
232Know many glorious women that are fam’d
233For masculine virtue, have been vicious
234Only a happier silence did betide them.
235She hath no faults, who that the art to hide them.

VITTORIA

236My soul, like to a ship in a black storm,
237Is driven I know not whither.

FLAMINEO

Is driven I know not whither. Then cast anchor.
238Prosperity doth bewitch men seeming clear,
239But seas do laugh, show white, when rocks are near.
240We cease to grieve, cease to be Fortune’s slaves,
241Nay cease to die by diyng. [to ZANCHE] Art thou gone?
242[to VITTORIA] And thou so near the bottom? False report
243Which says that women vie with the nine Muses
244For nine tough durable lives. I do not look
245Who went before, nor who shall follow me;
246No, at myself I will begin and end.
247While we look up to heaven we confound
248Knowledge with knowledge. O I am in a mist.

VITTORIA

249O happy they that never saw the court,
250VITTORIA dies Nor ever knew great man but by report.

FLAMINEO

251I recover like a spent taper, for a flash
252And instantly go out.
253Let all that belong to great men remember th’old wives’
254tradition, to be like the lions I’th’ Tower on Candlemas day,
255to mourn if the sun shine, for fear of the pitiful remainder of
256winter to come.
257‘Tis well yet there’s some goodness in my death,
258My life was a black charnel. I have cought
259An everlasting cold. I have lost my voice
260Most irrecoverably. Farewell glorious villains,
261This busy trade of life appears most vain,
262Since rest breeds rest, where all seek pain by pain.
263Let no harash flattering bells resound my knell,
264Strike thunder, and strike loud to my farewell.

He dies

Enter [AMBASSADORS] and GIOVANNI. [GUARDS follow.]

ENGLISH AMBASSADOR

265This way, this way, break ope the doors, this way.

LODOVICO

266Ha, are we betray’d?
267Why then let’s constantly die all together,
268And having finish’d this most noble deed,
269Defy the worst of fate; net fear to bleed.

ENGLISH AMBASSADOR

270Keep back the Prince: shoot, shoot —

[GUARDS shoot at conspirators]

LODOVICO

Keep back the Prince: shoot, shoot — O I am wounded.
271I fear I shall be tane.

GIOVANNI

I fear I shall be tane. You bloody villiains,
272By what authority have you commited
273This massacre?

LODOVICO

This massacre? By thine.

GIOVANNI

This massacre? By thine. Mine?

LODOVICO

This massacre? By thine. Mine? Yes, thy uncle,
274Which is a part of thee enjoin’d us to’t:
275Thou know’st me I am sure, I am Count Lodowick,
276And thy most noble uncle in disguise
277Was last night in thy court.

GIOVANNI

Was last night in thy court. Ha!

GASPARO

Was last night in thy court. Ha! Yes, that Moor
278Thy father chose his pensioner.

GIOVANNI

Thy father chose his pensioner. He turn’d murderer?
279Away with them to prision, and to torture;
280All that have hand in this, shall taste our justice,
281As I hope heaven.

LODOVICO

As I hope heaven. I do glory yet
282That I can call this act mine own. For my part,
283The rack, the gallows, and the torturing wheel
284Shall be but sound sleeps to me; here’s my rest:
285I limb’d this night-piece and it was my best.

GIOVANNI

286Remove the bodies; see my honoured lord,
287What use you ought make of their punishment.
288Let guilty men remember their black deeds
289Do lean on crutches, made of slender reeds.

[Exeunt]
