[Act III, Scene i]

Enter FRANCISCO, and MONTICELSO, their CHANCELLOR and REGISTER

FRANCISCO

1You have dealt discreetly to obtain the presence
2Of all the grave lieger ambassadors
3To hear Vittoria’s trial.

MONTICELSO

To hear Vittoria’s trial. ‘Twas not ill,
4For sir you know we have nought but circumstances
5To charge her with, about her husband’s death;
6Their approbation therefore to the proofs
7Of her black lust, shall make her infamous
8To all our neighbouring kingdoms. I wonder
9If Brachiano will be here.

FRANCISCO

If Brachiano will be here. O fie,
10‘Twere impudence too palpable.

[Exeunt]

Enter FLAMINEO and MARCELLO guarded, and a LAWYER

LAWYER

What, are you in by the week? So -I will not try now whether thy wit be close prisoner: methinks none should sit upon thy sister but old whore-masters, —

FLAMINEO

Or cuckholds, for your cuckhold is your most terrible tickler of lechery: whore-masters would serve, for none are judges at tilting, but those that have been old tilters.

LAWYER

My lord Duke and she have been very private.

FLAMINEO

Your are a dull ass; ‘tis threat’ned they have been very Public.

LAWYER

If it can be proved they have but kiss’d one another.

FLAMINEO

What then?

LAWYER

My lord cardinal will ferret them, —

FLAMINEO

A cardinal I hope will not catch conies.

LAWYER

For to sow kisses (mark what I say), to sow kisses, is to reap lechery, and I am sure a woman that will endure kissing is half won.

FLAMINEO

True, her upper part by that rule; if you will win her nether

21part too, you know what follows.

LAWYER

22Hark, the ambassadors are lighted, —

FLAMINEO

23I do put on this feigned garb of mirthaparte
24To gull suspicion.aparte

MARCELLO

To gull suspicion. O my unfortunate sister!
25I would my dagger’s point had cleft her hearth
26When she first saw Brachiano. You ‘tis said,
27Were made his engine, and his stalking-horse
28To undo my sister.

FLAMINEO

To undo my sister. I made a kind of path
29To her and mine own preferment.

MARCELLO

To her and mine own preferment. Your ruin.

FLAMINEO

30Hum! Thou art a soldier,
31Followest the great Duke, feedest his victories,
32As witches do their serviceable spirits,
33Even with thy prodigal blood; what hast got?
34But like the wealth of capitains, a poor handful,
35Which in thy palm thou bear’st, as men hold water;
36Seeking to gripe it fast, the frail reward
37Steals throught thy fingers.

MARCELLO

Steals throught thy fingers. Sir, —

FLAMINEO

Steals throught thy fingers. Sir, — Thou hast scarce maintenance
38To keep thee in fresh chamois.

MARCELLO

To keep thee in fresh chamois. Brother!

FLAMINEO

To keep thee in fresh chamois. Brother! Hear me, —
39And thus when we have even poured ourselves
40Into great fights, for their ambition
41Or idle spleen, how shall we find reward,
42But as we seldom find the mistletoe
43Sacred to physic on the builder oak
44Without a mandrake by it, so in our quest of gain.
45Alas the poorest of their forc’d dislikes
46At a limb proffers, but at hearth it strikes:
47This is lamented doctrine.

MARCELLO

This is lamented doctrine. Come, come.

FLAMINEO

48When age shall turn thee
49White as a blooming hawthorn, —

MARCELLO

White as a blooming hawthorn, — I’ll interrupt you.
50For love of virtue bear an honest hearth,
51And stride over every politic respect,
52Which where they most advance they most infect.
53Were I your father, as I am your brother,
54I should not be ambitious to leave you
55A better patrimony.

Enter Savoy [Ambassador]

FLAMINEO

A better patrimony. I’ll think on’t, —
56The lord ambassadors.

Here there is a passage of the lieger Ambassadors over the stage severally. Enter French Ambassador.

LAWYER

O my sprightly Frenchman, do you know him? He’s an admirable tilter.

FLAMINEO

I saw him at last tilting; he showed like a pewter candle-stick fashioned like a man in armour, holding a tilting staff in his hand, little bigger than a candle of twelve i’th’ pound.

LAWYER

O but he’s an excellent horseman.

FLAMINEO

A lame one in his lofty tricks; he sleeps o’ horseback like a poulter, —

Enter English and Spanish [Ambassadors]

LAWYER

Lo you my Spaniard.

FLAMINEO

Exeunt He carries his face in’s ruff, as I have seen a serving-man carry glasses in a cypress hat-band, monstrous steady for fear of breaking. He looks like the claw of a blackbird, first salted and then broiled in a candle.

[Act III, Scene ii]

THE ARRAINGMENT OF VITTORIA

Enter FRANCISCO, MONTICELSO, the six lieger Ambassadors, BRACHIANO, VITTORIA, [ZANCHE, FLAMINEO, MARCELLO,] LAWYER, and a guard

MONTICELSO

1Forbear my lord, here is no place assign’d you,
2The business by his holiness is left
3To our examination.

BRACHIANO

To our examination. May it thrive with you!

Lays a rich gown under him

FRANCISCO

4A chair there for his lordship.

BRACHIANO

5Forbear your kindness; and unbidden guest
6Should travel as Dutch women go to church;
7Bear their stools with them.

MONTICELSO

Bear their stools with them. At your pleasure sir.
8Stand to the table gentlewomen. Now signior
9Fall to your plea.

LAWYER

Domine Judex converte oculos in hanc pestem mulierum

11corruptissimam.

VITTORIA

corruptissimam. What's he?

FRANCISCO

12A lowyer, that pleads against you.

VITTORIA

13Pray my lord, let him speak his usual tongue.
14I'll make no answer else.

FRANCISCO

I'll make no answer else. Why you understand Latin.

VITTORIA

15I do sir, but amongst this auditory
16Which come to hear my cause, the half or more
17May be ignorant in't.

MONTICELSO

May be ignorant in't. Go on sir.

VITTORIA

May be ignorant in't. Go on sir. By your favour,
18I will not have my accusation clouded
19In a strange tongue: all this assembly
20Shall hear what you can charge me with.

FRANCISCO

Shall hear what you can charge me with. Signior,
21You need not stand on't much; pray change your language.

MONTICELSO

22Oh for God sake: gentlewoman, your credit
23Shall be more famous by it.

LAWYER

Shall be more famous by it. Well then have at you.

VITTORIA

24I am at the mark sir, I'll gice aim to you,
25And tell you how near you shoot.

LAWYER

26Most literated judges, please your lordships,
27So to connive your judgements to the view
28Of this debauch'd and diversivolent woman
29Who such a black concatenation
30Of mischief hath effected, that to extirp
31The memory of't, must be the consummation
32Of her and her projections –

VITTORIA

Of her and her projections – What's all this? –

LAWYER

33Hold your peace.
34Exorbitant sins must have exulceration.

VITTORIA

35Surely my lords this lawyer here hath swallowed
36Some pothecary's bills, or proclamations.
37And now the hard and undigestible words
38Come up like stones we use give hawks for physic.
39Why this is Welsh to Latin.

LAWYER

Why this is Welsh to Latin. My lords, the woman
40Knows not her tropes nor figures, nor is perfect
41In the academy derivation
42Of grammatical elocution.

FRANCISCO

Of grammatical elocution. Sir your pains
43Shall be well spared, and your deep eloquence
44Be worthily applauded amongst those
45Which understand you.

LAWYER

Which understand you. My good lord.

FRANCISCO

Which understand you. My good lord. speaks this in a scorn Sir,
46Put up your papers in your fustian bag, –
47Cry mercy sir, 'tis buckram, -and acept
48My notion of your learn'd verbosity.

LAWYER

49I most graduatically thank your lordship.
50I shall have use for them elsewhere.

[Exit]

MONTICELSO

51I shall be plainer with you, and paint out
52Your follies in more natural red and white
53Than that upon your cheek.

VITTORIA

Than that upon your cheek. O you mistake.
54You raise a blood as noble in this cheek
55As ever was your mother's

MONTICELSO

56I must spare you till proof cry whore to that;
57Observe this creature here my honoured lords,
58A woman of a most prodigious spirit
59In her effected.

VITTORIA

In her effected. Honourable my lord,
60It doth not suit a reverend cardinal
61To play the lawyer thus –

MONTICELSO

62Oh your trade instructs your language!
63You see my lords what goodly fruit she seems,
64Yet like those apples travellers report
65To grow where Sodom and Gomorrah stood
66I will but touch her and you straight shall see
67She'll fall to soot and ashes.

VITTORIA

She'll fall to soot and ashes. Your envenom'd
68Pothecary should do't –

MONTICELSO

Pothecary should do't – I am resolved
69Were there a second paradise to lose
70This devil would betray it.

VITTORIA

This devil would betray it. O poor charity!
71Thou art seldom found in scarlet.

MONTICELSO

72Who knows not how, when several night by night
73Her gates were chok'd with coaches, and her rooms
74Outbrav'd the stars with several kind of lights
75When she did conterfeit a prince's court?
76In music, banquets and most riotous surfeits
77This whore, forsooth, was holy.

VITTORIA

This whore, forsooth, was holy. Ha? Whore? What's that?

MONTICELSO

78Shall I expound whore to you? Sure I shall:
79I'll give their perfect character. They are first
80Sweetmeats which rot the eater: in man's nostril
81Poison'd perfumes. They are coz'ning alchemy,
82Shipwracks in calmest weather! What are whores?
83Cold Russian winters, that appear so barren,
84They are the true material fire of hell,
85Worse than those tributes i'th' Low Countries paid,
86Exactions upon meat, drink, garments, sleep;
87Ay even on man's perdition, his sin.
88They are those brittle evidences of law
89Which forfeit all a wretched man's estate
90For leaving out one syllable. What are whores?
91They are those flattering bells have all one tune,
92At weddings, and at funerals: your rich whores
93Are only treasuries by extortion fill'd,
94And emptied by curs'd riot. They are worse,
95Worse than dead bodies, which are begg'd at gallows
96And wrought upon by surgeons, to teach man
97Wherein he is imperfect. What's a whore?
98She's like the guilty counterfeited coin
99Which whosoe'er first stamps it brings in trouble
100All that receive it –

VITTORIA

All that receive it – This character scapes me.

MONTICELSO

101You gentlewoman?
102Take from all beasts, and from all minerals
103They deadly poison.

VITTORIA

They deadly poison. Well what then?

MONTICELSO

They deadly poison. Well what then? I'll tell thee.
104I'll find in thee a pothecary's shop
105To sample them all.

FRENCH AMBASSADOR

To sample them all. She hath liv'd ill.

ENGLISH AMBASSADOR

106True, but the cardinal's too bitter.

MONTICELSO

107You know what whore is; next the devil, Adult'ry,
108Enters the devil, Murder.

FRANCISCO

Enters the devil, Murder. Your unhappy
109Husband is dead.

VITTORIA

Husband is dead. O he's a happy husband
110Now he owes nature nothing.

FRANCISCO

111And by a vaulting engine.

MONTICELSO

And by a vaulting engine. An active plot.
112He jump'd into his grave.

FRANCISCO

He jump'd into his grave. What a prodigy was't,
113That form some two yards' height a slender man
114Should break his neck?

MONTICELSO

Should break his neck? I'th'rushes.

FRANCISCO

Should break his neck? I'th'rushes. And what's more,
115Upon the instant lose all use of speech,
116All vital motion, like a man had lain
117Wound up three days. Now mark each circumstance.

MONTICELSO

118And look upon this creature was his wife.
119She comes not like a widow: she comes arm'd
120With scorn and impudence. Is this a mourning habit?

VITTORIA

121Had I foreknown his death as you suggest,
122I would have bespoke my mourning.

MONTICELSO

I would have bespoke my mourning. O you are cunning.

VITTORIA

123You shame your wit and judgement
124To call it so. What, is my just defence
125By him that is my judge call'd impudence?
126Let me appeal then from this Christian court
127To the uncivil Tartar.

MONTICELSO

To the uncivil Tartar. See my lords,
128She scandals our proceedings.

VITTORIA

She scandals our proceedings. Humbly thus,
129Thus low, to the most worthy and respected
130Lieger ambassadors, my modesty
131And womanhood I tender; but withal
132So entangled in a cursed accusation
133That my defence of force like Perseus,
134Must personate masculine virtue to the point.
135Find me but guilty, sever head from body:
136We'll part good friends: I scorn to hold my life
137At yours or any man's entreaty, sir.

ENGLISH AMBASSADOR

138She hath a brave spirit –

MONTICELSO

She hath a brave spirit – Well, well, such counterfeit jewels
139Make true ones oft suspected.

VITTORIA

Make true ones oft suspected. You are deceived.
140For know that all your strict-combined heads,
141Which strike against this mine of diamonds,
142Shall prove but glassen hammers, they shall break;
143These are but feigned shadows of my evils.
144Terryfy babes, my lord, with painted devils,
145I am past such needless plasy; for your names
146Of whore and murd'ress, they proceed from you,
147As if a man should spit against the wind,
148The filth returns in's face.

MONTICELSO

149Pray you mistress satisfy me one question:
150Who lodg'd beneath your roof that fatal night
151Your husband brake his neck?

BRACHIANO

Your husband brake his neck? That question
152Enforcheth me break silence: I was there.

MONTICELSO

153Your business?

BRACHIANO

Your business? Why I came to confort her,
154And take some course for sett'ling her estate,
155Because I heard her husband was in debt
156To you my lord.

MONTICELSO

To you my lord. He was.

BRACHIANO

To you my lord. He was. And 'twas stragely fear'd
157That you would cozen her.

MONTICELSO

That you would cozen her. Who made you overseer?

BRACHIANO

158Why my charity, my charity, which should flow
159From every generous and noble spirit,
160To orphans and to widows.

MONTICELSO

To orphans and to widows. Your lust.

BRACHIANO

161Cowardly dogs bark loudest. Sirrah priest,
162I'll talk with you hereafter, –Do you hear?
163The sword you frame of such an excellent temper,
164I'll sheathe in your own bowels:
165There are a number of thy coat resemble
166Your common post-boys.

MONTICELSO

Your common post-boys. Ha?

BRACHIANO

Your common post-boys. Ha? Your mercenary post-boys;
167Your letters carry truth, but 'tis your guise
168To fill your mouths with gross and impudent lies.

FRANCISCO

177My lord there's great suspicion of the murder,
178But no sound proof who did it: for my part
179I do not think she hath a soul so black
180To act a deed so bloody; if she have,
181As in cold countries husbandmen plant vines,
182And with warm blood manure them, even so
183One summer she will bear unsavoury fruit,
184And ere next spring wither both branch and root.
185The act of blood let pass, only descend
186To matter of incontinence.

VITTORIA

To matter of incontinence. I discern poison
187Under your gilded pills.

MONTICELSO

188Now the Duke's gone, I will produce a letter,
189Wherein 'twas plotted he and you should meet,
190At an apothecary's summer-house,
191Down by the river Tiber: - view't my lords: –
192Where after wanton bathing and the heat
193Of a lascivious banquet. – I pray read it,
194I shame to speak the rest.

VITTORIA

I shame to speak the rest. Grant I was tempted
195Temptation to lust proves not the act,
196Casta est quam nemo rogavit,
197You read his hot love to me, buy you want
198My frosty answer.

MONTICELSO

My frosty answer. Frost i'th' dog-days! strange!

VITTORIA

199Condemn you me for that the Duke did love me?
200So may you blame some fair and crystal river
201For that some melancholic distracted man,
202Hath drown'd himself in't.

MONTICELSO

Hath drown'd himself in't. Truly drown'd indeed.

VITTORIA

203Sum up my faults I pray, and you shall find,
204That beauty and gay clothes, a merry heart,
205And a good stomach to a feast, are all,
206All the poor crimes that you can charge me with:
207In faitg my lord you might go pistol flies,
208The sport would be more noble.

MONTICELSO

The sport would be more noble. Very good.

VITTORIA

209But take you your course, it seems you have beggar'd me first
210And now would fain undo me; I have houses,
211Jewels, and a poor remnant of crusadoes,
212Would those would make you charitable.

MONTICELSO

Would those would make you charitable. If the devil
213Did ever take good shape behold his picture.

VITTORIA

214You have one virtue left,
215You will not flatter me.

FRANCISCO

You will not flatter me. Who brought this letter?

VITTORIA

216I am not compell'd to tell you.

MONTICELSO

217My lord Duke sent to you a thousand ducats,
218The twelfth of August.

VITTORIA

The twelfth of August. 'Twas to keep your cousin
219From prison; I paid use for't.

MONTICELSO

From prison; I paid use for't. I rather think
220'Twas interest for his lust.

VITTORIA

221Who says so but yourself? If you be my accuser
222Pray cease to be my judge, come from the bench,
223Give in your evidence 'gainst me, and let these
224Be moderators. My lord cardinal,
225Were your intelligencing ears as long
226As to my thoughts, had you an honest tongue
227I would not care though you proclaim'd them all.

MONTICELSO

228Go to, go to.
229After your goodly and vain-glorious banquet,
230I'll give you a choke-pear.

VITTORIA

I'll give you a choke-pear. O' your own grafting?

MONTICELSO

231You were born in Venice, honourably descended
232From the Vitelli; 'twas my cousin's fate, –
233Ill may I name the hour - to marry you;
234He bought you of your father.

VITTORIA

He bought you of your father. Ha?

MONTICELSO

He bought you of your father. Ha? He spent there in six months
235Twelve thousand ducats, and to my acquaintance
236Receiv'd in dowry with you not one julio:
237'Twas a hard penny-worth, the ware being so light.
238I yet but draw the curtain, now to your picture:
239You came from thence a most notorious strumpet,
240And so you have continued.

VITTORIA

And so you have continued. My lord.

MONTICELSO

And so you have continued. My lord. Nay hear me,
241You shall have time to prate. My lord Brachiano, –
242Alas I make but repetition,
243Of what is ordinary and Rialto talk,
244And ballated, and would be play'd o'th' stage,
245But that vice many times finds such loud friends
246That preachers are charm'd silent.
247You gentlemen Flamineo and Marcello,
248The court hath nothing now to charge you with,
249Only you must remain upon your sureties
250For your appearance.

FRANCISCO

For your appearance. I stand for Marcello.

FLAMINEO

251And my lord Duke for me.

MONTICELSO

252For you Vittoria, your public fault,
253Join'd to th' condition of the present time,
254Takes from you all the fruits of noble pity.
255Such a corrupted trial have you made
256Both of your life and beauty, and been styl'd
257No less in ominous fate than blazing stars
258To princes; here's your sentence: you are confin'd
259Unto a house of convertites and your bawd –

FLAMINEO

260Who I?aparte

MONTICESO

Who I? The Moor

FLAMINEO

VITTORIA

261A house of convertites, what's that?

MONTICELSO

A house of convertites, what's that? A house
262Of penitent whores.

VITTORIA

Of penitent whores. Do the noblemen in Rome
263Erect ir for their wives, that I am sent
264To lodge there?

FRANCISCO

265You must have patience.

VITTORIA

You must have patience. I must first have vengance.
266I fain would know if you have your salvation
267By patent, that you proceed thus.

MONTICELSO

By patent, that you proceed thus. Away with her.
268Taje her hence.

VITTORIA

269A rape, a rape!

MONTICELSO

A rape, a rape! How?

VITTORIA

A rape, a rape! How? Yes, you have ravish'd justice,
270Forc'd her to do your pleasure.

MONTICELSO

Forc'd her to do your pleasure. Fie, she's mad –

VITTORIA

271Die with those pills in your most cursed maw,
272Should bring you health, or while you sit o'th' bench,
273Let your own spittle choke you.

MONTICELSO

Let your own spittle choke you. She's turn'd fury.

VITTORIA

274That the last day of judgement may so find you,
275And leave you the same devil you were before.
276Instruct me some good horse-leech to speak treason,
277For since you cannot take my life for deeds,
278Take it for words. O woman's poor revenge
279Which dwells but in the tongue! I will not weep,
280No I do scorn to call up one poor tear
281To fawn on your injustice; bear me hence,
282Unto this house of – what's your mitigating title?

MONTICELSO

283Of convertites.

VITTORIA

284It shall not be a house of convertities.
285My mind shall male it honester to me
286Than the Pope's palace, and more peaceable
287That thy soul, thought thou art a cardinal,
288Know this, and let it somewhat raise their richest light.

Exit VITTORIA [with ZANCHE, guarded]

Enter BRACHIANO

BRACHIANO

289Now you and I are friends sir, we'll shake hands,
290In a friend's grave, together: a fir place,
291Being the emblem of soft peace t'atone our hatred.

FRANCISCO

292Sir, what's the matter?

BRACHIANO

293I will not chase more blood from that lov'd cheek,
294You have lost too much already; fare you well.

[Exit]

FRANCISCO

How strange these words sound? What's the interpretation?

FLAMINEO

Good, this is a preface to the discovery of the Duchess' death. He carries ir well. Because now I cannot counterfeit a whining passion for the death of my lady, I will feign a mad humour for the disgrace of my sister, and that will keep off idle questions. Treason's tongue hath a villainous palsy in't; I will talk to any man, hear no man, and for a time appear a politic madam.

[Exit]

FRANCISCO

297How now my noble cousin; what, in black?

GIOVANNI

298Yes uncle, I was taught to imitate you
299In virtue, and you must imitate me
300In colours for your garments; my sweet mother
301Is, –

FRANCISCO

Is, – How? Where?

GIOVANNI

302Is there, no yonder; indeed sir I'll not tell you,
303For I shall make you weep.

FRANCISCO

304Is dead.

GIOVANNI

Is dead. Do not blame me now,
305I did not tell you so.

LODOVICO

I did not tell you so. She's dead my lord.

FRANCISCO

306Dead?

MONTICELSO

Dead? Blessed lady; thou art now above thy woes.
307Wilt please your lordships yo withdraw a little?

[Exeunt AMBASSADORS]

GIOVANNI

308What do the dead do, uncle? Do they eat,
309Hear music, go a-hunting, and be merry,
310As we that live?

FRANCISCO

311No coz; they sleep.

GIOVANNI

No coz; they sleep. Lord, Lord, that I were dead, –
312I have not slept these six nights. When do they wake?

FRANCISCO

313When God shall please.

GIOVANNI

When God shall please. Good God let her sleep ever.
314For I have known her wake an hundred nights,
315Was brine-wet with her tears. I am to complain to you sir.
316I'll tell you how they have used her now she's dead:
317They wrapp'd her in a cruel fold of lead,
318And would not let me kiss her.

FRANCISCO

GIOVANNI

319I have often heard her say she gave me suck,

FRANCISCO

322O, all of my poor sister that remains!
323Take him away for God's sake.

[Exit GIOVANNI, attended]

MONTICELSO

Take him away for God's sake. How now my lord?

FRANCISCO

324Believe me I am nothing but her grave,
325And I shall keep her blessed memory
326Longer than thousand epitaphs.

[Act III, Scene iii]

Enter FLAMINEO as distracted [, MARCELLO and LODOVICO]

FLAMINEO

1We endure the strokes like anvils or hard steel,
2Till pain itself make us no pain to feel.
3Who shall do me right now? Is this the end of servide?
4I'd rather go weed garlic; travel through France, and
5be mine own ostler; wear sheep-skin linings; or shoes that
6stink of blancking; be ent'red into the list of the forty
7thousand pedlars in Poland.

Enter Savoy [Ambassador]

8Would I had rotted in some surgeon's house at Venice,
9built upon the pox as well as on piles, ere I had serv'd
10Brachiano

SAVOY AMBASSADOR

Brachiano You must have confort.

FLAMINEO

Your comfortable words are like honey. They relish well in your mouth that's whole; but in mine that's wounded they go down as if the sting of the bee were in them. Oh they have wrought their purpose cunningly, as if they would not seem to do it of malice. In this a politician imitates the devil, as the devil imitates a cannon. Wheresoever he comes to do mischief, he comes with his backside towards you.

Enter the French [Ambassador]

FRENCH AMBASSADOR

The proofs are evident.

FLAMINEO

Enter English Ambassador You are happy in England, my lord; here they sell justice with those weights they press men to death with. O horrible salary! Proof! 'twas corruption. O gold, what a god art thou! And O man, what a devil art thou to be tempted by that cursed mineral! Yon diversivolent lawyer; mark him; knaves turn informers, as maggots turn to flies; you may catch gudgeons with either. A cardinal; - I would he would hear me, - there's nothing so holy but money will corrupt and putrify it, like victual under the line.

ENGLISH AMBASSADOR

Fie, fie, Flamineo.

FLAMINEO

[Exeunt Ambassadors] Bells ne'er ring well, till they are at their full pitch, and I hope yon cardinal shall never have the grace to pray well, till he come to the scaffold. If they were rack'd now to know the conferacy! But your noblemen are privileged from the rack; and well may. For a little thing would pull some of them a' pieces afore they came yo their arraingment. Religion; o how it is commeddled with policy. The first bloodshed in the world happened about religion. Would I were a Jew.

MARCELLO

O, there are too many.

FLAMINEO

You are deceiv'd. There are not Jews enough; priests enough, nor gentlemen enough.

MARCELLO

How?

FLAMINEO

I'll prove it. For if there were Jews enough, so many Christians would not turn usurers; if priests enough, one many early mushrooms, whose best growth sprang from a dunghill, should not aspire to gentility. Farewell. Let others live by begging. Be thou one of them; practise the art of Wolner in England to swallow all's given thee; and let one purgation make thee as hungry again as fellows that work in a sawpit. I'll go hear the streech-owl.

Exit

With counterpoisons. Mark this strange encounter.

FLAMINEO

28The god of melancholy turn thy gall to poison,
29And let the stigmatic wrinkles in thy face,
30Like to the boisterous waves in a rough tide
31One still overtake another.

LODOVICO

One still overtake another. I do thank thee
32And I do wish ingeniously for thy sake
33The dog-days all year long.

FLAMINEO

The dog-days all year long. How croaks the raven?
34Is our good Duchess dead?

LODOVICO

Is our good Duchess dead? Dead.

FLAMINEO

Is our good Duchess dead? Dead. O fate!
35Misfortune comes like the crowner's bussines,
36Huddle upon huddle.

LODOVICO

37Shalt thou and I join housekeeping?

FLAMINEO

Shalt thou and I join housekeeping? Yes, content.
38Let's be unsociably sociable.

LODOVICO

39Sit some three days together, and discourse.

FLAMINEO

40Only with making faces;
41Lie in our clothes.

LODOVICO

42With faggots for our pillows.

FLAMINEO

With faggots for our pillows. And be lousy.

LODOVICO

43In taffeta linings; that's gentle melancholy;
44Sleep all day.

FLAMINEO

Sleep all day. Yes: and like your melancholic hare
45Feed after midnight.

Enter ANTONELLI [and GASPARO, laughing]

46We are observed: see how yon couple grieve.

LODOVICO

47What a strange creature is a laughing fool,
48As if a man were created to no use
49But only to show his teeth.

FLAMINEO

But only to show his teeth. I'll tell the what,
50It would do well instead of looking-glasses
51To set one's dace each morning by a saucer
52Of a witch's congealed bood.

LODOVICO

Of a witch's congealed bood. Precious girn, rogue.
53We'll never part.

FLAMINEO

We'll never part. Never: till the beggary of courtiers,
54The discontent of churchmen, want of soldiers,
55And all the creatures that hang manacled,
56Worse than strappado'd, on the lowest felly
57Of Fortune’s wheel be taught in our two lives
58To scorn that world wich life of means deprives.

ANTONELLI

59My lord I bring good news. The Pope on's death-bed,
60At th'earnest suit of the great Duke of Florence,
61Hath sign'd your pardon, and restor'd unto you –

LODOVICO

62I thank you for your news. Look up again
63Flamineo, see my pardon.

FLAMINEO

Flamineo, see my pardon. Why do you laugh?
64There was no such condition in our covenant.

LODOVICO

There was no such condition in our covenant. Why?

FLAMINEO

65You shall not seem a happier man that I;
66You know our vow sir, if you will be merry,
67Do it i'th' like posture, as if some great man
68Sat while his enemy were executed:
69Though it be very lechery unto thee,
70Do't with a crabbed politician's face.

LODOVICO

71Your sister is a damnable whore.

FLAMINEO

Your sister is a damnable whore. Ha?

LODOVICO

72Look you; I spake that laughing.

FLAMINEO

73Dost ever think to speak again?

LODOVICO

Dost ever think to speak again? Do you hear?
74Wilt sell me forty ounces of her blood,
75To water a mandrake?

FLAMINEO

To water a mandrake? Poor lord, you did vow
76To live a lousy creature.

LODOVICO

To live a lousy creature. Yes; –

FLAMINEO

To live a lousy creature. Yes; – Like one
77That had for ever forfeited the daylight,
78By being in debt, –

LODOVICO

By being in debt, – Ha, ha!

FLAMINEO

79I do not greatly wonder you do break:
80Your lordship learn't long since. But I'll tell you, –

LODOVICO

81What?

FLAMINEO

What? And't shall stick by you.

LODOVICO

What? And't shall stick by you. I long for it.

FLAMINEO

82This laughter scurvily becomes your face;
83Strikes him If you will not be melancholy, be angry.
84See, now I laugh too.

MARCELLO

85You are to blame, I'll force you hence.

LODOVICO

You are to blame, I'll force you hence. Unhand me.

Exit MAR[CELLO] & FLAM[INEO]

86That e'er I should be forc'd to right myself,
87Upon a pander!

ANTONELLI

Upon a pander! My lord.

LODOVICO

88H'had been as good meet with his fist a thunderbolt.

GASPARO

89How this shows!

LODOVICO

How this shows! Ud's death, how did my sword miss him?
90These rogues that are most weary of their lives,
91Sill scape the greatest dangers.
92A pox upon him: all his reputation; –
93Nay all the goodness of his family; –
94Is not worth half this earthquake.
95I learnt it of no fencer to shake thus;
96Come, I'll forget him, and go drink some wine.

Exeunt
