[Act II, Scene i]

Enter FRANCISCO DE MEDICI, Cardinal MONTICELSO, MARCELLO, ISABELLA, young GIOVANNI, with little JAQUES the Moor

FRANCISCO

1Have you not seen your husband since you arrived?

ISABELLA

2Not yet sir.

FRANCISCO

Not yet sir. Surely he is wondrous kind.
3If I had such a dove house as Camillo's
4I would set fire on't, were't but to destroy
5The pole-cats that haunt to't, - my sweet cousin.

GIOVANNI

6Lord uncle you did promise me a horse
7And armour.

FRANCISCO

And armour. That I did my pretty cousin.
8Marcello see it fitted.

MARCELLO

Marcello see it fitted. My lord, the Duke is here.

FRANCISCO

9Sister away, you must not yet be seen.

ISABELLA

10I do beseech you
11Entreat him mildly, let not your rough tongue
12Set us at louder variance; all my wrongs
13Are freely pardoned, and I do not doubt
14As men to try the precious unicorn's horn
15Make of the powder a preservative circle
16And in it put a spider, so these arms
17Shall charm his poison, force it to obeying
18And keep him chaste from an infected straying.

FRANCISCO

19I wish I may. Be gone.

Exit [ISABELLA]

Enter BRACHIANO and FLAMINEO

I wish I may. Be gone. Void the chamber.

[Exeunt FLAMINEO, MARCELLO, GIOVANNI and JAQUES]

20You are welcome, will you sit? I pray my lord
21Be you my orator, my heart's too full;
22I'll second you anon.

MONTICELSO

I'll second you anon. Ere I begin
23Let me entreat your Grace forgo all passion
24Which may be raised by my free discourse.

BRACHIANO

25As silent as i'th' church – you may proceed

MONTICELSO

26It is a wonder to your noble friends,
27That you that have as 'twere ent'red the world,
28With a free sceptre in your able hand,
29And have to th'use of nature well applied
30High gifts of learning, should in your prime age
31Neglect your awful throne, for the soft down
32Of an insatiate bed. Oh my lord,
33The drunkard after all his lavish cups,
34Is dry, and then is sober; so at length,
35When you awake from this lascivious dream,
36Repentance then will follow; like the sting
37Plac'd in the adder's tail: wretched are princes
38When fortune blasteth but a petty flower
39Of their unwiedly crowns; or ravisheth
40But one pearl from their sceptre: but alas!
41When they to wilful shipreck loose good frame
42All princely titles perish with their name.

BRACHIANO

43You have said my lord, –

MONTICELSO

You have said my lord, – Enough to give you taste
44How far I am from flattering your greatness?

BRACHIANO

45Now you that are his second, what say you?
46Do not like young hawks fetch a course about;
47Your game flies fair and for you, –

FRANCISCO

Your game flies fair and for you, – Do not fear it:
48I'll answer you in your own hawking phrase.
49Some eagles that should gaze upon the sun
50Seldom soar high, but take their lustful ease
51Since they from dunghill birds their prey can seize.
52You know Vittoria?

BRACHIANO

You know Vittoria? Yes.

FRANCISCO

You know Vittoria? Yes. You shift your shirt there
53When you retire from tennis.

BRACHIANO

When you retire from tennis. Happily.

FRANCISCO

54Her husband is lord of a poor fortune
55Yet she wears cloth of tissue, –

BRACHIANO

Yet she wears cloth of tissue, – What of this?
56Will you urge that my good lord cardinal
57As part of her confession at next shrift,
58And know from whence it sails?

FRANCISCO

And know from whence it sails? She is your strumpet, –

BRACHIANO

59Uncivil sir there's hemlock in thy breath
60And that black slander; were she a whore of mine
61All thy loud cannons, and thy borrowed Switzers
62Thy galleys, nor thy sworn confederates
63Durst not supplant her.

FRANCISCO

Durst not supplant her. Let's not talk on thunder.
64Thou hast a wife, our sister; would I had given
65Both her white hands to death, bound and lock'd fast
66In her last winding-sheet, when I gave thee
67But one.

BRACHIANO

But one. Thou hadst given a soul to God then.

FRANCISCO

But one. Thou hadst given a soul to God then. True:
68Thy ghostly father with all's absolution,
69Shall ne'er do so by thee.

BRACHIANO

Shall ne'er do so by thee. Spit thy poison, –

FRANCISCO

70I shall not need, lust carries her sharp whip
71At her own girdle; look to't for our anger
72Is making thunder-bolts.

BRACHIANO

Is making thunder-bolts. Thunder? in faith,
73They are but crackers.

FRANCISCO

They are but crackers. We'll end this with the cannon.

BRACHIANO

74Thou'lt get nought by it but iron in thy wounds,
75And gunpowder in thy nostrils.

FRANCISCO

And gunpowder in thy nostrils. Better that
76Than change perfumes for plasters, –

BRACHIANO

Than change perfumes for plasters, – Pity on thee,
77'Twere good you'ld show your slaves or men condemn'd
78Your new-plough'd forehead. Defiance!-and I'll meet thee,
79Even in a thicket of thy ablest men.

MONTICELSO

80My lords, you shall not word it any further
81Without a milder limit.

FRANCISCO

Without a milder limit. Willingly.

BRACHIANO

82Have you proclaimed a triumph that you bait
83A lion thus?

MONTICELSO

A lion thus? My lord.

BRACHIANO

A lion thus? My lord. I am tame sir.

FRANCISCO

84We send unto the Duke for conference
85'Bout levies 'gainst the pirates; my lord Duke
86Is not at home; we come ourself in person,
87Still my lord Duke is busied; but we fear
88Discovers flocks of wild ducks, then my lord
89('Bout moulting time I mean,) we shall be certain
90To find you sure enough and speak with you.

BRACHIANO

To find you sure enough and speak with you. Ha?

FRANCISCO

91A mere tale of a tub, my words are idle,
92But to express the sonnet by natural reason,
93When stags grow melancholic you'll find the season –

Enter GIOVANNI

MONTICELSO

94No more my lord; here comes a champion,
95Shall end the difference between you both,
96Your son the prince Giovanni. See my lords
97What hopes you store in him; this is a casket
98For both your crowns, and should be held like clear:
99Now is he apt for knowledge; therefore know
100It is a more direct and even way
101To train to virtue those of princely blood
102By examples than by precepts: if by examples
103Whom should he rather strive to imitate
104Than his own father? Be his pattern then,
105Leave him a stock of virtue that may last,
106Should fortune rend his sails, and split his mast.

BRACHIANO

107Your hand boy-growing to a soldier?

GIOVANI

Your hand boy-growing to a soldier? Give me a pike.

FRANCISCO

108What, practising your pike so young, fair coz?

GIOVANNI

109Suppose me one of Homer's frogs, my lord,
110Tossing my bullrush thus. Pray sir tell me
111Might not a child of good discretion
112Be leader to an army?

FRANCISCO

Be leader to an army? Yes cousin, a young prince
113Of good discretion might.

GIOVANNI

Of good discretion might. Say you so?
114Indeed I have head 'tis fit a general
115Should not endanger his own person oft,
116So that he make a noise, when he's horseback
117Like a Dansk drummer. O 'tis excellent!
118He need not fight; methinks his horse as well
119Might lead an army for him; if I live
120I'll challenge the French foe, in the very front
121Of all my troops, the foremost man.

FRANCISCO

Of all my troops, the foremost man. What, what, –

GIOVANNI

122And will not bid my soldiers up and follow
123But bid them follow me.

BRACHIANO

But bid them follow me. Forward lapwing.
124He files with the shell on's head.

FRANCISCO

He files with the shell on's head. Pretty cousin.

GIOVANNI

125The first year uncle that I go to war
126All prisoners that I take I will set free
127Without their ransome.

FRANCISCO

Without their ransome. Ha, without their ransome?
128That took those prisoners for you?

GIOVANNI

That took those prisoners for you? Thus my lord:
129I'll marry them to all the wealthy windows
130That falls that year.

FRANCISCO

That falls that year. Why then the next year following
131You'll have no men to go with you to war.

GIOVANNI

132Why then I'll press the women to the war,
133And then the men will follow.

MONTICELSO

And then the men will follow. Witty prince.

FRANCISCO

134See a good habit makes a child a man,
135Whereas a bad one makes a man a beast:
136Come, you and I are friends.

BRACHIANO

Come, you and I are friends. Most wishedly;
137Like bones which broke in sunder and well set
138Knit the more strongly.

FRANCISCO

Knit the more strongly. [calls offstage] Call Camillo hither.
139You have received the rumour, how Count Lodowick
140Is turn'd a pirate?

BRACHIANO

Is turn'd a pirate? Yes.

FRANCISCO

Is turn'd a pirate? Yes. We are now preparing
141Some ships to fetch him in.

[Enter ISABELLA]

142Behold your Duchess;
143We now will leave you and expect from you
144Nothing but kind entreaty.

BRACHIANO

Nothing but kind entreaty. You have charm'd me.

Exeunt FR[ANCISCO], MON[TICELSO], GIOV[ANNI]

145You are in health we see.

ISABELLA

You are in health we see. And above health
146To see my lord well.

BRACHIANO

To see my lord well. So I wonder much,
147What amorous whirlwind hurried you to Rome –

ISABELLA

148Devotion my lord.

BRACHIANO

Devotion my lord. Devotion?
149Is your soul charg'd with a grievous sin?

ISABELLA

150'Tis burdened with too many, and I think
151The oft'ner that we cast our reck'nings up,
152Our sleeps will be the sounder.

BRACHIANO

Our sleeps will be the sounder. Take your chamber.

ISABELLA

153Nay my dear lord, I will not have you angry;
154Doth not my absence from you two months
155Merit one kiss?

BRACHIANO

Merit one kiss? I do not use to kiss.
156If that will dispossess your jealousy,
157I'll swear it to you.

ISABELLA

I'll swear it to you. O my loved lord,
158I do not come to chide; my jealousy?
159I am to learn what that Italian means;
160You are as welcome to these longing arms,
161As I to you a virgin.

[She tries to embrace him; he turns away]

BRACHIANO

As I to you a virgin. O your breath!
162Out upon sweetmeats, and continued physic!
163The plague is in them.

ISABELLA

The plague is in them. You have oft for these two lips
164Neglected cassia or the natural sweets
165Of the spring violet; they are not yet much withered.
166My lord I should be merry; these your frowns
167Show in a helmet lovely, but on me,
168In such a peaceful interview methinks
169They are too roughly knit.

BRACHIANO

They are too roughly knit. O dissemblance!
170Do you bandy factions 'ganst me? Have you learnt
171The trick of imprudent baseness to complain
172Unto your kindred?

ISABELLA

Unto your kindred? Never my dear lord.

BRACHIANO

173Must I be haunted out, or was't your trick
174To meet some amorous gallant here ir Rome
175That must supply our discontinuance?

ISABELLA

176I pray sir burst my heart, and in my death
177Turn to your ancient pity, though not love.

BRACHIANO

178Beacuse your brother is the corpulent Duke,
179That is the great Duke, -'Sdeath I shall not shortly
180Racket away five hundred crowns at tennis,
181But it shall rest upon record. I scorn him
182Like a shav'd Polack; all his reverent wit
183Lies in his wardrobe; he's a discreet fellow
184When he's made up in his robes of state, –
185Your brother the great Duke, because h'as galleys,
186And now and then ransacks a Turkish fly-boat,
187(Now all the hellish Furies take his soul,)
188First made this match, -accursed be the priest
189That sang the wedding mass, and even my issue.

ISABELLA

190O too, too far you have curs'd.

BRACHIANO

O too, too far you have curs'd. Your hand I'll kiss:
191This is the latest ceremony of my love,
192Henceforth I'll never lie with thee, by this,
193This wedding ring: I'll ne'er more lie with thee.
194And this divorce shall be as truly kept,
195A if the judge had doom'd it: fare you well,
196Our sleeps are sever'd.

ISABELLA

Our sleeps are sever'd. Forbid it the sweet union
197Of all things blessed; why the saints in heaven
198Will knit their brows at that.

BRACHIANO

Will knit their brows at that. Let not thy love
199Shall never on my soul be satisfied
200With my repentance: let thy brother rage
201Beyond a horrid tempest or sea-fight,
202My vow is fixed.

ISABELLA

My vow is fixed. O my winding sheet,
203Now shall I need thee shortly. Dear my lord,
204Let me hear once more what I would not hear:
Let me hear once more what I would not hear: Never?

BRACHIANO

Let me hear once more what I would not hear: Never? Never!

ISABELLA

205O my unkind lord may your sins find mercy,
206As I upon a woeful widowed bed
207Shall pray for you, if not to turn your eyes
208Upon your wretched wife, and hope son,
209Yet that in time you'll fix them upon heaven.

BRACHIANO

210No more; go, go complain to the great Duke.

ISABELLA

211No my dear lord, you shall have present witness
212How I'll work peace between you; I will make
213Myself the author of your cursed vow.
214I have some cause to do it, you have none;
215Conceal it I beseech you, for the weal
216Of both your dukedoms, that you wrought the means
217Of such a separation; let the fault
218Remain with my supposed jealousy,
219And think with what a piteous and rent heart
220I shall perform this sad ensuing part.

Enter FRANCISCO, FLAMINEO, MONTICELSO; MARCELLO

BRACHIANO

221Well, take your course; my honourable brother!

FRANCISCO

222Sister! -this is not well my lord,- why sister!
223She merits not this welcome.

BRACHIANO

She merits not this welcome. Welcome, say?
224She hath given a sharp welcome.

FRANCISCO

She hath given a sharp welcome. Are you foolish?
225Come dry your tears; is this a modest course?
226To better what is nought, to rail, and weep?
227Grow to a reconcilement, or by heaven,
228I'll ne'er more deal between you.

ISABELLA

I'll ne'er more deal between you. Sir you shall not,
229No though Vittoria upon that condition
230Would become honest.

FRANCISCO

Would become honest. Was your husband loud,
231Since we departed?

ISABELLA

Since we departed? By my life sir no.
232I swear by that I do not care to lose.
233Are all these ruins of my former beauty
234Laid out for a whore's triumph?

FRANCISCO

Laid out for a whore's triumph? Do you hear?
235Look upon other women, with what patience
236They suffer these slight wrongs, with what justice
237They study to requite them; take that course.

ISABELLA

238O that I were a man, or that I had power
239To execute my apprehended wishes,
240I would whip some with scorpions.

FRANCISCO

I would whip some with scorpions. What? turn'd fury?

ISABELLA

241To dig the strumpet's eyes out, let her lie
242Some twenty months a-dying, to cut off
243Her nose and lips, pull out her rotten teeth,
244Preserve her flesh like mummia, for trophies
245Of my just anger. Hell to my affliction
246Is mere snow-water: by your favour sir, –
247Brother draw near, and my lord cardinal, –
248Sir let me borrow of you but one kiss,
249Henceforth I'll never lie with you, by this,
250This wedding-ring.

FRANCISCO

This wedding-ring. How? Ne'er more lie with him?
251And this divorce shall be as truly kept,
252As if in thronged court, a thousand ears
253Had heard it, and a thousand lawyers' hands
254Seal'd to the separation.

BRACHIANO

Seal'd to the separation. Ne'er lie with me?

ISABELLA

Seal'd to the separation. Ne'er lie with me? Let not my former dotage
255Make thee an unbeliever; this my vow
256Shall never on my soul be satisfied
257With my repentance: manet alta mente repostum.

FRANCISCO

258Was this your circle of pure unicorn's horn,
259You said should charm your lord? Now horn upon thee,
260For jealousy deserves them; keep your vow,
261And take your chamber.

ISABELLA

And take your chamber. No sir I'll presently to Padua,
262I will not stay a minute.

MONTICELSO

I will not stay a minute. O good madam.

BRACHIANO

263'Twere best to let her have her humour,
264Some half-day's journey will bring down her stomach,
265And then she'll turn in post.

FRANCISCO

And then she'll turn in post. To see her come
266To my lord cardinal for a dispensation
267Of her rash vow will beget excellent laughter.

ISABELLA

268Unkindness do thy office, poor hear break,
269Those are the killing griefs which dare not speak.

Exit

Enter CAMILLO

MARCELLO

270Camillo's come my lord.

FRANCISCO

Camillo's come my lord. Where's the commission?

MARCELLO

271'Tis here.

FRANCISCO

'Tis here. Give me the signet.

FLAMINEO

[to BRACHIANO] My lord do you mark their whispering; I will compound a medicine out of their two heads, stronger than garlic, deadlier than stibium; the cantharides which are scarce seen to stick upon the flesh when they work to the heart, shall not do it with more silence or invisible cunning.

Enter Doctor [JULIO]

BRACHIANO

About the murder.

FLAMINEO

They are sending him to Naples, but I'll send him to Candy; here's another property too.

BRACHIANO

O the doctor, –

FLAMINEO

A poor quack-salving knave, my lord, one that should have been lash'd for's lechery, but that he confess'd a judgement, had an execution laid upon him, and so put the whip to a non plus.

DOCTOR

And was cozen'd, my lord, by an arranter knave than myself, and made pay all the colourable execution.

FLAMINEO

He will shoot pills into a man's guts, shall make them have more ventages than a cornet or a lamprey; he will poison a kiss, and was once minded, for his masterpiece, because Ireland breeds no poison, to have prepared a deadly vapour in a Spaniard's fart that should have posion'd all Dublin.

BRACHIANO

O Saint Anthony's fire!

DOCTOR

Your secretary in merry my lord.

FLAMINEO

O thou cursed antipathy to nature! Look his eye's bloodshed like a needle a chirurgeon stitcheth a wound with. Let me embrace thee toad, and love thee, O thou abhominable loathsome gargarism, that will fetch up lungs, lights, heart, and liver by scrupules.

BRACHIANO

282No more; I must employ thee honest doctor,
283You must to Padua and by the way,
284Use some of your skill for us.

DOCTOR

Use some of your skill for us. Sir I shall.

BRACHIANO

285But for Camillo?

FLAMINEO

286He dies this night by such a politic strain,
287Men shall suppose him by's own engine slain.
288But for your Duchess' death?

DOCTOR

But for your Duchess' death? I'll make her sure.

BRACHIANO

289Small mischiefs are by greater made secure.

FLAMINEO

290Remember this you slave; when knaves come to preferment
291they rise as gallowses are raised i'th' Low Countries: one
292upon another's shoulders .

Exeunt [BRACHIANO, FLAMINEO and Doctor JULIO]

MONTICELSO

293Here is an emblem nephew, pray persuse it.
294'Twas thrown in at your window, –

CAMILLO

'Twas thrown in at your window, – At my window?
295Here ir a stag my lord hath shed his horns,
296And fot the loss of them the poor beast weeps.
297The word Inopem me copia fecit.

MONTICELSO

The word Inopem me copia fecit. That is:
298Plenty of horns hath made him poor of horns.

CAMILLO

299What should this mean?

MONTICELSO

What should this mean? I'll tell you: 'tis given out
300You are a cuckold.

CAMILLO

You are a cuckold. Is it given out so?
301I had rather such report as that my lord,
302Should keep within doors.

FRANCISCO

Should keep within doors. You are the happier:
303I'll tell you a tale.

CAMILLO

I'll tell you a tale. Pray my lord.

FRANCISCO

I'll tell you a tale. Pray my lord. An old tale.
304Upon a time Phoebus the god of light,
305Or him we call the sun, would need be married.
306The goods gave their consent, and Mercury
307Was sent to voice it to the general world.
308But what a piteous cry there straight arose
309Amongst smiths, and feltmakers, brewers and cooks,
310Reapers and butter-women, amongst fishmongers
311And thousand other trades, which are annoyed
312By his excessive heat; 'twas lamentable.
313They came to Jupiter all in a sweat
314And do forbid the bans; a great fat cook
315Was made their speaker, who entreats of Jove
316That Phoebus might be gelded, for if now
317When there was but one sun, so many men
318Were like to perish by his violent heat,
319What should they do if he were married
320And should beget more, and those children
321Make fireworks like their father? So say I,
322Only I will apply it to your wife:
323Her issue, should not providence prevent it,
324Would make both nature, time, and man repent it.

MONTICELSO

325Look you cousin,
326Go change the air for shame; see if your absence
327Will blast your cornucopia; Marcello
328Is chosen with you joint commissioner
329For the relieving our Italian coast
330From pirates.

MARCELLO

From pirates. I am much honour'd in't.

CAMILLO

From pirates. I am much honour'd in't. But sir
331Ere I return the stag's horns may be sprouted,
332Greater than these are shed.

MONTICELSO

Greater than these are shed. Do not fear it,
333I'll be your ranger.

CAMILLO

I'll be your ranger. You must watch i'th' nights,
334Then's the most danger.

FRANCISCO

Then's the most danger. Farewell good Marcello.
335All the best fortunes of a soldier's wish
336Bring you o' ship-board.

CAMILLO

337Were I not best now I am turn'd soldier,
338Ere than I leave my wife, sell all she hath
339And then take leave of her.

MONTICELSO

And then take leave of her. I expect good from you,
340Your parting is so merry.

CAMILLO

341Merry my lord, o'th' capitain's humour right;
342I am resolved to be drunk this night.

Exit [CAMILLO; with MARCELLO]

FRANCISCO

343So, 'twas well fitted: now shall we discern
344How his wish'd absence will give violent way
345To Duke Brachiano's lust, –

MONTICELSO

To Duke Brachiano's lust, – Why that was it;
346To what scorn'd purpose else should we make choice
347Of him for a sea-capiain, and besides,
348Count Lodowick which was rumour'd for a pirate,
349Is now in Padua.

FRANCISCO

Is now in Padua. It's true?

MONTICELSO

Is now in Padua. It's true? Most certain.
350I have letters from him, which are suppliant
351To work his quick repeal from banishment;
352He means to address himself for pension
353Unto our sister Duchess.

FRANCISCO

Unto our sister Duchess. O'twas well.
354We shall not want his absence past six days;
355I fain would have the Duke Brachiano run
356Into notorious scandal, for there's nought
357In such curs'd dotage, to repair his name,
358Only the deep sense of some deathless shame.

MONTICELSO

359It may be objected I am dishonourable,
360To play thus with my kinsman, but I answer,
361For my revenge I'd stake a brother's life,
362That being wrong'd durst not avenge himself.

FRANCISCO

363Come to observe this stumpet.

MONTICELSO

Come to observe this stumpet. Curse of greatness,
364Sure he'll not leave her.

FRANCISCO

Sure he'll not leave her. There's small pity in't.
365Like mistletoe on sere elms spent by weather,
366Let him cleave to her and both rot together.

Exeunt

[Act II, Scene ii]

Enter BRACHIANO with one in the habit of a Conjuror

BRACHIANO

1Now sir I claim your promise; 'tis dead midnight,
2The time prefix'd to show me by your art
3How the intended murder of Camillo,
4And our loathed Duchess grow to action.

CONJUROR

5You have won me by your bounty to a deed
6I do not often practise; some there are,
7Which by sophistic tricks, aspire that name
8Which I would glady lose, of nigromancer;
9As some that use to juggle upon cards,
10Seeming to conjure, when indeed they cheat;
11Others that raise up their confederate spirits,
12'Bout windmills, and endanger their own necks,
13For making of a squib; and some there are
14Will keep a curtal to show juggling tricks
15And give out 'tis a spirit: and some there are
16Will keep a curtal to show juggling tricks
17And give out 'tis a spirit: besides these
18Such a whole ream of almanac-makers, figure-flingers,
19Fellows indeed that only live by stealth,
20Since they do merely lie about stol'n goods,
21They'd make men think the devil were fast an loose,
22With speaking fustian Latin. Pray sit down,
23Put on this night-cap sir, 'tis charm'd, and now
24I'll show you by my strong-commanding art
25The circumstance that breaks your Duchess' heart.

A DUMB SHOW

Enter suspiciously, JULIO and CHRISTOPHERO; they draw a curtain where BRACHIANO's picture is, they put in spectacles of glass which cover their eyes and noses, and then burn perfumes afore the picture, and wash the lips of the picture; that done, quenching the fire, and putting off their spectacles off their depart laughing.

Enter ISABELLA in her nightgown as to bedward, with lights after her, Count LODOVICO, GIOVANNI, GUID-ANTONIO and others waiting on her; she kneels down as to prayers, then draws the curtain of the picture, does three reverences to it, and kisses thrice, she faints and will not suffer them to come near it, dies; sorrow express'd in GIOVANNI and in Count LODOVICO; she's convey'd out solemny.

BRACHIANO

26Excellent, then she's dead, –

CONJUROR

Excellent, then she's dead, – She's poisoned,
27By the fum'd picture: 'twas her custom nightly,
28Before she went to bed, to go and visit
29Your picture, and to fed her eyes and lips
30On the dead shadow; Doctor Julio
31Observing this, infects it with an oil
32And other poison’d stuff, which presently
33Did suffocate her spirits.

BRACHIANO

Did suffocate her spirits. Methought I saw
34Count Lodowick there.

CONJUROR

Count Lodowick there. He was, and by my art
35I find he did most passionately dote
36Upon your Duchess. Now turn another way,
37And view Camillo’s far more politic fate:
38Strike louder music from this charmed ground,
39To yield, as fits the act, a tragic sound.

THE SECOND DUMB SHOW

Enter FLAMINEO, MARCELLO, CAMILLO with four more as Capitains, they drink healths and dance; a vaulting-horse is brought into the room, MARCELLO and two more whisper’d out of the room, while FLAMINEO and CAMILLO strip themselves into their shirts, as to vault; compliment who shall begin, as CAMILLO is about to vault, FLAMINEO pitcheth him upon his neck, and with the help of the rest, writhes his neck about, seems to see if it be broke, and lays him folded double as ‘twere under the horse, makes shows to call for help, MARCELLO comes in, laments, sends for the Cardinal and Duke, who comes forth with armed men, wonder at the act; commands the body to be carried home, apprehends FLAMINEO, MARCELLO, and the rest, and go as ‘twere to apprehend VITTORIA.

BRACHIANO

40‘Twas quaintly done, but yet each circumstance
41I taste not fully.

CONJUROR

I taste not fully. O ‘twas most apparent,
42You saw them enter charged with their deep healths
43To their boon voyage, and to second that,
44Flamineo calls to have a valuating-horse
45Maintain their sport. The virtuous Marcello
46Is innocently plotted forth the room,
47Whilst your eye saw the rest, and can inform you
48The engine of all.

BRACHIANO

49It seems Marcello, and Flamineo
50Are both committed.

CONJUROR

Are both committed. Yes, you saw them guarded,
51And now they are come with purpose to apprehend
52Your mistress, fair Vittoria; we are now
53Beneath her roof: ‘twere fit we instantly
54Make your eye saw the rest, and can inform you
55The engine of all.
56Make out by some back postern.

BRACHIANO

Make out by some back postern. Noble friend,
57You bind me ever to you; this shall stand
58As the firm seal annexed to my hand
59It shall enforce a payment.

Exit BRACHIANO

CONJUROR

It shall enforce a payment. Sir I thank you.
60Both flowers and weeds spring when the sun is warm,
61As great men do great good, or else great harm.

Exit CONJUROR
