Act II

Scen. 1.

Satyre alone

SATYRE

648Small is the Bee, much smaller is her sting,
649Yet doth its wound both pain and sorrow bring,
650But what’s more smal then love? Since it conceals
651It self within such minute parts, and steals
652Into the smallest spaces, now it rests
653Within the valley of two alp-like breasts:
654Now creeps and hides it self within the fair
655And curled tresses of a golden hair:
656Now under twinkling eye-brows, now i’th’ sleek
657And rosie dimples of a laughing cheek;
658Yet are it’s wounds, it’s plagues, so sad, so sore,
659That nought can be imagin’d torment more.
660Ah me! my very bowels and my heart
661Boil o’re with blood, and like a cruel dart,
662So Silvia’s fair ey’s pierce me, I may say
663Cruel Love; but far more cruel Silvia,
664That it impos’d thee, woods in their greenbrakes
665Cru’ler then woods, Oh! how wel doth agree
666Thy nature with thy name, wel did he see
667Do hide fierce lions, tigers, wolves and snakes,
668Thou under covert of thy fairest breast,
669Hidʼst anger, hatred and disdain, which beasts
670And worse y much (alas) than those of prey,
671As lions, tygers, wolves or snakes; for they
672May be appeased, but ay me! these are
673Such, as will not be charm’d by gift or pray’r;
674Thou slights are flowr wch from the fields I chose
675Because thy cheek flowers much fairer showes.
676I from the orchards bring thee apples fair,
677Which thou rejectst disdainful, cause there are
678In thy fair bosome apples far more rare.
679I bring thee sweetest honey, even such
680From hives I stole thou, deignst it not to touch
681Cause on thy sweet lips sweeter ther’s by much;
682But if my poverty permits me not
683To give unto thee any thing but what
684In thee’s more sweet and fair, my self then take;
685Unjust, why shouldst thou such a gift forsake?
686I’m not to be despised, no, for I
687Saw my self in the sea when it did lie
688Becalm’d & free from waves, this my fierce, stern
689And sanguine look, these shoulders large, this arm
690So strong and nervous, this rough shaggy breast,
691These big-bon’d thighs of mine, and all my rest
692So well knit members, are a sign that I
693Am strong and lusty, it believ’st not, try;
694What wil’t do with these tender fooles, whose sleek
695Face, scarce allowes them down upon their cheek?
696Women, in show, and Workes they be, nor are
697Skill’d in ought else, but to dispose each haire,
698In it’s due order; ther’s not one that dare
699Follow thee through the woods, to hunt the Bear,
700Or, durst encounter wilde Boares for thy sake.
701I’me not so ugly, no: nor dost forsake
702Me, ʼcause I am thus form’d, alas, buy why?
703Because I’m poor, thou dost thy love deny.
704Alas, the Villages do follow now
705The custom of the Mighty Cities: how
706Well may the golden age this called be,
707Since gold alone Commands imperiously?
708O! thou, who first didst teach the way, to sell
709Divinest love, may torments, worse then hell,
710Still waite and then upon thee; maist thou dy
711Unpitied let thy cold ashes ly
712Unburied; let Nimphs and Shepherds cease
713Passing to say, Soule, do thou rest in Peace;
714Let the raine wet thee, move thee may the wind;
715Let flocks and strangers too, be so unkind,
716As for to trample on thee, not to be nam’d;
717Horrid and Monstrous, ʼtis not to be told,
718How Love is made the Price of abject Gold.
719But, why in vaine lament I? since I see,
720Beasts, for their safety, use those Armes, which be,
721Allotted them by Nature, Tigers paw’s
722Use for defence, Lions their teeth and claw’s;
723The Stag does for her safety use to fly,
724A womans weapon in her Beauty. Why?
725(Then since all these do use their nat’ral armes
726To conquer foes, or save themselves from harms)
727Should not I when that nature ha’s me made
728Apt for to ravish, make use of the trade?
729I’l force, I’l ravish what she me denies,
730Nor will be moved with her pray’rs nor cries.
731Not long ago I told was by a Swain,
732That in the fountain lies in yonder plain;
733She often baths her self, there I intend
734To hide me in the bushes, to the end
735That when she comes I may be sure to take
736Hold on her; what resistance can she make
737ʼGainst me, poor tender soule? and for her cries,
738I’l neither them nor of her beauty prize
739The power. Oh if I can but once entwine,
740This hand within her locks, why then she’s mine,
741Nor shal the gods release her, til that I
742For my revenge my armes in blood do dy.

Scen. 2.

Daphne and Thirsis

DAPHNE

743Thirsis, as I thee told, I long since thought,
744Amintas did love Silvia, and have sought,
745God knows, all way’s to further this his love,
746And shall the more, since thou art pleas’d to move
747Me in it: but I rather had by far
748Chuse for to tame a lion, or fierce bear,
749Then such a simple girle, who does not know
750How piercing be her beauties armes, and though
751She others kils, yet is her self stil sound,
752And wounding others knows, not how to wound.

THIRSIS

753And where’s that child so simple han’t a mind,
754As soon as out of swathling bands, to find
755Arts to seem handsom and to make her please?
756And how to kil with pleasing? and with ease
757Can tell what armes they be cause death? nay more,
758What armes they be which life again restore:

DAPHNE

759Who mistresse is of so much art?

THIRSIS

Who mistresse is of so much art? As though
760Thou knewst not Daphne, ‘tis the same doth shew
761Flight to the winged birds, and doth infuse
762Swimming to fishes, teaches Buls to use
763Their hornes, and makes Juno’s proud bird to spread
764Her Argus-eye-deckt feathers o’re his head:

DAPHNE

765I’d very fain know how you cal this same
766Mistress of arts?

THIRSIS

Mistress of arts? Why Daphne is her name.

DAPHNE

767Our filthy liar that thou art.

THIRSIS

Our filthy liar that thou art. Why
768Art thou not able then to keep at school (fool
769A thousand girls, although no need there is
770In this same Art of Love of Mistresses,
771For nature in their mistresse, though, ʼtis true,
772The mother and the nurse bear a share too.

DAPHNE

773Come, thou too knavish art, in sum I’l tel
774Thee that I am not yet resolved well,
775If Silvia be so simple as the feigns
776Her self to be; for down in yonder plains
777Where the clear waters of the silent lake
778Incircled round a pretty Island make,
779I t’other day saw Silvia counsel take,
780How she the golden tresses of her head
781In pretty curls might o’re her forehead spread,
782And then how she might fittest o’re them place
783Her snow-white vail, o’re that with better grace
784Order some fragrant flowers, with lilies fair
785Her white neck she’d adorn; and so compare
786Which was the whitest, now a lovely rose,
787To her sweet cheeks to see which did disclose
788The liveliest hue; then with delight would she
789Smile, as ʼtwere boasting of the victory:
790Methought she seem’d to say, I onely do
791For your disgrace base flowers carry you,
792Not for my ornament, since all may see
793How much in beauty you must yeild to me;
794But whilst she flatt’red thus her self, her eye
795By chance she turn’d, and turning did espie
796That I sat laughing in th’adjacent bowers,
797At which she blush’d, and strait let fall her flowers;
798When laugh the more to see her blush did I,
799Which her cheeks tinged in a deeper dy;
800But cause that onely on one side her head
801Her hair was gathered, on the other spread;
802She fearful I should see again, poor soule,
803At ‘twere from the clear fountain counsel stole,
804And though she were undrest, yet pleas’d was she
805That, so undrest, she look’d so handsomely:
806I saw it and was silent.

THIRSIS

I saw it and was silent. Thou dost tell
807What I before told thee; guest I not well?

DAPHNE

808Well didst thou guesse, but I have been told how
809Pastors and nymphs were not so sly as now
810In former times, nor young was I so bold:
811The world do’s grow more wicked, as more old.

THIRSIS

812ʼTis true that then Citizens us’d not so
813Oft in the Countrey come, nor Rusticks go
814Unto the City; now their crafty race
815As well as customes do with us take place;
816But to our purpose canst not thou procure,
817That the hard-hearted Silvia may endure
818To hear Amintas speak alone; or’t least
819In company with thee, which she’l think best:

DAPHNE

820I’ll tell thee, Silvia now so coy doth grow
821That well how to obtain it I don’t know.

THIRSIS

822Amintas too respectfully is nice.

DAPHNE

823True sayst thou Thirsis; there can no worse vice
824In lovers be; he that would learn the art
825Of divine love must lay respect apart,
826Must dare demand, solicite, importune;
827And if by these means he can’t overcome,
828Must ravish too: Thirsis, thou knowst so well,
829That I am sure there’s none need thee to tell,
830The nature of a woman, if she flies
831ʼTis cause she’d be pursu’d; if she deny’s,
832Alas ʼtis onely cause that she would have
833Men to force that which first they did but crave,
834She fights to be o’recome: laugh not at me
835That I speak thus in confidence to thee,
836And that I speak in rimes, but thou knowst, Thirsis,
837I can for rimes return thee more than verses.

THIRSIS

838Thou hast no reason to suspect that I
839Should e’r tell ought against thy modesty.
840But I conjure thee, Daphne, by the dear
841Remembrance of thy youth that thou wilt here
842Lend thy assistance with me for relief
843Of poor Amintas, who else dies with grief.

DAPHNE

844How neatly he conjures me, by the joy
845Of mine that’s past, and by my present’noy?
846But what wilt have me do?

THIRSIS

But what wilt have me do? Judgement and skil
847I know thou hast, then onely, that thou will.

DAPHNE

848In sum I’l tell thee then Silvi’ and I
849This day at Dians well, which is hard by,
850Intend to wash, where an inviting shade
851For nymphs and shepherds by a beech is made;
852And there she’l naked bath her tender wast.

THIRSIS

853What then?

DAPHNE

What then? What then! if thou hast wit thou maist
854Guest at the rest.

THIRSIS

Guest at the rest. I guest, but do not know
855Whether he’l have the heart to come or no.

DAPHNE

856If come he won’t, for ought that I can say,
857Stay until she looks after him he may.

THIRSIS

858He so deserves.

DAPHNE

He so deserves. But, Thirsis, may not we
859(At leisure now) talk somehow too of thee?
860Why dost not thou a sweet-heart get? Alas
861Thou’rt yet but young! and scarce dost four years passe
862Of the fifth lustre (and full well do I
863Know when these arms have danc’t thee lullaby)
864Why wilt neglected live, and void of joy?
865Since without love there’s no delight (my boy)

THIRSIS

866A man may shun fond love, and yet not fast
867From Venus pleasures, he the sweet do’s taste
868Without the bitter.

DAPHNE

Without the bitter. Most unsavorie ʼtis:
869The sweet’s not seasoned with some bitterness
870Soon satiate.

THIRSIS

Soon satiate. Better once satisfide:
871Then before meat and after to abide
872Stil hungry.

DAPHNE

Stil hungry. Thirsis, if the meat you have
873doth please; the more you tast, the more you crave

THIRSIS

874But who can alwaies present have that food
875Which, though he’s hungry, to his taste is good.

DAPHNE

876Who seeks no good shal find none

THIRSIS

Who seeks no good shal find none There’s no ease
877In seeking that, which found, ʼtis true doth please;
878But if not found when sought torments us more.
879Thirsis wil ne’r a lover be, before
880That cruel love shal leave it’s company
881Of sighs and tears, enough already I (though
882Have sigh’d and wept.

DAPHNE

Have sigh’d and wept. Enough enjoyed
883Perhaps that hast not.

THIRSIS

Perhaps that hast not. Nor desire, if so
884Dear I must but it.

DAPHNE

Dear I must but it. Well if nothing move
885Thee can, at length thou wilt be forc’d to love:

THIRSIS

886Love cannot force who’s out of his command.

DAPHNE

887But who is’t can out of loves empire stand?

THIRSIS

888Who fears his witching charms and flies away.

DAPHNE

889Where wilt thou fly if love his wings display?

THIRSIS

890But love when young ha’s his wings short, and so
891Can’t overtake the man will from him go. (cry,

DAPHNE

892The birth of love, there’s no man can dis-
893And when discri’d, his wings grown great, he’l fly.

THIRSIS

894Some by experience his first birth can tel.

DAPHNE

895Thirsis, since thou pretendst to know so wel
896That Art, we’l see if thou canst from him run,
897This I protest to thee, I’l not be won
898A hand, a foot, or eye-brow for to move,
899Either to help or aid thee in thy love.

THIRSIS

900Fy cruel one then couldst thou dead me see?
901If thou wilt have me love, why, love thou me.

DAPHNE

902Why doft thou mock me, Thirsis? perhaps thou
903Dost not deserve so fine a mistresse; how
904Many a colour’d and smooth face beguiles.

THIRSIS

905I do not mock thee; no, but thou mean-whiles
906With this pretext my love dost not accept;
907This is the common trick: but if reject
908My love thou dost, I without love will live.

DAPHNE

909Thirsis, maist thou contently live and thrive,
910More then e’re yet thou didst, maist live in ease
911And leisure, without which love ne’re doth please.

THIRSIS

912Daphne, this leisure god hath granted me,
913One who a god may here esteemed be;
914To whom on verdant plains and cliffy rocks,
915From see to see we feed our herds and flocks:
916Thirsis, said he, let others take a care,
917To chase the wolves, and thieves, let others share
918Rewards unto the servants; others guard
919My wal’d in sheep; thou (from these labours bar’d,)
920Shalt sing: then just it is my pipe should move,
921Not in fond fancies of a worldly love;
922But that in higher strains it onely hollow
923The ancestors of my Jove, or Apollo.
924(For which of those to call him well, in troth,
925I know not, since he do’s resemble both)
926Progenitors of greater worth by far,
927Then or bright Saturn or the Heavens are;
928(Low Muse to such a merit,) but yet he,
929Sing I or clear or hoarse, rejects not me;
930His praise I dare not chant, nor well can I
931Worthily honour him, but silently,
932And with due reverence; but his altars ne’r
933Without my flowers or sweet perfumes yet were
934And when I do forget him to adore,
935Or worship; then let Thirsis be no more:
936Let Rivers change their beds, the soane advance
937To wash the Persian banks, the Tigris France.

DAPHNE

938High ho, thou’rt flown too high, prethee descend
939Unto our purpose.

THIRSIS

Unto our purpose. Daphne, I intend,
940That thou shouldst as thou go’st along the way
941Endeavour for to soften Silvia;
942I’l procure that he come, this task of mine
943I doubt will harder be by far than thine,
944Then go thy waies:

DAPHNE

Then go thy waies: I do, but I did mean
945By this talk part of which thou didst not dream;

THIRSIS

946If I can wel discern him by the face,
947Aminta ʼtis appears in yonder place.

Scen. 3.

Aminta Thirsis

AMINTA

948I’ll see what Thirsis may have done for me,
949In what to do he promis’d; but if he
950Have nothing done, why then, before that I
951Will be consum’d to nothing, I will die
952Before the rigid Silvia; that abate
953My death, may somewhat of her cruell hate,
954She (that so pleas’d is with that plague, the Dart
955Of her fair ey’s ha’s gi’n my love-sick heart)
956Wil with that wound which my brest shal endure
957From mine own hands be doubly pleas’d I’m sure.

THIRSIS

958I new’s of comfort bring thee, and content,
959Then deare Aminta, cease thus to lament.

AMINTA

960Why say’st thou Thirsis, must I live or die?

THIRSIS

961I bring thee life and safety, for if I
962The other had, I’d not it bring, but need
963There is of courage, man, and that with speed?

AMINTA

964What need is there of courage, pray and where
965Must I employ it?

THIRSIS

Must I employ it? It thy Silvia were
966Within a wood, encompass’d round with rocks,
967Where lions and fierce Tigers by whole flocks
968Did range, no way to scape by strength or art,
969To go to rescue her, would’st have the heart?

AMINTA

970More joyfull farr then on a holy day,
971A Countrey wench doth run to dance or play.

THIRSIS

972If the ʼmongst murd’rers were, I fain would know,
973Whether thou dar’st into her rescue go.

AMINTA

974I’d go more readier then the Stag does fly
975To the long-with’d-for fountain, when he’s dry.

THIRSIS

976But greater proofes more courage do require.

AMINTA

977I would for Silvia’s sake passe through the fire,
978Or through the floods, when down the Mountains throw,
979With a full torrent, their dissolved snow;
980I’d go to hell it selfe, if hell could be,
981Where there is one so fair so good as she;
982But tell me pray, where is’t?

THIRSIS

But tell me pray, where is’t? Hark then.

AMINTA

983Go on.

THIRSIS

Go on. Silvia attends thee naked and alone,
984At the same fountain call’d Diana’s well;
985Dar’st thou to go.

AMINTA

Dar’st thou to go. Alas! what dost thou tell
986Me, Thirsis? naked and alone do’s she
987Stay for me, sayst thou?

THIRSIS

Stay for me, sayst thou? Unless Daphne be
988There (who of our side is) alone I say.

AMINTA

989But naked prethee do’s she for me stay?

THIRSIS

990I say she there stayes naked for thee, but-

AMINTA

991But what? thou kilst me it that word thou put.

THIRSIS

992But I can’t tel if thou hadst best to go.

AMINTA

993Oh hard conclusion! which all the forego-
994Ing sweetness doth imbitter: with what art
995Doth strive to wound my almost bloodlesse heart?
996Is’t not enough that thus unhappy I
997Am, but thou wilt increase my misery?

THIRSIS

998Aminta, if thou wilt my counsel take,
999Thou mayst be happy:

AMINTA

Thou mayst be happy: What, for the gods sake,
1000Dost thou advise me;

THIRSIS

Dost thou advise me; What! That thou be bold,
1001And on what fortune thee presents lay hold.

AMINTA

1002The gods I hope will keep me that I may
1003Ne’re think a thought to displease Silvia:
1004I ne’re displeas’d her yet, but by my love,
1005Nor was’t my fault, her beauties power did move
1006Me to’t: but let me when I cease to strive
1007Silvia to please, no longer care to live.

THIRSIS

1008Answer me now, if in thy Pow’r it were
1009To leave her love, wouldst do’t to pleasure her

AMINTA

1010Love lets me not say so, may’t does deny
1011That I should think to leave her love, though I
1012Could do’t.

THIRSIS

Could do’t. Why then though leave her love thou could’st;
1013Yet, in despight of her, love her thou would’st.

AMINTA

1014Not in despight, but yet I’d love her still.

THIRSIS

1015Why then thou love would’st against her will.

AMINTA

1016Yes, certainly I should.

THIRSIS

Yes, certainly I should. Then dar’st thou not
1017Against her will (fool as thou art) take what
1018At first may hard and heavy seem, once past
1019Sweeter and sweeter growes unto the last.

AMINTA

1020Thirsis, love for me answers, and my heart
1021Know’s it’s own means, but cannot then impart,
1022Thee constant use ha’s skil’d in loves great Art,
1023But that ha’s bound my tongue which bound my heart.

THIRSIS

1024Why then we wil not go.

AMINTA

Why then we wil not go. Yes go wil I:
1025But not where thou dost deem.

THIRSIS

But not where thou dost deem. Whither?

AMINTA

1026To die;
1027To die alas I’l go, if this be all
1028The favour thou hast done me.

THIRSIS

The favour thou hast done me. Think’st it small?
1029Think’st Daphne counsel would to go, if find
1030In part she did not that ʼtwas Silvia’s mind?
1031And who can tell, it may be Silvia too
1032Know’s it her selfe, but would not have that you
1033Should know she knows it; now, if thou dost crave
1034Her full consent, thou do’st desire to have
1035What would displease her most, and then (foole) where
1036Is that desire of thine to pleasure her?
1037Or if she would that thy injoyment were
1038Thy theft, and not her gift, what need’st thou care?

AMINTA

1039But who assures me such is her desire?

THIRSIS

1040Why see? thou do’st that certainty require,
1041Which does and ought displease her, and which thou
1042Ought’st not to seek: but pray imagine now
1043That such were her desire, and thou not go,
1044Equal the doubt is, the losse equal; so,
1045I think it better that couragiusly
1046Thou like a man, than like a coward, die.
1047Ar’t silent? thou’rt o’recome, confesse I pray
1048This love Aminta; it occasion may
1049Thy greater Vict’ry; let’s go then.

AMINTA

Thy greater Vict’ry; let’s go then. I pray.

THIRSIS

1050What, stay! seest not how fast the time does run?

AMINTA

1051Pray let us think first wht is to be done.

THIRSIS

1052We by the way wil think the rest, but who
1053Do’s too much think things, seldom wel doth do.

CHORUS

1054Love, who can teach us thy mysterious art?
1055Who thy divinest fancies can impart?
1056Where may we learne them? who can them display?
1057Since (though the mind them comprehend) away?
1058Arm’d with thy wing’d prowes, they soare above,
1059Not learned Athens ere the art of love
1060Could teach, nor its Liceo could it shew,
1061Phebus in Helicon thy art ne’r knew;
1062He could discourse of love, ʼtis true, but so,
1063As if he did thy art but blindly know,
1064Or were a learner, cold were his desires,
1065Nor did his voice burn with celestiall fires,
1066As fits thy power, nor could his thoughts arise
1067To comprehend thy sacred mysteries;
1068Thou only, Love, a worthy master art,
1069In thy sublime, and more then heav’nly art;
1070To rustick brests thou mak’st known those Divine
1071And most Celestiall Characters of thine,
1072Writ by thine own hand in anothers eye,
1073Thy faithfull servants tongues thou dost untie:
1074And mak’st them glide in pure and nobler stream’s,
1075Then ever issu’d from Poetick veines;
1076By thee divinest love there’s more exprest
1077In broken words and speeches, then the best
1078Of Orators can do, and more doth move
1079Thy silence, then their Rhetorick, to love:
1080Heart-conquering, Love to others, leave will I
1081To learn thy art in blind philosophy,
1082Or from the learned schooles: I only will
1083In faire ey’s study this mysterious skill;
1084And their high stiles and Poetry will lesse,
1085Then shall my rough or rustick rimes, express:
