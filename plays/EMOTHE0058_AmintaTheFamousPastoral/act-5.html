Act V

Scen. 1.

Elpino Chore of Shepherds.

ELPINO

1666In truth, the divine law by which love sway’s
1667His sacred Empire is not hard nor stray’s
1668In oblique paths, sage providence the guide
1669Is of his workers, and all his actions glide
1670In deepest mysteries, see by what art
1671By what strange uncouth meanes he leads a heart
1672To happinesse! and when man thinks that he
1673Is now arriv’d at th’depth of misery
1674And wretchednesse, why then the winged boy
1675Transports him to the paradise of joy.
1676Behold Aminta, by this fall ascends
1677To his long with’d-for, and desired ends;
1678Happy Aminta, happy so much more,
1679By how much more mis’rable thou before;
1680By thy example why should I despaire,
1681But rather hope that cruell one, though faire,
1682My wounded heart, and may true pity have
1683Who under smiles of pity doth conceale
1684Her mortall rigor, now at length may heale
1685To cure that wound, her feigned pity gave.

CHORUS

1686See how the sage Elpino yonder walkes,
1687And to himselfe of poor Aminta talkes,
1688As though he living were; he fortunate
1689And happy calls him, O the sad estate
1690Of lovers! in his judgement so misled,
1691To call those happy, who find pity dead?
1692Is this the paradise of joy? is this
1693His wish’d contentment, his desired blisse?
1694Is this the great reward, the greatest ease
1695Love gives his servants? can this so much please?
1696Can this suffice? Elpino, is so bad,
1697Thy luck, that thou stil’st fortunate the sad
1698Death of Aminta? such an end do’st crave?

ELPINO

1699No Shepherds, no; but let your sorrows have
1700An end; false the report is, he was dead.

CHORUS

1701O! how much comfort hath this good newʼs bred?
1702Did he not then himselfe precipitate?

ELPINO

1703He did, but his more than thrice blessed fate
1704Under deaths dolefull image did him give
1705Both life and joy: shepherds, he still doth live,
1706And lives content; for now his head doth rest
1707On his deare Nymphs so much desired breast;
1708She whom before, nor tears, nor sighs could move
1709To pity him, is now as full of love,
1710Is now as mercifull; and from his eyʼs
1711With her own mouth his teares of joy she dryʼs.
1712I go to seek her father, old Montane,
1713And to conduct him unto yonder plaine
1714Where they two be, for nothing to the fill
1715Of their content there wants, but his good will.

CHORUS

1716Equal their birth is, equal are their years
1717Their wills agree, and good Montane, who bears
1718Defiers to have nephews, and to fence,
1719His drooping age with such a sweet defence,
1720Will make his will be theirs; but dear Elpine
1721Relate to us what fate, what god divine
1722Could of our poor Aminta such care have
1723As might in that fame despʼrate fall him save.

ELPINO

1724I am content to tell it, and no man
1725I’m sure can tell it better then I can.
1726Just at the entrance of my cave stood I,
1727Which you know well at the hils foot doth lie;
1728I reasoning was of her, who in a net
1729First Thirsis caught, and after me, and yet
1730I did my thraldom far prefer above
1731His life of freedom, or his flight from love,
1732When a strange voice our ears and ey’s did call
1733Up to the top, from whence to see one fall,
1734And see him on a bush of briers light,
1735Which stood in the mid-way, was all one sight;
1736Nature had planted on the mountain’s side
1737A sett of thornes, so thick, that they seem’d ti’d,
1738Or wove together, here at first he fell,
1739Which, though ʼtwas like a net, yet could not well
1740His bodi’s weight susteine, but let him go
1741Quite through, that at our feet he fell; yet so
1742Much of the force and shock it took away,
1743That ʼtwas not mortall, only there he lay
1744Astonished, and void of sence. Amaz’d
1745We at the sad chance stood, but when we gaz’d
1746More neerly on, and knew him, then our trouble,
1747Our sorrow, and our anguish did redouble;
1748But to our griefe it was some small reprieve
1749That he was not quite dead, perhaps might live;
1750Then Thirsis t’ me the secrets did discover
1751Of his disconsolate and desp’rate lover
1752We knowing ʼtwas in vain him to lament
1753Bethought a remedy; and while we sent
1754For learned Esculapius, whom Apollo
1755Taught, Physicks art when he him us’d to follow:
1756At the same time he did on me bestow
1757The skil to tune a harp, and bend a bow.
1758Silvia and Daphne thither seeking came
1759That body which they did believe was slain;
1760When Silvia saw Aminta’s cheeks once fair
1761Now void of life, now grown so pallid were,
1762So wan that the bleak violet display’s
1763Not half that palenesse scorch’d by Phebus rayes:
1764She stupifi’d neer void of sense doth rest
1765Lamenting, crying, beating her fair breast
1766She on him falls, to take her latest kisse,
1767And joyn’d his face to hers, her mouth to his.

CHORUS

1768Could she that was so coy, now not restrain
1769Her passion but extend the bounds of shame.

ELPINO

1770Weak love is held by shame, but love grows bold
1771As strong, what is it then can it with-hold:
1772She as though in her ey’s she did contain
1773Fountains of tears, did with such plenty rain
1774Them on his cheeks, and they such vertue had,
1775That it reviv’d again the breathlesse lad;
1776His ey’s he weakly opens, and he sends
1777From his afflicted soule a sigh, which ends
1778In his dear Silvia’s spirit, by her sweet
1779Mouth it was catch’t and seem’d with it to meet;
1780Her Echo quite enliv’ned him, but who
1781Could now declare the joy possest these two;
1782Aminta thought ʼtwas more then heav’nly charms,
1783That thus enclasp’d him in his Silvia’s armes;
1784He that loves servant is, perhaps may guesse
1785Their blisse; but none there is can it expresse.

CHORUS

1786And is Aminta fully cur’d canst tell?
1787No danger of his life?

ELPINE

No danger of his life? He’s fully well:
1788ʼTis true he’s somewhat hat bruis’d, but nothing he
1789Do’s it account, and nothing it will be;
1790Thrice happy he who did so fully prove
1791Love’s worst, and now doth tast the sweets of love;
1792His sufferings and his torments onely be
1793Sweet seasoning of his felicitie.
1794Shepherds farewel. For I must once again
1795Make haste to find out the good old Montane.

CHORUS

1796Can that Martyrdom he prov’d,
1797While he serv’d, and while he lov’d,
1798While he hop’d, while de dispair’d,
1799While he sigh’d, and while he car’d,
1800Ever recompensed be
1801Though with joy’s eternally?
1802What if sweet things sweeter more
1803Are when bitter go before?
1804What if good things better will
1805Be, if savour’d with some ill?
1806Yet Love I pray give me the lesse
1807Not this greater blessednesse.
1808If thou wilt, blesse others so,
1809Who this full blisse crave to know:
1810But let me my nymph enjoy,
1811For a word or for a toy,
1812Without sighs, and without care,
1813Without tears, without despair.
1814Let the season’ngs of our love,
1815Such be as may onely move,
1816(Not such torments or such pain)
1817Sweet repulses, sweet disdain;
1818To which kind kisses may succeed,
2397To show our hearts are still agreed.
