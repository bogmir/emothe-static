Act III

Scen. 1.

Thirsis Chorus of Shepherds

THIRSIS

1086O Horrid cruelty! unheard! unseen!
1087Or ever yet remembered to have been
1088In humane brest; three hundred times, nay four
1089Ingratefull sex; thou nymph ingrateful more.
1090Why didst thou, Nature, show thy utmost art,
1091Thus in adorning womans outward part?
1092Why did’st her beaut’ous countenance indue
1093With such a sun-like soul-bewitching hue?
1094And didst afford to grace her mind within
1095Nothing but falsenesse, cruelty and sin.
1096Negligent Mistresse! But ay me, my dear
1097Aminta will have slain himself, I fear;
1098ʼTis full three houres that round about the ground,
1099Where I him left I sought him, but have found
1100Nor him, nor yet his footsteps, ’tis too plain
1101May tell me news of him, friends did you see
1102I clearly see that he himself hath slain;
1103Aminta or hear of him?
1104But stay, perhaps those shepherds yonder be

CHORUS

1105Thou dost show
1106So troubled; that the cause we fain would know
1107Of this thy grief; from whence proceeds this sweat?
1108This sorrow; tel us if thou think’st it meet.

THIRSIS

1109I fear Aminta’s ill, did you him see?

CHORUS

1110No him we have not seen, since that from thee
1111Long since he parted; but pray tell us plain
1112What dost thou fear?

THIRSIS

What dost thou fear? Lest he himself have slain.

CHORUS

1113That he ha’s slain himself! but what could move
1114Him such an act to do?

THIRSIS

Him such an act to do? Hatred and love.

CHORUS

1115Who two such potent enemies don’t fear?
1116What can’t they do? but tel us pray more clear.

THIRSIS

1117His great love of a nymph, and her no smal
1118Hatred of him.

CHORUS

Hatred of him. Nay prethee tell us all;
1119This is a place of passage, and whilst you
1120Relate it, news may brought be; perhaps too
1121Himself may come mean-while.

THIRSIS

Himself may come mean-while. I willingly
1122Recount it wil, that the just infamy
1123Of such a strange ingratitude may rest
1124Upon that cruel and remorselest breast;
1125Aminta told was (I alas was he
1126That told it him and he was led by me,
1127Now I repent me) that his Silvia dear
1128This day with Daphne in a fountain clear
1129Would bath her naked limbs, he thither went
1130Not mov’d by his desire, but to content
1131My importunities: oft back have gone
1132He would; but that I still did thrust him on;
1133Now when we come were to the fountain nigh;
1134We thought we heard a womans woefull cry,
1135And not far off we Daphne saw, her ey’s
1136Swollen with tears, when she saw us, her cries
1137Did pierce the heav’ns, run and make haste, said she,
1138Silvia is forc’d. Amintas presently,
1139Not staying to hear more, flew away so
1140Swift as I never yet saw swifter Do:
1141I followed him; when, lo, we straightaway see
1142The beauteous Silvia ti’d unto a tree;
1143The rope to tie her was her brightest hair,
1144Which in a thousand knots intangled were
1145About the plants; that girdle us’d to be
1146The former warder of her chastitie,
1147Was us’d now to her rape, and serv’d to bind
1148Unto the tree her snow-white hands behind.
1149The very plant it self did condescend
1150To this foule act, and seem’d as ’twere to lend
1151It’s ful assistance to the rape; for round
1152About her legs two pliant twigs were bound;
1153Just before her a cruel Satyre stood,
1154With looks denoting villany and blood,
1155Who finisht had to bind her; she poor heart
1156Did strive with all her force and all her art,
1157But what (alas!) could all her force or skil
1158Prevail ’gainst him who had such strength at will;
1159Aminta with his sharp wel-brandish’d dart
1160Aim’d at, but mist, the cursed Satyres heart;
1161I gather’d up what stones I could, but he
1162Seeing us two, thought it was best to flee:
1163We him pursu’d, Aminta as his flight
1164Gave leave, turn’d back his ey’s to have the sight
1165Of her fair members, which more soft by far
1166Seem’d than Swans down, and whiter much than are
1167The snow-deck’d Alpes, but when we had in vain
1168Pursu’d the rogue, he turning back again
1169Accosts her thus, Pardon, O Silvia fair,
1170These hands of mine, which with such boldness dare
1171Approach thy members, for they’re forc’d to loose
1172These knots which ministred to thy abuse;
1173Then since that fortune has been pleas’d to send
1174Them such a blisse, let it not thee offend.

CHORUS

1175Words that would mollifie a heart of stone!
1176What answer did she gave him them?

THIRSIS

What answer did she gave him them? Why none:
1177But looking down disdainfully, she tride,
1178All that she could, her fairest breasts to hide;
1179He from the tree begins her hair to loose;
1180And thus he seem’d to say, Durst thou abuse
1181These golden curls, base plant? which do to thee
1182So great an honour; ah, unworthy be
1183Thy branches of such knots, what vantage pray
1184Can we poor Lovers boast of? Since trees may
1185Enjoy those comforts which we want, entwine
1186By force those locks which blesse these hands of mine
1187By their bare touch; this done he from behind
1188Unties the knot, and does her hands unbind;
1189In manner so as if he seem’d to fear
1190Their touch, and yet to touch could not forbear;
1191Then stoops that he might loose her feet, but she
1192Finding her hands were now at libertie,
1193Looks on him scornfully, says touch not, I
1194Am Dians nymph. I can my feet untie

CHORUS

1195Can so much pride reign in so fair a heart?
1196O for so good an act ingrateful part!,

THIRSIS

1197He did respectfully himself retire
1198Not looking on her, though he did desire,
1199Deny’d himself that pleasure, which on trial
1200He fear’d would give her trouble of denial;
1201I heard and saw all this, and hid did lie,
1202And though about to check her cruelty;
1203Yet I with-held: but heare the most unkind
1204Part of all yet; she did at length unbind
1205Her self, scarce loose, nor bidding him adieu,
1206Faster then fleetest Stag away she flew;
1207And yet to fear I’m sure she cause had none,
1208For his respect to her was ful wel known.

CHORUS

1209Why did she fly then?

THIRSIS

Why did she fly then? She would of her rape
1210The rescue attribute to her escape,
1211Not to his modest love.

CHORUS

Not to his modest love. Ingrateful too
1212In this! but what said he? what did he do?

THIRSIS

1213I know not, I vext at her rigor, ran
1214To overtake and hold her but in vain;
1215For soon I lost her track, then turning, where
1216I left Aminta, could not find him there;
1217My heart presages ill, for this I’m sure
1218He’d rather die than this sad chance endure:

CHORUS

1219The custom ʼtis (we know) of those that are
1220In love, to threaten death, but very rare
1221Are those effect it.

THIRSIS

Are those effect it. I’l go to the cave
1222Of sage Elpino, where I sure shal have
1223News of him if he lives, for that’s his hant;
1224There on his oaten pipe he us’d to chant
1225Sweet songs, and there lament him of his love,
1226And make the very rocks and mountains move;
1227The Rivers stop their course to hear his layes,
1228The trees incline, and wild beasts leave their preys.

Scen. 2.

Aminta Daphne Nerina.

AMINTA

1229Pitilesse pity Daphne sure was thine,
1230When thou with-heldst this hand, this dart of mine;
1231That so my death being delay’d by fate
1232Might be more bitter by how much more late;
1233And why dost trace me thus? or thinkst to force
1234Hope into me by this thy vain discourse:
1235What dost thou fear? that I my self should slay,
1236Thou fear’st my good; then prethee goe away;

DAPHNE

1237Despair not thus Aminta, for if I
1238Know Silvia wel, ʼtwas shame that made her fly,
1239Not cruelty.

AMINTA

Not cruelty. Alas! there is for me
1240No way but to despair, since hope wil be
1241As’t ha’s been yet my ruine, stil I find
1242It strives as ʼtwere to blossom in my mind;
1243And whispers to me live, and can ought be
1244Worse then a life, to such a wretch as me?

DAPHNE

1245Live wretch, live stil I say, and let this be
1246Support unto thee in thy misery;
1247That if thy hope in life do but maintain thee,
1248At length thar naked fair one it will gain thee.

AMINTA

1249Both love and fortune, though that at the brink
1250Of wrechednesse I was, yet did not think
1251Me yet forlorn enough, until that I
1252Had fully seen what fully both denie.

NERINA

1253Must I then alwaies have the luck to be
1254The messenger of such sad news? ay me!
1255Wretched Mantano! what sad heart wil thine
1256Be when thou com’st to hear this newes of mine?
1257O sad and des’late father thou, nay rather,
1258By losse of thy dear Silvia, no more Father!

DAPHNE

1259I a sad voice to hear.

AMINTA

I a sad voice to hear. And I hear sound
1260The name of Silvia, which my heart do’s wound.
1261Who is it names her?

DAPHNE

Who is it names her? it seemes at first sight
1262To be Nerina, Cynthia’s cheife delight,
1263Who ha’s so sweet comportment, so fair face,
1264Such charming ey’s, such a bewitching Grace.

NERINA

1265And yet alas, I’m forc’d to let him know
1266Of this most fatal chance of thine, that so
1267He may seek those unhappy bones, if no
1268More rests of thee; oh! my deare Silvia, oh!

AMINTA

1269Ay me, what say’s she!

NERINA

Ay me, what say’s she! Daphne,

DAPHNE

Ay me, what say’s she! Daphne, what, and why
1270Do’st to thy self name Silvia, and then cry.

NERINA

1271Alas! with reason I lament her sad
1272And cruel fate;

AMINTA

And cruel fate; What fate can be so bad
1273To make thee thus lament? Ay me, I feele
1274My heart a dying, as though pierc’d with steele,
1275Is she alive?

DAPHNE

Is she alive? Prethee to us relate
1276What thus thou mutter’st of her desp’rate fate.

NERINA

1277Ye Gods! why am I messenger? yet I
1278Must tel’t: did she, you know the reason why
1279Come e’n now naked to my Lodg, and there
1280Re-cloath’d her selfe again; she by her pray’r
1281Prevail’d with me, that I would with her go
1282In Eliceto for to hunt a Do.
1283I pleas’d her, and we went when, neer the ground,
1284Gather’d together many Nymphs we found;
1285Scarce had we spoke to them, when lo we see
1286A mighty wolfe start from behind a tree,
1287Great out of measure was he, and with blood
1288His nose was di’d, he fiercely looking stood;
1289Silvia streight to her bow an arrow fits,
1290And she nere us’d to misse; so now she hits
1291Him just upon the head, he fly’s again
1292Into the wood, she followes him amaine.

AMINTA

1293O sad beginning, which do’s pierce my heart!
1294What end wilt have?

NERINA

What end wilt have? I with another dart
1295Follow’d her track, but at great distance, since
1296She was i’th wood ere I could move me thence,
1297Yet I her footsteps made a shift to trace,
1298Until I came to the most desert place
1299Of all the wood, where, lo, by chance I found
1300My dearest Silvia’s dart upon the ground,
1301And not farr off from it I did espy
1302That snow-white vaile, which I my selfe did ty
1303Upon her head, which as you see is di’d
1304With crimson gore, and looking round I spi’d
1305Seven fierce wolves, which seem’d to lick the blood
1306Of a nak’d body, about which they stood,
1307Such was my hap, and so intent they were
1308Upon their prey, they saw not I was there;
1309So I return’d, and this is the full tale
1310I tell of Silvia can, see here the vaile.

AMINTA

1311Th’ast told too much, this vaile, and this blood be
1312Sure tokens of her death.

DAPHNE

Sure tokens of her death. Ah poore wretch! he
1313Is dead with greife too.

NERINA

Is dead with greife too. No, he breathes, revives,
1314ʼTis but a sudden fainting, see he lives.

AMINTA

1315Greise, why? (alas!) do’st thou torment me so,
1316And do’st not kill outright? if thou too flow
1317Beest, or wouldst have my hand the instrument
1318To do’t, to this I’me very well content;
1319And it shal execute that death which thou
1320Refusest, or to do, or know’st not how;
1321Then since the certainty of this we know,
1322And nothing wants now to augment my woe;
1323Why should I longer stay? oh Daphne! why
1324Did’st not permit me, when I would, to die?
1325To this sad end reserv’dst thou me? to this!
1326Sure sweet my death had been, nay more a blisse,
1327And greatest happinesse, had but that dart,
1328Which cruel thou withhelden, pier’d my heart.
1329But heav’ns deni’d it, and did feare least that
1330I by my sudden death forerun should, what
1331They had ordain’d me, now my utmost ill
1332They executed have, permit they will
1333To make the full summe of their cruelty:
1334And thou permi too must) that I may dy.

DAPHNE

1335Restrain this frantick humour of thy youth,
1336Until we do heare further of the truth.

AMINTA

1337Why do’st thou strive to keep me off with such
1338Delay’s? I’ve staid too long, and heard too much.

NERINA

1339I would I had been dumb.

AMINTA

I would I had been dumb. Nymph, pray bestow
1340On me that blood-bespotted Vail; that so
1341Small, but whole relique of my Silvia;
1342That since no more there rests of her, it may
1343Witnesse my death, and if there ought can be
1344To do’t may serve t’augment my misery;
1345Though, I confest, ʼtis not so small that I
1346Need to increase it, to perswade me dy.

NERINA

1347Had I best giv’t him, or deny’t him? I
1348Think for th’occasion I were best deny.

AMINTA

1349What cruell one! so small a gift to me,
1350Who am resolv’d nere to ask more of thee?
1351In this too wretched fate, I yeeld with you,
1352Then let the Vaile remain, and so adieu;
1353I go to turn no more.

DAPHNE

I go to turn no more. Aminta, stay,
1354Gods! with what fury do’s he fly away?

NERINA

1355He flies so fast, that it will be in vaine
1356To follow him, I down in yonder plaine
1357Wil seek Montano, but I do not know
1358If I were best to tell the newes or no.

CHORUS

1359There is no cause, which death may move
1360To bind him, ha’s a noble heart;
1361His faith’s enough first, then his love,
1362Nor is so hard of love the art.
1363By him alone that loves wel, love is sought,
1364And like a Merchandise by love is bought;
1365And seeking love we often find
1366A Glory, which we leave behind.
