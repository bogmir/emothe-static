Torquato Tasso

Aminta. The famous Pastoral

Act I

PROLOGUE

CUPID IN PASTORAL HABIT

1How could it enter into mortall breast:
2That under humane shape, and past’ral vest,
3Lay hid a god nay more, no god o’th woods,
4Or one of the inferiour rank of gods,
5A god Celestial: who doth instly claim,
6Among the greatest the most powerful name:
7Who often makes victorious Mars to lay
8His conquering sword, and bloody spear away.
9Great Neptune who so fils the world with wonder,
10Teilds him his trident; Jove himself his thunder.
11Sure with this aspect, in this habit clad,
12Venus can ne’re discern her smooth-fac’d lad,
13Or know him thus transform’d. I’m forc’d to fly,
14And hide my self from her, because that I
15Scorn for to be her subject, or my darts
16Should be her slaves: and only wound those hearts
17There’re levell’d at by her, and what’s that vain,
18Ambitious woman dare my shafts refrain?
19At Kings and Princes Courts she only will
20That ’mongst great Ladies I employ my skill,
21And to my lower Ministers conceads
22To practice theirs amongst the woods and Meads,
23In runstick breasts. I though you may me deem
24A boy, am none, and will, as best shall seem
25To me, dispose my shafts, since mine of old
26Are both th'omnipotent torch, and bow of gold:
27Wherefore (to hide my self and to fly from
28Her force, not, but her prayrs strong when they come
29From an importunate mother) in these plains,
30I refuge seek amongst the rurall swains,
31And my self shelter in these woods, but she
32Ceases nor day nor night to follow me,
33Promising those would tell her where I were
34Or kisses sweet, or what’s than them more dear:
35As if that she my giving too could bar
36Of kisses sweet, or what’s more pleasant far,
37To those that hide me from her, this at least;
38I know my kisses would be counted best:
39(If I that love am of love understand)
40So that sheoft seeks for me vainly, and
41Looses her pains: Since none so foolish are,
42As to disclose or tell her where I were.
43But least by counter-signs she should me know
44wings, my dart, my quiver, and my bow
45I have laid by, yet come not I disarm’d,
46Or weaponlesse, for see how I have charm’d
47My torch into this rod; which has the same
48Force it had first, and burns though with a flame
49Invisible, this likewise is my old
50Dart that I us’d, which though its head of gold
51Be chang’d, yet this of divine temper’d steel
52Who er’e it pierces makes loves flames to feel.
53This day I’l make the triall; and my dart
54Shall shew its divine power o’re the heart-
55Lesse bosome of the cruel’st nymph before
56This time that ever followed Dians Chore,
57And chastest Silvia’s plague shall be the same,
58(For that’s of this hard-hearted Nymph the name,)
59As that of fam’d Aminta, who did feel
60Long since, the pow’r of my Divinest steel;
61When he and she both tender us’d to sport,
62In the chast pleasures of bright Cynthia’s Court .
63But canse the wound shall be more inward, I
64Will stay untill some pitie mollifie
65That so hard frost which round about her heart
66Her virgin-pride hath fix’d, then I’l my dart
67Let fly with it’s full force; and cause I’l please
68My self in doing it, and do’t with ease,
69I’l mix my self among the shepheards crev
70Which passed by just now, who alwaies do
71On holydayes make sport within this plain,
72And to be one of them my self I’l fain;
73Them with the best occasion give the blow,
74So flyly, as no mortall eye shal’t know.
75In a strange manner shall these woods, to day,
76Be heard of love to reason, that it may,
77Clearly (my sacred Deity is here)
78(And not my under Ministers) appear.
79These rustick Silvane brests I will endue
80With divine sences, teach them how to wooe
81In lofty language, that it may be known
82That love where e’re he is, it alwaies one,
83Whether he pleases for to make his nest
84Or in a Swains, or in a Hero’s breast.
85Then if my beauteous mother, who ha’s tride
86Her utmost art to know where I abide
87By these feats know not, she’s more blind then I
88For blind by wrong, men call me Deity.

Scen. I.

Daphne and Silvia

DAPHNE

89Art thou resolv’d then Silvia to consume
90Thy fairest youth? can’t thy heart find a room
91For Venus pleasures? wilt not know those joy’s
92A Mother takes to sport with her sweet boyes?
93Shall so much beauty be to woods confin’d?
94Change, fondling that thou art, change, change thy mind.

SILVIA

95Let others follow loves delights for me,
96If that in love any delight there be.
97This life me pleases best; Nor do I know
98Ought I take pleasure in, but in my bow;
99This is my chiefest comfort, for to follow
100The wild beasts in the chase, hear huntsmen hollow:
101Nor do I fear such sports will wanting be.

DAPHNE

102More sottish Sports, more sottish life, dost see?
103This life thee pleases cause thou hast not prov’d
104As yet another, cause thou hast not lov’d.
105“So in the infant world, and when as yet,
106“The simple people knew no better meat,
107“Acorns and water, were accounted sweet:
108“But when th’earth did abound with corn and wine,
109“Acorns and water then were food for swine.
110So shouldst thou taste but one poor thousand part
111Of the sweet joys of love, thou’dst say, dear heart,
112Why didst thou fix my pleasure on such toyes
113And leave me widow to more reall joyes?
114Thou’lt sorrowfull repent the lost time past
115And curse it now for running on so fast.
116Howmany a widow night, how many a day
117I’ve ignorantly spent! thou’lt sighing say.
118Change then, fond fool, change change thy mind I pray,
119Lest when’ts too late thou do repent in vain.

SILVIA

120Daphne, when I repent or o’re again
121Repeat these words of thine, wch thou dost feign
122Just as thou list, then let the rivers turn
123Back to their fountains; let the Ocean burn,
124The wolves fly from the tender lamb, the hares
125Pursue the greyhounds; then let the fierce bears,
126Inhabit waters: and let Dolphins range
127The fields, whenever I my mind do change.

DAPHNE

128This is meer childishness, Sister, as thou
129Art, I was once. (Alas!) although that now
130I’me chang’d by age, even just so neat, so fair
131My count’nance was, so golden was my hair,
132Just such a cherry lip, just such a Rose,
133I did in th’middle of my cheek disclose;
134‘Twas my delight, then, Silvia, (now I find
135A foolish one it was) to hunt the Hind,
136Follow the footsteps of wild beasts, my net
137Spread to untrap the birds, take care to whet
138My dart; and if I did but chance to spy
139Some youthfull Shepheard cast an am’rous eye,
140I looked down with scorn, and with disdain,
141Counting what was my grace to be my pain,
142And nought was more displeasing unto me
143Then see me pleasing unto others be:
144But yet at length, time and th’importunate
145Pray’rs of my faithful lover, did abate
146That Virgin frost; Sister, I was (and blesse
147The hour) o’recome at last, I do confesse,
148And in one night’s black shade I did learn more
149Then I had done in all my life before.
150So now I did my former folly know,
151I quite renounc’d both Cynthia’s life and bow:
152And so I hope thou wilt too, and at last
153Aminta’s sighs may melt thy flinty breast.
154How canst thou choose but love him? canst deny
155He’s handsom? then pray tell the reason, why?
156Thinkst thou he loves thee not? or does love any
157Better than thee, though he be lov’d by many?
158If so, why do’s he so thy love pursue?
159Think’st him not enough noble? if thou do
160Though from his Rivers god the third thou be,
161Third from great Pan the shepherds god is he.
162Why then despi’st thou him? thinkst thou the lilies
163Of the fair cheeks of fairest Amarillis,
164Are not as beautiful as thine? yet he
165Thou seest despiseth her, and follows thee;
166She follows him and seeks his love, and thou,
167Though he seeks thine, refusest his; pray now
168Imagine, he, thus croft by thy disdain
169(God grant th’imagination be but vain)
170Should leave thee and take her, I fain would know
171Whether it would not grieve thee or no?
172’Tis better to accept him then, by half,
173Then taking her both at thy folly laugh.

SILVIA

174Let ’minta and his loves disposed be,
175As best him pleaseth little’t imports me:
176Nor who, his love has, so I do it misse,
177Care I, mine he can’t be, since I’me not his:
178And though he mine were, his I would not be.

DAPHNE

179From whence proceeds this hatred now from thee?
180If I were thee I’d strive to love him rather.

SILVIA

181It’s from his love,

DAPHNE

It’s from his love, Strange child of such a Father,
182When was fierce tyger born of gentle lamb?
183Or when from Swan’s egge a black crow e’re came?
184Sister thou cheatest or thy self or me.

SILVIA

185I hate his love, cause he my honesty:
186And I should love him while he would not crave
187Ought else of me but what my self would have,

DAPHNE

188Thou onely wil’st thy heart, he only would
189With that to thee which with himself he should:

SILVIA

190Daphne, or talk of somewhat else, or hold
191Thy peace I pray.

DAPHNE

Thy peace I pray. O gods! do but behold
192This perverse wench! but prethee Sister do,
193But answer this, If that another sue
194Should for thy love, would’st thus requite his pain?

SILVIA

195Who would entrap my chastity, the same
196Requite should find from me, what e’re he be,
197Whom thou stil’st lover, I am enemy.

DAPHNE

198The Ram unto the ewe dost thou esteem
199Or to the tender heifer the bull deem,
200En’mies to be, or doth the Turtle dove
201Reject her mate, because he doth her love:
202Seest thou the fragrant season of the year,
203How every thing doth sweet and green appear?
204This pleasant verdure covering o’re the plains,
205Invites, alas, not only nymphs and Swains,
206But very beasts to love, and dost not see,
207How all things in the world inamoured be?
208See how those two doves whisper, with what willing,
209And joynt consent as ʼtwere they two are billing!
210You Nightingale which hops from grove to grove
211Still as she hops, she sings, I love, I love;
212The cruell Adder who doth stop his ears,
213And having stung, will not be charm’e by tears,
214Or cries, is charm’d by sweetest love: ‘ith woods
215The tigers love, the fishes in the floods
216Love too, but thou more cruel then a beast
217Denyest sweet love an entrance in thy breast;
218But what talk I of beasts; seest thou each tree
219In this vast forrest? they inamoured be.
220Behold with what a sweet embrace the vine
221Does her dear confort lovingly intwine,
222The firr doth love the firr, the pine the pine;
223You stubborn oak, which scarce’the wind can move,
224Is mov’d by th’power of divinest love,
225Hadst thou a spir’t of love, or if of stone,
226Were not thy heart, thou’dst hear it sigh and groan,
227And utter forth it’s am’rous plaints: yet thou
228For all this art not mov’d to love, why now
229Wilt thou than plants or beasts be more unkind?
230Change, fondling that thou art, change, change thy mind.

SILVIA

231When I of plants the am’rous sighs shal hear
232I’l likewise be in love, til then forbear.

DAPHNE

233Well though thou now laugh at, and dost disdain
234My faithful counsels; know that to thy pain
235Thou wilt repent, thou dost not while thou may
236Them follow; for be sure there comes a day,
237When what’s to thee a pleasure and delight,
238Shall be thy greatest grief, thy greatest spight;
239And as thou now disdainest others, so,
240Thou’lt then despise thy very self; and know
241Those so clear springs in which thou oft dost use
242Thy most resplendent beauty to peruse,
243Thou’lt leave forsaken and neglected now,
244Or stand amazed at thy wrinkled brow.
245But why speak I of this? Since ʼtis a sure
246And common evil we must all endure.
247I’ll tell thee more, and mark me what I say,
248ʼTis what the sage Elpino t’other day
249Recounted unto Licoris the fair
250The gentile Licoris: whose beauty rare,
251Has such pow’r o’re Elpino, as his art
252In singing ought t’have o’re her pliant heart;
253If there be any debt in love. He told
254It before am’rous Thir’s and Battus old,
255Just at the entrance of Aurora’s cave,
256Where writ in golden Characters you have;
257All you that the profane depart from hence,
258And said that it was told to him long since
259By the grand Poet, who so high did sing
260The life of many a Heroe, many a King,
261Who dying did leave him his pipe, There is
262Sayes he, at bottom o’th profound abysse,
263A horrid cave, vomiting smoak with fire,
264And a most constant stink out of the dire
265Fornace of Acheronta, where do lie,
266In torments dark, and that eternally
267Those women; who perswaded here above,
268Neither by pray’rs, nor tears, could be to love:
269And ʼtis a just, and well deserved law,
270Smoak should force tears, which pity could not draw:
271Then if thou thusthy cruelty continue,
272Look to be confort with that curst retinue.

SILVIA

273But what said Licoris to this, and how
274Answer’d she him;

DAPHNE

Answer’d she him; Why fee! thou fain wouldst know
275Others affairs, but car’st not for thine own:
276She answered with her eyes.

SILVIA

She answered with her eyes. With eys alone
277How could she answer him?

DAPHNE

How could she answer him? Those very ey’s
278Were, as ʼtwere, messengers, or rather spies;
279Which mix’d with pleasant smiles to him made known,
280Licoris and her heart were now his own:
281Except he did believe, faith was as rare
282In them as beauty; she as false as fair.

SILVIA

283Why should he so believe?

DAPHNE

Why should he so believe? knowst thou what Thir’s,
284That famous Master both of Love and Verse,
285Did write, when burning with the flames of love
286He wandring through the forrest, did both move
287The nymphs & swains to laughter? but though he
288Did things worth laughter, yet his writings be
289Not to be jeer’d; this in a tree he writ,
290With th’tree it grew, and there I oft read it:
291The looking-glasses of false hearts your eyes
292Are; and in them all deceit hidden lies,
293But what avails’t since shun them love denies:

SILVIA

294I here in pratling cast my time away,
295And had forgot this is th’appointed day,
296In which we ought to go, as we were wont,
297In Eliceto for to raise the Hunt.
298Prethee do thou stay for me while i’th nigh
299Fountain I cleanse the sweat, and dust which I,
300Got yesterday by hunting of a Deer,
301Which at the last I kil’d;

DAPHNE

Which at the last I kil’d; I’l stay thee here
302Perhaps I’l bath me too, but first my gate,
303I’l homewards bend for it’s not yet so late
304As’t seems, and thou at home for me mayst stay:
305But in the mean time think of that, I pray,
306Concerns thee more then hunting, and say I
307Bid thee take a fools counsel, so god bu’y.

Scen. 2.

Aminta and Thirsis

AMINTA

308I have with my laments, my sighs, my groans
309To pity moved, both the rocks and stones;
310But mov’d I have not nor can hope to move
311Her I adore, to pity or to love:
312Remorselest Nymph, I know not which I best
313May stile thee, or a woman; or fierce beast
314For thou to be a woman dost deny,
315Since more than best’al is thy cruelty.

THIRSIS

316The lambs on tender grasse, wolves on lambs feed
317And both do fatishe their hungry need:
318But though lovefeed on tears, the more it have
319Of tears and sighs, it still the more doth crave;

AMINTA

320Alas! alas! love long since with the food
321Of my tears satiate is, now for my blood
322It onely thirst, and therefore to the eyes
323Of it and that cru’l nymph I’l mak’t a prize:

THIRSIS

324Why talkst thou thus Aminta? if unkind
325And cruel she be, thou’lt another find.

AMINTA

326How can I find another, when that I
327Can’t find my very self, or pray whereby
328Can one lost to himself e’re pleasure gain?

THIRSIS

329Do not despair, at length thou mayst obtain
330This cruel fair; time teaches men to check,
331Lions, and bring fierce tygers to their beck:

AMINTA

332But whilst that I wait out this long delay,
333I shall with grief be quite consum’d away:

THIRSIS

334Short the delay will be, for the disdain
335Of woman’s kindled soon, soon quench’t again:
336They’re moveable by nature, nor the wind,
337Can breath more changes then a womans mind:
338Lighter than feathers, and will readier bow,
339Then ripened ears before a storm; but thou,
340Of thy condition farther let me know,
341And the hard fortune of thy love, for though
342Thou long since toldst me that thou wast in love,
343Yet thou hidst from me, who it was could move
344In thee this am’rous fire, although that faith,
345And ever constant friendship ‘twixt us hath
346Oblig’d thee to discover that to me,
347Which unto others might concealed be.

AMINTA

348Thirsis, I am content to tell thee what
349The woods and mountains know, but men know not,
350And ʼtis but just that I being now so nigh,
351My death, should leave the reason why I die
352To some dear friend, who may when I am gone
353Report it, or may grav’t in tree or stone,
354Near the place where my bloodlesse corps shall lie,
355That if that pit’lesse Nymph chance to pass by,
356She may stamp on’t with her proud foot, and boast,
357That her dire cruelty my life me cost;
358And that she may rejoice to see me lie,
359There as a trophee of her victory.
360Perhaps at last she may (but things above
361Desert I hope) both pity then and love
362Him dead, who living by her hate was slain,
363And with salt tears may wish me back again;
364But listen now.

THIRSIS

But listen now. Proceed; I wel thee mind,
365And am to help thee at thy need inclin’d.

AMINTA

366I was as yet a child and scarce could crop
367Those fruits the willing trees did seem to drop,
368From their full loaden branches near the earth,
369When full of jollity and harmless mirth,
370Not knowing love, or caring it to know,
371With th’sweetest nymph I did familiar grow,
372That in the wind e’re spread a golden hair,
373It was the rich Cidippe’s daughter fair;
374Grandchild unto the great Montan, whose flocks
375And mighty heards fill both the woods and rocks,
376Silvia, the glory of the woods, the fire
377Of every heart, of all fouls the desire:
378With her I kept such faithful company,
379That ʼtwixt two Turtles ne’re the like could be;
380Nothing to me than Silvia was dearer,
381Near were our houses, but our hearts were nearer:
382Her age was equal almost with my own,
383But ʼtwixt our thoughts, there was no diff’rence known;
384With her I often us’d to spread the snares,
385To catch the simple birds, or fearful hares:
386With her I us’d to force swift Does to flight,
387Equal the prey was, equal the delight;
388But whilst we thus made prize of beasts: a prize
389My self was made to her’ll-conqu’ring eyes;
390And like a weed which of it self doth grow,
391So grew there in my bosom, from I know
392Not yet what root, a strange and unknown fire,
393Which made me Silvia’s presence more desire:
394Her eyes were food to me, sweet, but I did leave
395A bitter; did me of all jokes bereave:
396And though my sighs, alas, now were not few,
397Yet of those many sighs no cause I knew.
398Thus was I, e’re that I could it discover,
399By her fair ey’s forc’d to become a lover:
400But how I came to know’t at last, do thou
401Take notice pray.

THIRSIS

Take notice pray. Proceed and tell me how.

AMINTA

402When Phebus bright his hottest beams displai’d,
403Silvi’ and Phillis once chanc’t seek a shade
404Under a spreading beech, when, lo, a Bee
405Buzzing about Phillis fair cheeks chanc’d see
406The lovely red she did in them disclose,
407Did make the Bee mistake them for a Rose,
408And think to gather honey from that sweet,
409She lights: but with too harsh salute did greet
410Those rosean cheeks, and Phillis with the pain,
411Of the sharp sting most sadly did complain;
412When my Dear Silvia told her: Phillis pray
413Lament not thus, I soon will take away
414Thy pain with an inchantment, which I learn’d
415Of the sage Aresia, and I earn’d
416It well, for in requital of it I
417Gave unto her my horn of Ivory,
418Which was adorned with gold. Thus having said
419Her sweeter lips to Phillis cheek she laid:
420Just where the Bee had stung her, and she there
421Muttered some words which well I could hear:
422But O most wonderful! Phillis had ease
423Immediately, and all her pain did cease.
424Whether it was her Magick art or no,
425That so soon wrought the cure I don’t well know,
426But I believe in her sweet mouth was such
427Vertue that it did heal all it did touch.
428I, that before had to restrain’d the fire
429Of love, that nothing else I did desire,
430But Silvia’s presence, and a paradice
431It was to feast my self on her fair ey’s,
432Or hear the sweet tunes of her warbling voice,
433More pleasing far to me than was the noise,
434The little pibbles make in murm’ring floods,
435Or than the wind when’t signs among the woods
436Was now by an ambitious spirit inclin’d,
437To a desire which wish’d my lips were join’d
438To hers. This amorous desire me taught,
439A very subtile craft which at length brought
440Me wish’d successe (do but observe how love
441Makes mens ingenio’s nimbly for to move)
442Her wondrous cure on Phillis I did see,
443The sight of that caught me to feign a Bee
444Had bit my under lip, and though her pray
445I durst not for to take my pain away
446By her inchantment, yet did my lament
447Expresse as ‘twere to her my hearts intent;
448She harmlesse soul pittying the grief that I
449Made shew of, proffered freely to apply
450Her cure to my feign’d wound, which added fuel,
451To my hearts wound, and made it far more cruel.
452Oh Thirsis! never Bee did suck from Rose,
453Honey so sweet as I did suck from those
454Sweet cherries of her lips, though every kisse,
455Me thought did yet want some part of its blisse,
456For though I had a ful desire, yet I
457Was or restrain’d by fear, or modesty:
458But whilst this honey mixt with gall descended
459Thus to my heart, I sorry so soon ended
460Should be my blisse, feign’d that her charms did ease
461Me somewhat, but not quite the pain surcease;
462Which made her willingly to take the trouble,
463That to me sweet inchantment to redouble;
464Thus so increas’d this love within my breast,
465That at the last’twas forced to leave its nest:
466Nor would it be kept secret, so one day,
467When we were set as we were wont to play;
468Shepherds and Nymphs together on the grasse,
469Each lad some secrets whispering to his lasse:
470I Silvia told, Silvia, I burn for thee,
471And sure shal die unlesse thou helpest me;
472Strait at my words there in her face arose
473A blush both shame and anger did disclose:
474Bow’d to the ground her fair face was, and she
475With nought but an harsh silence answer’d me,
476And from me turn’d, nor ever would she deign,
477Since that to see or hear me speak again,
478Though ful three years are past and I have tri’d
479All means I could to get her pacifi’d,
480Except my deaths; and could but that appease
481Her anger, I methinks could die with ease,
482And should account it highly worth my pain,
483If I by death her pity could obtain,
484And for my faith would deem it recompence,
485Should she lament me when I’m gone from hence;
486But why should I with grief unto that breast,
487In which I fixed have my joy, my rest?

THIRSIS

488But is it possible if she should hear
489Thee say thus much? She longer could forbear,
490This true love to requite?

AMINTA

This true love to requite? That know not I,
491But when I’d speak she more my words doth fly,
492Than doth an aspe the charmer.

THIRSIS

Than doth an aspe the charmer. Trust to me,
493Aminta, and I will procure that she
494Shal hearken to thee.

AMINTA

Shal hearken to thee. Nothing from her can you,
495Thirsis procure, or if procure you do
496That I speak to her, yet alas in vain
497Will be my speech, and I shall nothing gain:

THIRSIS

498Why dost despair so?

AMINTA

Why dost despair so? Just occasion I
499Have to despair, nay rather for to die;
500For the wife Mopsu’s long since did foreshow
501This my hard fortune, Mopsus, who doth know
502The vertue of all herbs, and talk of birds.

THIRSIS

503Which Mopfus speakst thou of? of him whose words
504Are honi’d, and who has a friendly smile
505In’s face, but in his heart has nought but guile:
506Be of good cheer, Aminta, do not fear,
507Those uncouth prophesies he sels so dear
508To unadvised persons, I reject
509As foolish, for they never have effect;
510This know I by experience, and I,
511Cause he hath thus foretold, do hope thereby
512That in thy love all things wil succed wel,

AMINTA

513If by experience thou canst ought me tell
514Which comfort may my hope, pray don’t it hide,

THIRSIS

515When I came hither first for to abide
516Within these woods, I knew and did esteem,
517This Mopsus such as thou dost now him deem;
518I had by chance occasion for to go
519To the great Citie; which I let him know
520As hoping his advice, when thus to me
521He spake, To the great land thou go’st, said he,
522Where the fly Citizens and those o’th Court
523Of simple rusticks make a scoffing sport;
524Therefore be sure this counsel take of mine,
525Come not near those whom thou shalt see to shine
526With gold and stones, and other vain devices,
