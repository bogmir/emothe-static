Félix Lope de Vega y Carpio

Punishment Without Revenge

ACT I

The Duke of Ferrara, at night; Febo and Ricardo, servants

RICARDO

1That was some mimicry!Endecasílabos sueltos

FEBO

That was some mimicry! Indeed it was,
2But who’d believe ‘twas the Duke of Ferrara?

DUKE

3I am afraid I will be recognised.

RICARDO

4Because it is disguise, all is permitted;
5Even the heavens can conceal themselves
6By sometimes resorting to camouflage.
7What do you think’s the veil that night conceals?
8An ornamented cape that hides the heavens,
9And, so as to shed some light, the stars it holds
10Are silver ornaments; the moon, a cross.

DUKE

11How can you say such foolery already?

FEBO

12The poet member of the latest sect
13Who deem themselves divine has not so thought.

RICARDO

14If I employ their licence, don’t blame me,
15I know of one who called the moon a cheese.

DUKE

16Well, do not think it wrong that poetry
17Has reached a state so very miserable
18That it has become a conjuror now
19–Much sleight of hand and little cognizance–
20Who can spit out many-coloured gay ribbons.
21But let us set aside to other ends
22These jaded themes: that dame is no eyesore.

RICARDO

23Eyesore, you say? She is in truth a seraph.
24But there’s an obstacle to overcome.

DUKE

25What obstacle is that?

RICARDO

What obstacle is that? A certain spouse
26Who accepts favours but will offer none.

FEBO

27He fancies to safeguard appearances.

DUKE

28Such has e’er been the cruellest pedigree
29Of those who dwell in this community.

FEBO

30May he who accepts garments and bijous
31Have pity on the one who purchased them,
32For, should his wife’s demise come before his,
33Half will be his according to the law.

RICARDO

34It’s true that such people lack charity,
35Speak devilish tongues and garble words at will.

DUKE

36These cunning men are cousins of the devil,
37Who makes them give consent but stalls their acts.

RICARDO

38Here I could knock but there’s a lot to say.

DUKE

39How’s that?

RICARDO

How’s that? A devout bawd with two young girls
40‘Twixt tender fruit and vines, one pearl, one plate.

DUKE

41I never trust their outward appearance.

RICARDO

42Nearby there dwells a lady sweet as pie
43Fair and dark-skinned.

DUKE

Fair and dark-skinned. But what about her spirit?

RICARDO

44The one who picks her clothes and lives with her
45Does ruminate, downhearted, visitors.

FEBO

46‘Twas always oxen that would chew the cud.

RICARDO

47Nearby I’ve seen a woman whose opinion
48Would make one think she held a law degree.

DUKE

49Let’s go.

RICARDO

Let’s go. She will not open at this hour.

DUKE

50Won’t she? And if I tell her who I am?

RICARDO

51In that case, she’ll be sure to open up.

DUKE

52Knock on her door without any more fuss!

RICARDO

53She was expecting someone, for out she sped.

Cintia, from above

CINTIA

54Who goes there at this hour?

RICARDO

Who goes there at this hour? It is but I.

CINTIA

55Who, may I ask, is “I”?

RICARDO

Who, may I ask, is “I”? A friend, Cintia.
56Pray open up, the Duke is here with me,
57Attracted by the praise I sang of you.

CINTIA

58The Duke, you say?

RICARDO

The Duke, you say? You doubt my word?

CINTIA

The Duke, you say? You doubt my word? I don’t
59Doubt that the duke may have come here with you,
60But the fact that such an important lord
61Should come to visit me at this late hour.

RICARDO

62He comes disguised to see a great lady.

CINTIA

63Ricardo, if but last month you had said
64What now you say to me about the Duke
65You’d have persuaded me he’s at my door.
66For all his youth he’s lived unworthily,
67And all his immoral licentiousness
68Is known to every common man and girl.
69And since he has not married, to pursue
70Still this lecherous way of life of his,
71Not minding whether it is a disgrace
72To have a bastard heir –though Federico
73Is quite a worthy youth– well, I’d believe
74The Duke had come to visit me himself.
75But now he’s acting as a gentleman
76And has left his wanton ways to be betrothed,
77And sent his son to Mantua for Casandra,
78His wife, he cannot still be running wild
79While waiting for her to come at any time.
80And if t’were wantonness in Federico
81What would it be then in the Duke’s own case?
82And were you faithful, though he gave you grounds,
83You would not dare to slur his reputation,
84For now the Duke, your lord, is fast asleep;
85And so I close my window, ‘cause I know
86‘Twas an excuse to engage in conversation.
87Adieu, farewell, and come again tomorrow.

Exit

DUKE

88To what house of repute you have brought me!

RICARDO

89I, sir? I’m not to blame.

DUKE

I, sir? I’m not to blame. ‘Twas a mistake
90To leave her unrebuked until tomorrow.

FEBO

91I’ll knock the door down should you so desire.

DUKE

92I heard you say it!

FEBO

I heard you say it! It’s Ricardo’s fault.
93But if you want to know the current state
94Of those who are in power, my lord, whether
95Feared or beloved, forget the smooth-tongued words
96Of your obsequious man, and clothed by night
97In simple garments or in a charabanc,
98Go forth to know their worth. This stratagem
99Was known to work for many an emperor.

DUKE

100Listeners don’t hear any good of themselves,
101And they were, although you speak up for them,
102Overparticular philosophers,
103Because the common people are not fit
104To judge the truth, and it is an error
105Of ignorant minds to trust the good opinion
106Of the fickle and the unreliable,
107Whose premises defeat all laws of reason.
108A whiner or a discontent will lie
109To commoners eager for novelties,
110Whose lowliness will oft impede the news
111From reaching them, and they have no access
112To palaces and so can hear no rumours.
113I say I have lived wanton and unwed,
114Preferring not to be restrained, and thinking
115Federico, although a bastard, is my heir;
116But since I now await Casandra’s coming,
117Who hies from Mantua in company of my son,
118I shall forget all else for the time being.

FEBO

119‘Twill do you well to marry.

RICARDO

‘Twill do you well to marry. If you want
120Some entertainment, listen at this door.

DUKE

121Are they singing?

RICARDO

Are they singing? Can you not tell?

DUKE

Are they singing? Can you not tell? But who
122Is it lives here?

RICARDO

Is it lives here? It is a theatrical manager.

FEBO

123And he’s the best in Italy, what’s more.

DUKE

124They do sing well!
They do sing well! And does he perform good plays?

RICARDO

125It all depends on whether you’re foe or friend:
126His friends exalt them with their loud applause
127His enemies have no good word to say.

FEBO

128They cannot all be good.

DUKE

They cannot all be good. For my wedding,
129Febo, prepare the best rooms and comedies;
130For I would not like you to commission
131Anything but the very best of plays.

FEBO

132We shall put on those praised by wits and lords.

DUKE

133Are they rehearsing?

RICARDO

Are they rehearsing? A lady is speaking.

DUKE

134If it is Andrelina, she’s well known.
135What acting! What passion! And what feeling!

From inside the house

136Away, my thoughts! No more, no more memory
137That my past glory turns into torment,
138And I don’t want to recall this distress,
139I want not memory, I seek oblivion,
140For these are sad reflections of lost joys
141Although you claim they mitigate my pain.

DUKE

142Nice acting!

FEBO

Nice acting! Yes, it’s very nice indeed!

DUKE

143I’d listen to some more but I am tired.
144I’m off to bed.

RICARDO

I’m off to bed. So early? It’s just ten.

DUKE

145I find it dull.

RICARDO

I find it dull. But she is quite unique!

DUKE

146I am afraid she’ll have something to say.

RICARDO

147Something to do with you? How could that be?

DUKE

148You well know, Ricardo, that comedy’s
149A mirror where the foolish and the wise,
150The young, the strong, the brave and monarchs all,
151And governors, maidens, married women too
152Are used as an example of life and name,
153To show our customs, be they loose or pure,
154By mixing fiction with reality,
155Delightfulness with disillusionment.
156May it suffice to say that once I heard
157One prima donna speak quite ill of me.
158And now you would persuade me to stay on
159And hear what this one has to say of me.
160You should remember that to hear such truths
161Is not what every gentleman would choose.

Exeunt

Federico, dressed in his walking clothes, and Batín, a servant

BATÍN

162I don’t know what it is you wish to do.
163Why do you stop among these trees, Federico,
164When you have such important things at hand?

FEDERICO

165Because I feel so very out of sorts,
166I am unable, although I would wish,
167To proceed fast and more attentively.
168Instead I leave my people in this place
169And, prey to many thoughts, I stand right here
170Under the shelter of these trees, whose crowns,
171Adorning them with apparel of green,
172Watch o’er the sleeping waves of this river,
173So musical and cold and crystal clear.
174I wish I could escape from being myself
175For I am tired of hearing all about
176My father’s marriage, when I was quite sure
177That I would be his rightful heir one day.
178And though I put a brave face on the act,
179As is expected, it’s with heavy heart
180That I am off to Mantua against my will
181To bring back my stepmother as I’m bid.

BATÍN

182Despite your father’s vicious way of life,
183So criticised by all and sundry here,
184He did fall victim of a virtuous maid
185And, to assuage his need, must marry her.
186A vassal, as a gift to the French king,
187Presented him with an untamed wild horse
188Endowed with extraordinary grace.
189Swan was its name, in honour of its coat
190Of snowy white and of its curly mane,
191That from the neck that held its dainty head
192Fell to the ground. Its nature was so wrought
193As though its model was a lovely girl,
194But so disdainful in its violence
195That no horse tamer e’er could harness it.
196On seeing such beauty and such wild fury,
197The king had placed a lion in a den
198And there he put the horse, whose vital spirit
199Was so intimidated that its mane stood up
200Around its head, like a white sphere of spears
201Or a porcupine, whose every spine oozed ice,
202And the proud steed became so tame and calm
203That it allowed a tiny dwarf to put
204A saddle on its back, and from that day
205It never caused a worry to its grooms.

FEDERICO

206Batín, I know the best solution for
207My shameless father would be a marriage,
208But I cannot help feeling very sad
209For having harboured these false hopes so long.
210I know the haughty and the most phlegmatic
211Can fall under the spell of a lady,
212And that quite soon this lion proud and brave
213Will hold within his arms the first-born babe
214That with its tenderness and babbling words
215Shall let it take him by his manly beard.
216Not even a rough and ready farmer looks
217Upon his crops the way a married man
218Will look upon his breed, and thus renounce
219His every act of immorality.
220But what is it to me if my father
221Should find a quiet life and leave behind
222His life of vice, if his offspring inherit
223His worldly goods, while I, a lowly serf,
224Must carry in a lion that will destroy me?

BATÍN

225My lord, all sensible and discreet men,
226When subject to evils they can’t avoid,
227Will clothe themselves in patience and good will,
228Put on a happy face and do their best
229To show no sign of envy or revenge.

FEDERICO

230And shall I have to bear a stepmother?

BATÍN

231No more than you have consented till now
232All of the Duke’s former conquests. And now
233You only will have one: a great lady.

FEDERICO

234What is that din of voices that I hear?

BATÍN

235There are some people at the river ford.

FEDERICO

236They’re women. I shall go and see.

BATÍN

They’re women. I shall go and see. Stop there!

FEDERICO

237Don’t be a coward, it’s the right thing to do.

Exit

BATÍN

238It’s brave indeed to face predicaments.
239Come Lucindo, Albano and Floro!

Exeunt

LUCINDO

240The count is calling.

ALBANO

The count is calling. Where is Federico?

FLORO

241I wonder if he’s asking for his horses.

BATÍN

242The cries of a lady took him away,
243Dim-witted and quite firm of foot indeed
244While I follow him close upon his footsteps,
245Call the others.

Exit

LUCINDO

Call the others. Where are you going? Wait!

ALBANO

246I think it is a sham.

FLORO

I think it is a sham. And so do I,
247Although I certainly can hear the voices
248Of people walking down by the river.

LUCINDO

249Federico seems ill disposed to obey
250His new mistress, although he’s going for her.

ALBANO

251It’s clear for all to see how sad he feels.

Federico enters with Casandra in his arms

FEDERICO

252I trust you’ll give me leave to carry you here.

CASANDRA

253I thank you for such courtesy, kind sir.

FEDERICO

254And I thank my luck for bringing me here
255To this recondite and secluded copse.

CASANDRA

256And who, my lord, I pray, are these good folk?

FEDERICO

257They are the servants who accompany me.
258Fear not, fair lady, for they’ll serve you too.

Batín enters with the maid Lucrecia in his arms

BATÍN

259Tell me, good woman, how you weigh so much
260When everybody says you are so light?

LUCRECIA

261Kind sir, please tell me where you’re taking me.

BATÍN

262At least I’ll get you out of all this mud
263The river concentrates upon its banks.
264I think it played a trick to overturn
265Your coach as it passed by, so as to have
266Such gorgeous nymphs within its view.
267And should it not have been so near dry land
268You might have found yourselves in grave danger.

FEDERICO

269Milady, so that I may address you
270With all of the respect that is your due
271Please tell me who you are.

CASANDRA

Please tell me who you are. Indeed, my lord,
272There is no reason why I should not do so.
273I am Casandra, Duchess of Ferrara,
274The daughter of the Duke of Mantua.

FEDERICO

275How can your highness travel quite alone?

CASANDRA

276I’m not alone, for such could never be.
277Not far from here awaits Marquis Gonzaga,
278Whom I implored to leave me on the path
279And let me cross the river on my own
280Under this sweltering afternoon sunshine
281For I believed the shore to be protected
282By leafy trees, but found it was all wet.
283‘Twas not so wet that Fortune chose to drown me
284But wet enough to stop the carriage wheels.
285Pray tell me, lord, who you may be, although
286Your presence warrants courtesy and valour,
287Not I alone would thank you for this favour,
288But the Marquis and my father would also
289Be very grateful for you kind rescue.

FEDERICO

290Once you have given me your sweet hand to kiss,
291Your Highness will be told just who I am.

CASANDRA

292You kneel? It is too much! It is not fair
293That I should let you show such high regard.

FEDERICO

294Milady, it is fair and it is right,
295Because I have to tell you I’m your son.

CASANDRA

296In faith I’ve been quite foolish not to know you.
297Who else but you would save me from my plight?
298Embrace me then.

FEDERICO

Embrace me then. I kiss your hand instead.

CASANDRA

299It’s not enough.
It’s not enough. My arms should pay their debt,
300Federico, kind sir and son-in-law.

FEDERICO

301I thank you in my soul for your kindness.

They speak to each other in low voices and Batín speaks

BATÍN

302Since we have had such luck that this lady
303Is the person we were to seek in Mantua,
304All that I need to know is if I’ll call you
305Your grace, your excellence or milady
306So that I can adjust my words full well
307To the honour warranted by your merits.

LUCRECIA

308Since I was but a child I am, my friend,
309A servant to the Duchess, her lady’s maid.

BATÍN

310Are you a maid-in-waiting then?

LUCRECIA

Are you a maid-in-waiting then? I’m not.

BATÍN

311You’ll be a would-be maid-in waiting then,
312That was about to be but never made it.
313Sometimes the nobles have what you tell me:
314A kind of wild card, between maids and servants,
315Who are everything and yet nothing to them.
316What is your name?

LUCRECIA

What is your name? It is Lucrecia.

BATÍN

317From Rome?

LUCRECIA

From Rome? From somewhere much closer than that.

BATÍN

318I thank the Lord that I ran into you!
319Since I have heard your story, my head’s full
320Of forced restraint and unjust consequence.
321Did you see Tarquin?

LUCRECIA

Did you see Tarquin? I?

BATÍN

Did you see Tarquin? I? And if you saw him
322What would you do?

LUCRECIA

What would you do? Well, do you have a wife?

BATÍN

323Why do you ask?

LUCRECIA

Why do you ask? So I can seek her counsel.

BATÍN

324Your words offend me. Do you know who I am?

LUCRECIA

325And how could I? What are you renowned for?

BATÍN

326I can’t believe my fame has not reached Mantua.

LUCRECIA

327What are your merits? But you must be like
328Those fools who think they’re famous the world over
329Though hardly anybody knows their name.

BATÍN

330May God forbid that such should be my case
331Or that I envy virtues not my own.
332I said it out of verve and not because
333I wish to show off or from arrogance.
334The truth is that I wish I were well-known
335Among wise men of science and the arts;
336Because in common ignorance one’s fame
337Is just the consequence of foolish acts,
338For one reaps what one sows, I do declare.

CASANDRA

339You can’t believe how glad I am to meet you.
340I little heard to equal what I see:
341The speech, the manners form the character,
342My son and lord, in such a way that shows
343In these your deeds what a huge soul you have
344That lies within your breast and makes you great.
345What luck that I got lost on my way here,
346For it was thanks to that bad luck I met you.
347As in the angry sea after the tempest
348You see the beautiful St Elmo’s fire,
349So was my error night, the river sea,
350The carriage ship, I pilot, you my star.
351As from today your mother I shall be,
352My lord Count Federico, and I beg
353That you will honour me as such today.
354I am so happy with you, and my soul
355Salutes you as a very fond loved one
356And I’m content to have you for my son
357More than for being Duchess of Ferrara.

FEDERICO

358To see you, lovely lady, is enough
359To fill my heart with reverence for you;
360I trust your kindness will not hinder me
361From corresponding to your graciousness.
362Today the Duke, my lord, cuts me in two,
363For my first body has been born again
364So that my soul could occupy its place.
365For these two births, milady, you’re to blame,
366I would be born of you to gain my soul;
367Which, though a gift from God, before you came
368I knew not where it was; you’ve fashioned me
369Anew, who hereto lived without a soul.
370From this you can deduce, that as I wish
371To be the child you carry, I’ll be the son
372The Duke expects that you will give birth to.
373And it is no chimera that I wish
374To be born as a grown man, and my excuse
375Is that the eye of heaven that’s the sun
376Is six thousand years old, and born each day.

Marquis Gonzaga, Rutilio & servants

RUTILIO

377And this is where I left them, sir.

MARQUIS

And this is where I left them, sir. It would
378Have been a great misfortune if this man,
379This cavalier, had not come to her aid.

RUTILIO

380He sent me off for snow, for his intent
381Was to cool the bright water, and to bathe
382Her pure white feet in its precious pearl drops;
383And thus I was unable to rescue her
384So the Duchess emerged within his arms;
385But when I saw them safe on the river bank
386I ran to call you.

MARQUIS

I ran to call you. I see the carriage there
387Between the water and the silvery sand.

RUTILIO

388Those willow trees impeded seeing her.
389But there she is among the gentleman’s crew.

CASANDRA

390Here come my people!

MARQUIS

Here come my people! Milady!

CASANDRA

Here come my people! Milady! Marquis!

MARQUIS

391Your Highness had us all in quite a state
392Until this moment. Thank God you are safe.

CASANDRA

393For that you must thank this gentleman too:
394His kindly arms rescued me from the fall.

MARQUIS

395Dear Count, who else could be the one to favour
396Her who is about to be your mother?

FEDERICO

397Good Marquis, I would I were a Jupiter
398That I might become that imperial eagle,
399Although my feathers were scorched by such sun;
400Like Phaeton I would have sealed my fate
401With the Toison clutched in my golden claws,
402And in my arms I would have borne the treasure
403Into the arms of her enamoured Duke.

MARQUIS

404The heavens, Lord, dispose these deeds you see
405So that Casandra’ll be indebted to you
406For such courage; and from this moment on
407The affection ‘twixt the mother and the son
408Will be renowned all over Italy:
409For two such enemies can show their truce
410And even be like two peas in a pod.

The two go on speaking and Casandra and Lucrecia speak in an aside

CASANDRA

411While these two go on talking, tell me please,
412Lucrecia, what you think of Federico.

LUCRECIA

413Milady, if you give me your permission,
414I shall be glad to give you my opinion.

CASANDRA

415Although I fear what I’m about to hear,
416I give you my permission.

LUCRECIA

I give you my permission. Well I say…

CASANDRA

417So say it.

LUCRECIA

So say it. Your luck would be much greater
418If the tables were turned.

CASANDRA

If the tables were turned. You’re right, Lucrecia,
419And yet the die is cast; and even if
420With some pretext I should go back to Mantua,
421I’m sure my father would order me killed,
422And my folly would be news in all Italy
423Besides the fact I could not wed Federico.
424And so it is not right that I return
425To Mantua, but must go to Ferrara,
426Wherein I am awaited by the Duke,
427Whose wanton ways and lecherous customs
428Come to my ears in detail without fail.

MARQUIS

429Well! Round up our people and let’s be off
430Let’s leave this dangerous and barbaric spot.
431Go on ahead to Ferrara, Rutilio,
432And tell the Duke all about the event,
433In case the happy news does not precede us
434For mishaps travel faster than good news.
435Let’s go, milady. Prepare the Count’s horse!

FLORO

436May the grooms get the Count’s horse ready!

CASANDRA

437Your excellence should travel in my carriage.

FEDERICO

438Whatever Your Highness ordains I’ll do.

The Marquis leads Casandra out by the hand and Federico and Batín remain

BATÍN

439The Duchess is pretty!

FEDERICO

The Duchess is pretty! You think so, Batín?

BATÍN

440I think she is a lily asking dawn
441With the four candid tongues that are her petals
442To give her pearls of dew to slake her thirst.
443I’ve never seen so fair a gentlewoman.
444By God, my lord, if this were but the moment
445To tell you…
To tell you… But they’re mounting the carriage.

FEDERICO

446Say nothing, for your quick intelligence
447Has seen my soul’s desire and flatters me.

BATÍN

448Is not this fresh young bud, this orange blossom,
449This bunch of flowers, this amber sugar icing,
450This Venus, this Helen of Troy, the best for you?
451I curse all worldly laws that ban her from you!

FEDERICO

452Let’s go, don’t give them reason to suspect.
453I’m not the first stepson to fall in love
454With such a beautiful stepmother.

BATÍN

455But all you need is patience then, my lord,
456For after a stormy exchange or two
457You may well find her anything but fair.

Exeunt

Enter the Duke of Ferrara and Aurora, his niece

DUKE

458Federico will meet her on her way
459If he departed when they say he did.

AURORA

460He was quite wrong to go because he should
461Have waited to accompany Your Grace.

DUKE

462I fear some sadness may’ve delayed his parting
463For Federico was convinced he’d be
464My only heir; ‘twas natural he’d think so
465Because I really do love Federico
466And my consent to marry was betrayal.
467It was my vassals who persuaded me
468To treat him this inconsiderately,
469Although they said they liked him as their lord
470Was it because they knew the love I felt
471For him or they felt that same love themselves?
472They say those others with a right to claim
473To be my legal heirs will litigate
474Or might decide to take up arms against us
475And if they can’t be otherwise contented
476Will lay waste to these lands, for vassals pay
477The highest price for wars in every case.
478For that reason I gave them my compliance
479To wed. I felt unable to refuse.

AURORA

480My lord, you are forgiven; t’was Fortune’s way,
481But the sad melancholy of the Count
482Will be alleviated so his trust
483May be relieved by patience and endurance;
484Although I’ll give you one word of advice
485In this confusion, in part to assuage
486His disappointment and show him your love.
487Forgive my boldness, but I shall proceed
488To give you my opinion, for I know
489Your fondness for me will allow me to.
490I am, dear Duke, your niece, the daughter of
491Your brother, whom inexorable death
492Reaped at an early age, but twenty-five
493As almond blossom is oft crushed by wind.
494You reared me in your house, for very soon,
495My mother too I lost; and I had only you
496As my dear father, who did guide me through
497The terrible blind maze of my misfortune.
