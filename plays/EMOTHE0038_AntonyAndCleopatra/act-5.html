Scene 1

Enter Caesar with Agrippa, Dolabella, Maecenas, Gallus, and Proculeius, his council of war.

CAESAR

2429Go to him, Dolabella, bid him yield.aparte
2430Being so frustrate, tell him, he mocksaparte
2431The pauses that he makes.aparte

DOLABELLA

The pauses that he makes. Caesar, I shall.

Dolabella exits.

Enter Dercetus with the sword of Antony.

CAESAR

2432Wherefore is that? And what art thou that dar’st
2433Appear thus to us?

DERCETUS

Appear thus to us? I am called Dercetus.
2434Mark Antony I served, who best was worthy
2435Best to be served. Whilst he stood up and spoke,
2436He was my master, and I wore my life
2437To spend upon his haters. If thou please
2438To take me to thee, as I was to him
2439I’ll be to Caesar; if thou pleasest not,
2440I yield thee up my life.

CAESAR

I yield thee up my life. What is ’t thou say’st?

DERCETUS

2441I say, O Caesar, Antony is dead.

CAESAR

2442The breaking of so great a thing should make
2443A greater crack. The round world
2444Should have shook lions into civil streets
2445And citizens to their dens. The death of Antony
2446Is not a single doom; in the name lay
2447A moiety of the world.

DERCETUS

A moiety of the world. He is dead, Caesar,
2448Not by a public minister of justice,
2449Nor by a hirèd knife, but that self hand
2450Which writ his honor in the acts it did
2451Hath, with the courage which the heart did lend it,
2452Splitted the heart. This is his sword.
2453I robbed his wound of it. Behold it stained
2454With his most noble blood.

CAESAR

With his most noble blood. Look you sad, friends?
2455The gods rebuke me, but it is tidings
2456To wash the eyes of kings.

AGRIPPA

To wash the eyes of kings. And strange it is
2457That nature must compel us to lament
2458Our most persisted deeds.

MAECENAS

Our most persisted deeds. His taints and honors
2459Waged equal with him.

AGRIPPA

Waged equal with him. A rarer spirit never
2460Did steer humanity, but you gods will give us
2461Some faults to make us men. Caesar is touched.

MAECENAS

2462When such a spacious mirror’s set before him,
2463He needs must see himself.

CAESAR

He needs must see himself. O Antony,
2464I have followed thee to this, but we do lance
2465Diseases in our bodies. I must perforce
2466Have shown to thee such a declining day
2467Or look on thine. We could not stall together
2468In the whole world. But yet let me lament
2469With tears as sovereign as the blood of hearts
2470That thou my brother, my competitor
2471In top of all design, my mate in empire,
2472Friend and companion in the front of war,
2473The arm of mine own body, and the heart
2474Where mine his thoughts did kindle—that our stars
2475Unreconciliable should divide
2476Our equalness to this. Hear me, good friends—

Enter an Egyptian.

2477But I will tell you at some meeter season.
2478The business of this man looks out of him.
2479We’ll hear him what he says.—Whence are you?

EGYPTIAN

2480A poor Egyptian yet, the Queen my mistress,
2481Confined in all she has, her monument,
2482Of thy intents desires instruction,
2483That she preparedly may frame herself
2484To th’ way she’s forced to.

CAESAR

To th’ way she’s forced to. Bid her have good heart.
2485She soon shall know of us, by some of ours,
2486How honorable and how kindly we
2487Determine for her. For Caesar cannot live
2488To be ungentle.

EGYPTIAN

To be ungentle. So the gods preserve thee.

He exits.

CAESAR

2489Come hither, Proculeius. Go and say
2490We purpose her no shame. Give her what comforts
2491The quality of her passion shall require,
2492Lest, in her greatness, by some mortal stroke
2493She do defeat us, for her life in Rome
2494Would be eternal in our triumph. Go,
2495And with your speediest bring us what she says
2496And how you find of her.

PROCULEIUS

And how you find of her. Caesar, I shall.

Proculeius exits.

CAESAR

2497Gallus, go you along.

Gallus exits.

Gallus, go you along. Where’s Dolabella,
2498To second Proculeius?

ALL

To second Proculeius? Dollabella!

CAESAR

2499Let him alone, for I remember now
2500How he’s employed. He shall in time be ready.
2501Go with me to my tent, where you shall see
2502How hardly I was drawn into this war,
2503How calm and gentle I proceeded still
2504In all my writings. Go with me and see
2505What I can show in this.

They exit.

Scene 2

Enter Cleopatra, Charmian, and Iras.

CLEOPATRA

2506My desolation does begin to make
2507A better life. ’Tis paltry to be Caesar;
2508Not being Fortune, he’s but Fortune’s knave,
2509A minister of her will. And it is great
2510To do that thing that ends all other deeds,
2511Which shackles accidents and bolts up change,
2512Which sleeps and never palates more the dung,
2513The beggar’s nurse, and Caesar’s.

Enter Proculeius.

PROCULEIUS

2514Caesar sends greeting to the Queen of Egypt,
2515And bids thee study on what fair demands
2516Thou mean’st to have him grant thee.

CLEOPATRA

Thou mean’st to have him grant thee. What’s thy name?

PROCULEIUS

2517My name is Proculeius.

CLEOPATRA

My name is Proculeius. Antony
2518Did tell me of you, bade me trust you, but
2519I do not greatly care to be deceived
2520That have no use for trusting. If your master
2521Would have a queen his beggar, you must tell him
2522That majesty, to keep decorum, must
2523No less beg than a kingdom. If he please
2524To give me conquered Egypt for my son,
2525He gives me so much of mine own as I
2526Will kneel to him with thanks.

PROCULEIUS

Will kneel to him with thanks. Be of good cheer.
2527You’re fall’n into a princely hand; fear nothing.
2528Make your full reference freely to my lord,
2529Who is so full of grace that it flows over
2530On all that need. Let me report to him
2531Your sweet dependency, and you shall find
2532A conqueror that will pray in aid for kindness
2533Where he for grace is kneeled to.

CLEOPATRA

Where he for grace is kneeled to. Pray you tell him
2534I am his fortune’s vassal and I send him
2535The greatness he has got. I hourly learn
2536A doctrine of obedience, and would gladly
2537Look him i’ th’ face.

PROCULEIUS

Look him i’ th’ face. This I’ll report, dear lady.
2538Have comfort, for I know your plight is pitied
2539Of him that caused it.

Gallus and Soldiers enter and seize Cleopatra.

GALLUS

2540You see how easily she may be surprised.
2541Guard her till Caesar come.

IRAS

Guard her till Caesar come. Royal queen!

CHARMIAN

2542O, Cleopatra, thou art taken, queen!

CLEOPATRA

2543Drawing a dagger. Quick, quick, good hands!

PROCULEIUS

2544Seizing the dagger. Hold, worthy lady, hold!
2545Do not yourself such wrong, who are in this
2546Relieved, but not betrayed.

CLEOPATRA

Relieved, but not betrayed. What, of death, too,
2547That rids our dogs of languish?

PROCULEIUS

That rids our dogs of languish? Cleopatra,
2548Do not abuse my master’s bounty by
2549Th’ undoing of yourself. Let the world see
2550His nobleness well acted, which your death
2551Will never let come forth.

CLEOPATRA

Will never let come forth. Where art thou, Death?
2552Come hither, come! Come, come, and take a queen
2553Worth many babes and beggars.

PROCULEIUS

Worth many babes and beggars. O, temperance, lady!

CLEOPATRA

2554Sir, I will eat no meat; I’ll not drink, sir.
2555If idle talk will once be necessary—
2556I’ll not sleep neither. This mortal house I’ll ruin,
2557Do Caesar what he can. Know, sir, that I
2558Will not wait pinioned at your master’s court,
2559Nor once be chastised with the sober eye
2560Of dull Octavia. Shall they hoist me up
2561And show me to the shouting varletry
2562Of censuring Rome? Rather a ditch in Egypt
2563Be gentle grave unto me; rather on Nilus’ mud
2564Lay me stark naked, and let the waterflies
2565Blow me into abhorring; rather make
2566My country’s high pyramides my gibbet
2567And hang me up in chains!

PROCULEIUS

And hang me up in chains! You do extend
2568These thoughts of horror further than you shall
2569Find cause in Caesar.

Enter Dolabella.

DOLABELLA

Find cause in Caesar. Proculeius,
2570What thou hast done thy master Caesar knows,
2571And he hath sent for thee. For the Queen,
2572I’ll take her to my guard.

PROCULEIUS

I’ll take her to my guard. So, Dolabella,
2573It shall content me best. Be gentle to her.
2574To Cleopatra. To Caesar I will speak what you shall please,
2575If you’ll employ me to him.

CLEOPATRA

If you’ll employ me to him. Say I would die.

Proculeius, Gallus, and Soldiers exit.

DOLABELLA

2576Most noble empress, you have heard of me.

CLEOPATRA

2577I cannot tell.

DOLABELLA

I cannot tell. Assuredly you know me.

CLEOPATRA

2578No matter, sir, what I have heard or known.
2579You laugh when boys or women tell their dreams;
2580Is ’t not your trick?

DOLABELLA

Is ’t not your trick? I understand not, madam.

CLEOPATRA

2581I dreamt there was an emperor Antony.
2582O, such another sleep, that I might see
2583But such another man.

DOLABELLA

But such another man. If it might please you—

CLEOPATRA

2584His face was as the heavens, and therein stuck
2585A sun and moon, which kept their course and lighted
2586The little O, the Earth.

DOLABELLA

The little O, the Earth. Most sovereign creature—

CLEOPATRA

2587His legs bestrid the ocean, his reared arm
2588Crested the world. His voice was propertied
2589As all the tunèd spheres, and that to friends;
2590But when he meant to quail and shake the orb,
2591He was as rattling thunder. For his bounty,
2592There was no winter in ’t; an autumn ’twas
2593That grew the more by reaping. His delights
2594Were dolphin-like; they showed his back above
2595The element they lived in. In his livery
2596Walked crowns and crownets; realms and islands were
2597As plates dropped from his pocket.

DOLABELLA

As plates dropped from his pocket. Cleopatra—

CLEOPATRA

2598Think you there was, or might be, such a man
2599As this I dreamt of?

DOLABELLA

As this I dreamt of? Gentle madam, no.

CLEOPATRA

2600You lie up to the hearing of the gods!
2601But if there be nor ever were one such,
2602It’s past the size of dreaming. Nature wants stuff
2603To vie strange forms with fancy, yet t’ imagine
2604An Antony were nature’s piece ’gainst fancy,
2605Condemning shadows quite.

DOLABELLA

Condemning shadows quite. Hear me, good madam.
2606Your loss is as yourself, great; and you bear it
2607As answering to the weight. Would I might never
2608O’ertake pursued success but I do feel,
2609By the rebound of yours, a grief that smites
2610My very heart at root.

CLEOPATRA

My very heart at root. I thank you, sir.
2611Know you what Caesar means to do with me?

DOLABELLA

2612I am loath to tell you what I would you knew.

CLEOPATRA

2613Nay, pray you, sir.

DOLABELLA

Nay, pray you, sir. Though he be honorable—

CLEOPATRA

2614He’ll lead me, then, in triumph.

DOLABELLA

2615Madam, he will. I know ’t.

Flourish. Enter Caesar, Proculeius, Gallus, Maecenas, and others of his train.

ALL

2616Make way there! Caesar!

CAESAR

2617Which is the Queen of Egypt?

DOLABELLA

2618It is the Emperor, madam.

Cleopatra kneels.

CAESAR

2619Arise. You shall not kneel.
2620I pray you, rise. Rise, Egypt.

CLEOPATRA

I pray you, rise. Rise, Egypt. Sir, the gods
2621Will have it thus. My master and my lord
2622I must obey.

She stands.

CAESAR

I must obey. Take to you no hard thoughts.
2623The record of what injuries you did us,
2624Though written in our flesh, we shall remember
2625As things but done by chance.

CLEOPATRA

As things but done by chance. Sole sir o’ th’ world,
2626I cannot project mine own cause so well
2627To make it clear, but do confess I have
2628Been laden with like frailties which before
2629Have often shamed our sex.

CAESAR

Have often shamed our sex. Cleopatra, know
2630We will extenuate rather than enforce.
2631If you apply yourself to our intents,
2632Which towards you are most gentle, you shall find
2633A benefit in this change; but if you seek
2634To lay on me a cruelty by taking
2635Antony’s course, you shall bereave yourself
2636Of my good purposes, and put your children
2637To that destruction which I’ll guard them from
2638If thereon you rely. I’ll take my leave.

CLEOPATRA

2639And may through all the world. ’Tis yours, and we,
2640Your scutcheons and your signs of conquest, shall
2641Hang in what place you please. Here, my good lord.

She holds out a paper.

CAESAR

2642You shall advise me in all for Cleopatra.

CLEOPATRA

2643This is the brief of money, plate, and jewels
2644I am possessed of. ’Tis exactly valued,
2645Not petty things admitted.—Where’s Seleucus?

Enter Seleucus.

SELEUCUS

2646Here, madam.

CLEOPATRA

2647This is my treasurer. Let him speak, my lord,
2648Upon his peril, that I have reserved
2649To myself nothing.—Speak the truth, Seleucus.

SELEUCUS

2650Madam, I had rather seel my lips
2651Than to my peril speak that which is not.

CLEOPATRA

2652What have I kept back?

SELEUCUS

2653Enough to purchase what you have made known.

CAESAR

2654Nay, blush not, Cleopatra. I approve
2655Your wisdom in the deed.

CLEOPATRA

Your wisdom in the deed. See, Caesar, O, behold
2656How pomp is followed! Mine will now be yours,
2657And should we shift estates, yours would be mine.
2658The ingratitude of this Seleucus does
2659Even make me wild.—O slave, of no more trust
2660Than love that’s hired! What, goest thou back? Thou shalt
2661Go back, I warrant thee! But I’ll catch thine eyes
2662Though they had wings. Slave, soulless villain, dog!
2663O rarely base!

CAESAR

O rarely base! Good queen, let us entreat you—

CLEOPATRA

2664O Caesar, what a wounding shame is this,
2665That thou vouchsafing here to visit me,
2666Doing the honor of thy lordliness
2667To one so meek, that mine own servant should
2668Parcel the sum of my disgraces by
2669Addition of his envy! Say, good Caesar,
2670That I some lady trifles have reserved,
2671Immoment toys, things of such dignity
2672As we greet modern friends withal, and say
2673Some nobler token I have kept apart
2674For Livia and Octavia, to induce
2675Their mediation, must I be unfolded
2676With one that I have bred? The gods! It smites me
2677Beneath the fall I have. To Seleucus. Prithee, go hence,
2678Or I shall show the cinders of my spirits
2679Through th’ ashes of my chance. Wert thou a man,
2680Thou wouldst have mercy on me.

CAESAR

Thou wouldst have mercy on me. Forbear, Seleucus.

Seleucus exits.

CLEOPATRA

2681Be it known that we, the greatest, are misthought
2682For things that others do; and when we fall,
2683We answer others’ merits in our name—
2684Are therefore to be pitied.

CAESAR

Are therefore to be pitied. Cleopatra,
2685Not what you have reserved nor what acknowledged
2686Put we i’ th’ roll of conquest. Still be ’t yours!
2687Bestow it at your pleasure, and believe
2688Caesar’s no merchant to make prize with you
2689Of things that merchants sold. Therefore be cheered.
2690Make not your thoughts your prisons. No, dear queen,
2691For we intend so to dispose you as
2692Yourself shall give us counsel. Feed and sleep.
2693Our care and pity is so much upon you
2694That we remain your friend. And so adieu.

CLEOPATRA

2695My master and my lord!

CAESAR

My master and my lord! Not so. Adieu.
2696Flourish. Caesar and his train exit.

CLEOPATRA

2697He words me, girls, he words me, that I should not
2698Be noble to myself. But hark thee, Charmian.

She whispers to Charmian.

IRAS

2699Finish, good lady. The bright day is done,
2700And we are for the dark.

CLEOPATRA

And we are for the dark. To Charmian. Hie thee again.
2701I have spoke already, and it is provided.
2702Go put it to the haste.

CHARMIAN

Go put it to the haste. Madam, I will.

Enter Dolabella.

DOLABELLA

2703Where’s the Queen?

CHARMIAN

Where’s the Queen? Behold, sir.

She exits.

CLEOPATRA

Where’s the Queen? Behold, sir. Dolabella.

DOLABELLA

2704Madam, as thereto sworn by your command,
2705Which my love makes religion to obey,
2706I tell you this: Caesar through Syria
2707Intends his journey, and within three days
2708You with your children will he send before.
2709Make your best use of this. I have performed
2710Your pleasure and my promise.

CLEOPATRA

Your pleasure and my promise. Dolabella,
2711I shall remain your debtor.

DOLABELLA

I shall remain your debtor. I your servant.
2712Adieu, good queen. I must attend on Caesar.

CLEOPATRA

2713Farewell, and thanks.

He exits.

Farewell, and thanks. Now, Iras, what think’st thou?
2714Thou an Egyptian puppet shall be shown
2715In Rome as well as I. Mechanic slaves
2716With greasy aprons, rules, and hammers shall
2717Uplift us to the view. In their thick breaths,
2718Rank of gross diet, shall we be enclouded
2719And forced to drink their vapor.

IRAS

And forced to drink their vapor. The gods forbid!

CLEOPATRA

2720Nay, ’tis most certain, Iras. Saucy lictors
2721Will catch at us like strumpets, and scald rhymers
2722Ballad us out o’ tune. The quick comedians
2723Extemporally will stage us and present
2724Our Alexandrian revels. Antony
2725Shall be brought drunken forth, and I shall see
2726Some squeaking Cleopatra boy my greatness
2727I’ th’ posture of a whore.

IRAS

I’ th’ posture of a whore. O the good gods!

CLEOPATRA

2728Nay, that’s certain.

IRAS

2729I’ll never see ’t! For I am sure mine nails
2730Are stronger than mine eyes.

CLEOPATRA

Are stronger than mine eyes. Why, that’s the way
2731To fool their preparation and to conquer
2732Their most absurd intents.

Enter Charmian.

Their most absurd intents. Now, Charmian!
2733Show me, my women, like a queen. Go fetch
2734My best attires. I am again for Cydnus
2735To meet Mark Antony. Sirrah Iras, go.—
2736Now, noble Charmian, we’ll dispatch indeed,
2737And when thou hast done this chare, I’ll give thee leave
2738To play till Doomsday.—Bring our crown and all.

Iras exits. A noise within.

2739Wherefore’s this noise?

Enter a Guardsman.

GUARDSMAN

Wherefore’s this noise? Here is a rural fellow
2740That will not be denied your Highness’ presence.
2741He brings you figs.

CLEOPATRA

2742Let him come in.

Guardsman exits.

Let him come in. What poor an instrument
2743May do a noble deed! He brings me liberty.
2744My resolution’s placed, and I have nothing
2745Of woman in me. Now from head to foot
2746I am marble-constant. Now the fleeting moon
2747No planet is of mine.

Enter Guardsman and Countryman, with a basket.

GUARDSMAN

No planet is of mine. This is the man.

CLEOPATRA

2748Avoid, and leave him.

Guardsman exits.

2749Hast thou the pretty worm of Nilus there
2750That kills and pains not?

COUNTRYMAN

Truly I have him, but I would not be the party that should desire you to touch him, for his biting is immortal. Those that do die of it do seldom or never recover.

CLEOPATRA

2751Remember’st thou any that have died on ’t?

COUNTRYMAN

Very many, men and women too. I heard of one of them no longer than yesterday—a very honest woman, but something given to lie, as a woman should not do but in the way of honesty—how she died of the biting of it, what pain she felt. Truly, she makes a very good report o’ th’ worm. But he that will believe all that they say shall never be saved by half that they do. But this is most falliable, the worm’s an odd worm.

CLEOPATRA

2752Get thee hence. Farewell.

COUNTRYMAN

I wish you all joy of the worm.

He sets down the basket.

CLEOPATRA

2753Farewell.

COUNTRYMAN

You must think this, look you, that the worm will do his kind.

CLEOPATRA

2754Ay, ay, farewell.

COUNTRYMAN

Look you, the worm is not to be trusted but in the keeping of wise people, for indeed there is no goodness in the worm.

CLEOPATRA

2755Take thou no care; it shall be heeded.

COUNTRYMAN

Very good. Give it nothing, I pray you, for it is not worth the feeding.

CLEOPATRA

2756Will it eat me?

COUNTRYMAN

You must not think I am so simple but I know the devil himself will not eat a woman. I know that a woman is a dish for the gods if the devil dress her not. But truly these same whoreson devils do the gods great harm in their women, for in every ten that they make, the devils mar five.

CLEOPATRA

2757Well, get thee gone. Farewell.

COUNTRYMAN

Yes, forsooth. I wish you joy o’ th’ worm.

He exits.

Enter Iras bearing Cleopatra’s royal regalia.

CLEOPATRA

2758Give me my robe. Put on my crown. I have
2759Immortal longings in me. Now no more
2760The juice of Egypt’s grape shall moist this lip.

Charmian and Iras begin to dress her.

2761Yare, yare, good Iras, quick. Methinks I hear
2762Antony call. I see him rouse himself
2763To praise my noble act. I hear him mock
2764The luck of Caesar, which the gods give men
2765To excuse their after wrath.—Husband, I come!
2766Now to that name my courage prove my title.
2767I am fire and air; my other elements
2768I give to baser life.—So, have you done?
2769Come then, and take the last warmth of my lips.
2770Farewell, kind Charmian.—Iras, long farewell.

She kisses them. Iras falls and dies.

2771Have I the aspic in my lips? Dost fall?
2772If thou and nature can so gently part,
2773The stroke of death is as a lover’s pinch,
2774Which hurts and is desired. Dost thou lie still?
2775If thus thou vanishest, thou tell’st the world
2776It is not worth leave-taking.

CHARMIAN

2777Dissolve, thick cloud, and rain, that I may say
2778The gods themselves do weep!

CLEOPATRA

The gods themselves do weep! This proves me base.
2779If she first meet the curlèd Antony,
2780He’ll make demand of her, and spend that kiss
2781Which is my heaven to have.—Come, thou mortal wretch,

She places an asp on her breast.

2782With thy sharp teeth this knot intrinsicate
2783Of life at once untie. Poor venomous fool,
2784Be angry and dispatch. O, couldst thou speak,
2785That I might hear thee call great Caesar ass
2786Unpolicied!

CHARMIAN

Unpolicied! O eastern star!

CLEOPATRA

Unpolicied! O eastern star! Peace, peace!
2787Dost thou not see my baby at my breast,
2788That sucks the nurse asleep?

CHARMIAN

That sucks the nurse asleep? O, break! O, break!

CLEOPATRA

2789As sweet as balm, as soft as air, as gentle—
2790O Antony!—Nay, I will take thee too.

She places an asp on her arm.

2791What should I stay—

Dies.

CHARMIAN

What should I stay— In this wild world? So, fare thee well.
2792Now boast thee, Death, in thy possession lies
2793A lass unparalleled. Downy windows, close,

She closes Cleopatra’s eyes.

2794And golden Phoebus, never be beheld
2795Of eyes again so royal. Your crown’s awry.
2796I’ll mend it, and then play—

Enter the Guard rustling in.

FIRST GUARD

2797Where’s the Queen?

CHARMIAN

Where’s the Queen? Speak softly. Wake her not.

FIRST GUARD

2798Caesar hath sent—

CHARMIAN

Caesar hath sent— Too slow a messenger.

She takes out an asp.

2799O, come apace, dispatch! I partly feel thee.

FIRST GUARD

2800Approach, ho! All’s not well. Caesar’s beguiled.

SECOND GUARD

2801There’s Dolabella sent from Caesar. Call him.

A Guardsman exits.

FIRST GUARD

2802What work is here, Charmian? Is this well done?

CHARMIAN

2803It is well done, and fitting for a princess
2804Descended of so many royal kings.
2805Ah, soldier!

Charmian dies.

Enter Dolabella.

DOLABELLA

2806How goes it here?

SECOND GUARD

How goes it here? All dead.

DOLABELLA

How goes it here? All dead. Caesar, thy thoughts
2807Touch their effects in this. Thyself art coming
2808To see performed the dreaded act which thou
2809So sought’st to hinder.

Enter Caesar and all his train, marching.

ALL

2810A way there, a way for Caesar!

DOLABELLA

2811O sir, you are too sure an augurer:
2812That you did fear is done.

CAESAR

That you did fear is done. Bravest at the last,
2813She leveled at our purposes and, being royal,
2814Took her own way. The manner of their deaths?
2815I do not see them bleed.

DOLABELLA

I do not see them bleed. Who was last with them?

FIRST GUARD

2816A simple countryman that brought her figs.
2817This was his basket.

CAESAR

This was his basket. Poisoned, then.

FIRST GUARD

This was his basket. Poisoned, then. O Caesar,
2818This Charmian lived but now; she stood and spake.
2819I found her trimming up the diadem
2820On her dead mistress; tremblingly she stood,
2821And on the sudden dropped.

CAESAR

And on the sudden dropped. O, noble weakness!
2822If they had swallowed poison, ’twould appear
2823By external swelling; but she looks like sleep,
2824As she would catch another Antony
2825In her strong toil of grace.

DOLABELLA

In her strong toil of grace. Here on her breast
2826There is a vent of blood, and something blown.
2827The like is on her arm.

FIRST GUARD

2828This is an aspic’s trail, and these fig leaves
2829Have slime upon them, such as th’ aspic leaves
2830Upon the caves of Nile.

CAESAR

Upon the caves of Nile. Most probable
2831That so she died, for her physician tells me
2832She hath pursued conclusions infinite
2833Of easy ways to die. Take up her bed,
2834And bear her women from the monument.
2835She shall be buried by her Antony.
2836No grave upon the earth shall clip in it
2837A pair so famous. High events as these
2838Strike those that make them; and their story is
2839No less in pity than his glory which
2840Brought them to be lamented. Our army shall
2841In solemn show attend this funeral,
2842And then to Rome. Come, Dolabella, see
2843High order in this great solemnity.

They all exit, the Guards bearing the dead bodies.
