Act III

Scene 1

Enter Ventidius as it were in triumph, the dead body of Pacorus borne before him; with Silius and Soldiers.

VENTIDIUS

1106Now, darting Parthia, art thou struck, and now
1107Pleased Fortune does of Marcus Crassus’ death
1108Make me revenger. Bear the King’s son’s body
1109Before our army. Thy Pacorus, Orodes,
1110Pays this for Marcus Crassus.

SILIUS

Pays this for Marcus Crassus. Noble Ventidius,
1111Whilst yet with Parthian blood thy sword is warm,
1112The fugitive Parthians follow. Spur through Media,
1113Mesopotamia, and the shelters whither
1114The routed fly. So thy grand captain, Antony,
1115Shall set thee on triumphant chariots and
1116Put garlands on thy head.

VENTIDIUS

Put garlands on thy head. O, Silius, Silius,
1117I have done enough. A lower place, note well,
1118May make too great an act. For learn this, Silius:
1119Better to leave undone than by our deed
1120Acquire too high a fame when him we serve ’s away.
1121Caesar and Antony have ever won
1122More in their officer than person. Sossius,
1123One of my place in Syria, his lieutenant,
1124For quick accumulation of renown,
1125Which he achieved by th’ minute, lost his favor.
1126Who does i’ th’ wars more than his captain can
1127Becomes his captain’s captain; and ambition,
1128The soldier’s virtue, rather makes choice of loss
1129Than gain which darkens him.
1130I could do more to do Antonius good,
1131But ’twould offend him. And in his offense
1132Should my performance perish.

SILIUS

1133Thou hast, Ventidius, that
1134Without the which a soldier and his sword
1135Grants scarce distinction. Thou wilt write to Antony?

VENTIDIUS

1136I’ll humbly signify what in his name,
1137That magical word of war, we have effected;
1138How, with his banners and his well-paid ranks,
1139The ne’er-yet-beaten horse of Parthia
1140We have jaded out o’ th’ field.

SILIUS

We have jaded out o’ th’ field. Where is he now?

VENTIDIUS

1141He purposeth to Athens, whither, with what haste
1142The weight we must convey with ’s will permit,
1143We shall appear before him.—On there, pass along!

They exit.

Scene 2

Enter Agrippa at one door, Enobarbus at another.

AGRIPPA

1144What, are the brothers parted?

ENOBARBUS

1145They have dispatched with Pompey; he is gone.
1146The other three are sealing. Octavia weeps
1147To part from Rome. Caesar is sad, and Lepidus,
1148Since Pompey’s feast, as Menas says, is troubled
1149With the greensickness.

AGRIPPA

With the greensickness. ’Tis a noble Lepidus.

ENOBARBUS

1150A very fine one. O, how he loves Caesar!

AGRIPPA

1151Nay, but how dearly he adores Mark Antony!

ENOBARBUS

1152Caesar? Why, he’s the Jupiter of men.

AGRIPPA

1153What’s Antony? The god of Jupiter.

ENOBARBUS

1154Spake you of Caesar? How, the nonpareil!

AGRIPPA

1155O Antony, O thou Arabian bird!

ENOBARBUS

1156Would you praise Caesar, say “Caesar.” Go no further.

AGRIPPA

1157Indeed, he plied them both with excellent praises.

ENOBARBUS

1158But he loves Caesar best, yet he loves Antony.
1159Hoo, hearts, tongues, figures, scribes, bards, poets, cannot
1160Think, speak, cast, write, sing, number—hoo!—
1161His love to Antony. But as for Caesar,
1162Kneel down, kneel down, and wonder.

AGRIPPA

Kneel down, kneel down, and wonder. Both he loves.

ENOBARBUS

1163They are his shards and he their beetle.

Trumpet within.

They are his shards and he their beetle. So,
1164This is to horse. Adieu, noble Agrippa.

AGRIPPA

1165Good fortune, worthy soldier, and farewell.

Enter Caesar, Antony, Lepidus, and Octavia.

ANTONY

Good fortune, worthy soldier, and farewell. No further, sir.

CAESAR

1166You take from me a great part of myself.
1167Use me well in ’t.—Sister, prove such a wife
1168As my thoughts make thee, and as my farthest bond
1169Shall pass on thy approof.—Most noble Antony,
1170Let not the piece of virtue which is set
1171Betwixt us, as the cement of our love
1172To keep it builded, be the ram to batter
1173The fortress of it. For better might we
1174Have loved without this mean, if on both parts
1175This be not cherished.

ANTONY

This be not cherished. Make me not offended
1176In your distrust.

CAESAR

In your distrust. I have said.

ANTONY

In your distrust. I have said. You shall not find,
1177Though you be therein curious, the least cause
1178For what you seem to fear. So the gods keep you,
1179And make the hearts of Romans serve your ends.
1180We will here part.

CAESAR

1181Farewell, my dearest sister, fare thee well.
1182The elements be kind to thee and make
1183Thy spirits all of comfort. Fare thee well.

OCTAVIA

1184My noble brother.

She weeps.

ANTONY

1185The April’s in her eyes. It is love’s spring,
1186And these the showers to bring it on.—Be cheerful.

OCTAVIA

1187To Caesar. Sir, look well to my husband’s house, and—

CAESAR

1188What, Octavia?

OCTAVIA

What, Octavia? I’ll tell you in your ear.

Caesar and Octavia walk aside.

ANTONY

1189Her tongue will not obey her heart, nor can
1190Her heart inform her tongue—the swan’s-down feather
1191That stands upon the swell at the full of tide
1192And neither way inclines.

ENOBARBUS

And neither way inclines. Will Caesar weep?aparte

AGRIPPA

1193He has a cloud in ’s face.

ENOBARBUS

1194He were the worse for that were he a horse;
1195So is he being a man.

AGRIPPA

So is he being a man. Why, Enobarbus,
1196When Antony found Julius Caesar dead,
1197He cried almost to roaring. And he wept
1198When at Philippi he found Brutus slain.

ENOBARBUS

1199That year indeed he was troubled with a rheum.
1200What willingly he did confound he wailed,
1201Believe ’t, till I wept too.

CAESAR

Believe ’t, till I wept too. Coming forward with Octavia. No, sweet Octavia,
1202You shall hear from me still. The time shall not
1203Outgo my thinking on you.

ANTONY

Outgo my thinking on you. Come, sir, come,
1204I’ll wrestle with you in my strength of love.
1205Look, here I have you, thus I let you go,
1206And give you to the gods.

CAESAR

And give you to the gods. Adieu, be happy.

LEPIDUS

1207To Antony. Let all the number of the stars give light
1208To thy fair way.

CAESAR

To thy fair way. Farewell, farewell.

Kisses Octavia.

ANTONY

To thy fair way. Farewell, farewell. Farewell.

Trumpets sound. They exit.

Scene 3

Enter Cleopatra, Charmian, Iras, and Alexas.

CLEOPATRA

1209Where is the fellow?

ALEXAS

Where is the fellow? Half afeard to come.

CLEOPATRA

1210Go to, go to.—Come hither, sir.

Enter the Messenger as before.

ALEXAS

Go to, go to.—Come hither, sir. Good Majesty,
1211Herod of Jewry dare not look upon you
1212But when you are well pleased.

CLEOPATRA

But when you are well pleased. That Herod’s head
1213I’ll have! But how, when Antony is gone,
1214Through whom I might command it?—Come thou near.

MESSENGER

1215Most gracious Majesty!

CLEOPATRA

Most gracious Majesty! Did’st thou behold Octavia?

MESSENGER

1216Ay, dread queen.

CLEOPATRA

Ay, dread queen. Where?

MESSENGER

Ay, dread queen. Where? Madam, in Rome.
1217I looked her in the face and saw her led
1218Between her brother and Mark Antony.

CLEOPATRA

1219Is she as tall as me?

MESSENGER

Is she as tall as me? She is not, madam.

CLEOPATRA

1220Didst hear her speak? Is she shrill-tongued or low?

MESSENGER

1221Madam, I heard her speak. She is low-voiced.

CLEOPATRA

1222That’s not so good. He cannot like her long.

CHARMIAN

1223Like her? O Isis, ’tis impossible!

CLEOPATRA

1224I think so, Charmian: dull of tongue, and dwarfish!—
1225What majesty is in her gait? Remember,
1226If e’er thou looked’st on majesty.

MESSENGER

If e’er thou looked’st on majesty. She creeps.
1227Her motion and her station are as one.
1228She shows a body rather than a life,
1229A statue than a breather.

CLEOPATRA

A statue than a breather. Is this certain?

MESSENGER

1230Or I have no observance.

CHARMIAN

Or I have no observance. Three in Egypt
1231Cannot make better note.

CLEOPATRA

Cannot make better note. He’s very knowing.
1232I do perceive ’t. There’s nothing in her yet.
1233The fellow has good judgment.

CHARMIAN

The fellow has good judgment. Excellent.

CLEOPATRA

1234To Messenger. Guess at her years, I prithee.

MESSENGER

1235Madam, she was a widow.

CLEOPATRA

1236Widow? Charmian, hark.

MESSENGER

1237And I do think she’s thirty.

CLEOPATRA

1238Bear’st thou her face in mind? Is ’t long or round?

MESSENGER

1239Round even to faultiness.

CLEOPATRA

1240For the most part, too, they are foolish that are so.
1241Her hair what color?

MESSENGER

1242Brown, madam, and her forehead
1243As low as she would wish it.

CLEOPATRA

As low as she would wish it. Giving money. There’s gold for thee.
1244Thou must not take my former sharpness ill.
1245I will employ thee back again. I find thee
1246Most fit for business. Go, make thee ready.
1247Our letters are prepared.

Messenger exits.

CHARMIAN

Our letters are prepared. A proper man.

CLEOPATRA

1248Indeed he is so. I repent me much
1249That so I harried him. Why, methinks, by him,
1250This creature’s no such thing.

CHARMIAN

This creature’s no such thing. Nothing, madam.

CLEOPATRA

1251The man hath seen some majesty, and should know.

CHARMIAN

1252Hath he seen majesty? Isis else defend,
1253And serving you so long!

CLEOPATRA

1254I have one thing more to ask him yet, good Charmian,
1255But ’tis no matter. Thou shalt bring him to me
1256Where I will write. All may be well enough.

CHARMIAN

1257I warrant you, madam.

They exit.

Scene 4

Enter Antony and Octavia.

ANTONY

1258Nay, nay, Octavia, not only that—
1259That were excusable, that and thousands more
1260Of semblable import—but he hath waged
1261New wars ’gainst Pompey; made his will and read it
1262To public ear;
1263Spoke scantly of me; when perforce he could not
1264But pay me terms of honor, cold and sickly
1265He vented them, most narrow measure lent me;
1266When the best hint was given him, he not took ’t,
1267Or did it from his teeth.

OCTAVIA

Or did it from his teeth. O, my good lord,
1268Believe not all, or if you must believe,
1269Stomach not all. A more unhappy lady,
1270If this division chance, ne’er stood between,
1271Praying for both parts.
1272The good gods will mock me presently
1273When I shall pray “O, bless my lord and husband!”
1274Undo that prayer by crying out as loud
1275“O, bless my brother!” Husband win, win brother
1276Prays and destroys the prayer; no midway
1277’Twixt these extremes at all.

ANTONY

’Twixt these extremes at all. Gentle Octavia,
1278Let your best love draw to that point which seeks
1279Best to preserve it. If I lose mine honor,
1280I lose myself; better I were not yours
1281Than yours so branchless. But, as you requested,
1282Yourself shall go between ’s. The meantime, lady,
1283I’ll raise the preparation of a war
1284Shall stain your brother. Make your soonest haste,
1285So your desires are yours.

OCTAVIA

So your desires are yours. Thanks to my lord.
1286The Jove of power make me, most weak, most weak,
1287Your reconciler. Wars ’twixt you twain would be
1288As if the world should cleave, and that slain men
1289Should solder up the rift.

ANTONY

1290When it appears to you where this begins,
1291Turn your displeasure that way, for our faults
1292Can never be so equal that your love
1293Can equally move with them. Provide your going;
1294Choose your own company, and command what cost
1295Your heart has mind to.

They exit.

Scene 5

Enter Enobarbus and Eros.

ENOBARBUS

1296How now, friend Eros?

EROS

1297There’s strange news come, sir.

ENOBARBUS

1298What, man?

EROS

1299Caesar and Lepidus have made wars upon Pompey.

ENOBARBUS

1300This is old. What is the success?

EROS

1301Caesar, having made use of him in the wars ’gainst Pompey, presently denied him rivality, would not let him partake in the glory of the action; and, not resting here, accuses him of letters he had formerly wrote to Pompey; upon his own appeal seizes him. So the poor third is up, till death enlarge his confine.

ENOBARBUS

1302Then, world, thou hast a pair of chaps, no more,
1303And throw between them all the food thou hast,
1304They’ll grind the one the other. Where’s Antony?

EROS

1305He’s walking in the garden, thus, and spurns
1306The rush that lies before him; cries “Fool Lepidus!”
1307And threats the throat of that his officer
1308That murdered Pompey.

ENOBARBUS

That murdered Pompey. Our great navy’s rigged.

EROS

1309For Italy and Caesar. More, Domitius:
1310My lord desires you presently. My news
1311I might have told hereafter.

ENOBARBUS

I might have told hereafter. ’Twill be naught,
1312But let it be. Bring me to Antony.

EROS

1313Come, sir.

They exit.

Scene 6

Enter Agrippa, Maecenas, and Caesar.

CAESAR

1314Contemning Rome, he has done all this and more
1315In Alexandria. Here’s the manner of ’t:
1316I’ th’ marketplace, on a tribunal silvered,
1317Cleopatra and himself in chairs of gold
1318Were publicly enthroned. At the feet sat
1319Caesarion, whom they call my father’s son,
1320And all the unlawful issue that their lust
1321Since then hath made between them. Unto her
1322He gave the stablishment of Egypt, made her
1323Of lower Syria, Cyprus, Lydia,
1324Absolute queen.

MAECENAS

Absolute queen. This in the public eye?

CAESAR

1325I’ th’ common showplace where they exercise.
1326His sons he there proclaimed the kings of kings.
1327Great Media, Parthia, and Armenia
1328He gave to Alexander; to Ptolemy he assigned
1329Syria, Cilicia, and Phoenicia. She
1330In th’ habiliments of the goddess Isis
1331That day appeared, and oft before gave audience,
1332As ’tis reported, so.

MAECENAS

As ’tis reported, so. Let Rome be thus informed.

AGRIPPA

1333Who, queasy with his insolence already,
1334Will their good thoughts call from him.

CAESAR

1335The people knows it and have now received
1336His accusations.

AGRIPPA

His accusations. Who does he accuse?

CAESAR

1337Caesar, and that, having in Sicily
1338Sextus Pompeius spoiled, we had not rated him
1339His part o’ th’ isle. Then does he say he lent me
1340Some shipping, unrestored. Lastly, he frets
1341That Lepidus of the triumvirate
1342Should be deposed and, being, that we detain
1343All his revenue.

AGRIPPA

All his revenue. Sir, this should be answered.

CAESAR

1344’Tis done already, and the messenger gone.
1345I have told him Lepidus was grown too cruel,
1346That he his high authority abused
1347And did deserve his change. For what I have conquered,
1348I grant him part; but then in his Armenia
1349And other of his conquered kingdoms I
1350Demand the like.

MAECENAS

Demand the like. He’ll never yield to that.

CAESAR

1351Nor must not then be yielded to in this.

Enter Octavia with her Train.

OCTAVIA

1352Hail, Caesar, and my lord! Hail, most dear Caesar.

CAESAR

1353That ever I should call thee castaway!

OCTAVIA

1354You have not called me so, nor have you cause.

CAESAR

1355Why have you stol’n upon us thus? You come not
1356Like Caesar’s sister. The wife of Antony
1357Should have an army for an usher and
1358The neighs of horse to tell of her approach
1359Long ere she did appear. The trees by th’ way
1360Should have borne men, and expectation fainted,
1361Longing for what it had not. Nay, the dust
1362Should have ascended to the roof of heaven,
1363Raised by your populous troops. But you are come
1364A market-maid to Rome, and have prevented
1365The ostentation of our love, which, left unshown,
1366Is often left unloved. We should have met you
1367By sea and land, supplying every stage
1368With an augmented greeting.

OCTAVIA

With an augmented greeting. Good my lord,
1369To come thus was I not constrained, but did it
1370On my free will. My lord, Mark Antony,
1371Hearing that you prepared for war, acquainted
1372My grievèd ear withal, whereon I begged
1373His pardon for return.

CAESAR

His pardon for return. Which soon he granted,
1374Being an abstract ’tween his lust and him.

OCTAVIA

1375Do not say so, my lord.

CAESAR

Do not say so, my lord. I have eyes upon him,
1376And his affairs come to me on the wind.
1377Where is he now?

OCTAVIA

Where is he now? My lord, in Athens.

CAESAR

1378No, my most wrongèd sister. Cleopatra
1379Hath nodded him to her. He hath given his empire
1380Up to a whore, who now are levying
1381The kings o’ th’ Earth for war. He hath assembled
1382Bocchus, the King of Libya; Archelaus
1383Of Cappadocia; Philadelphos, King
1384Of Paphlagonia; the Thracian king, Adallas;
1385King Manchus of Arabia; King of Pont;
1386Herod of Jewry; Mithridates, King
1387Of Comagen; Polemon and Amyntas,
1388The Kings of Mede and Lycaonia,
1389With a more larger list of scepters.

OCTAVIA

1390Ay me, most wretched,
1391That have my heart parted betwixt two friends
1392That does afflict each other!

CAESAR

That does afflict each other! Welcome hither.
1393Your letters did withhold our breaking forth
1394Till we perceived both how you were wrong led
1395And we in negligent danger. Cheer your heart.
1396Be you not troubled with the time, which drives
1397O’er your content these strong necessities,
1398But let determined things to destiny
1399Hold unbewailed their way. Welcome to Rome,
1400Nothing more dear to me. You are abused
1401Beyond the mark of thought, and the high gods,
1402To do you justice, makes his ministers
1403Of us and those that love you. Best of comfort,
1404And ever welcome to us.

AGRIPPA

And ever welcome to us. Welcome, lady.

MAECENAS

1405Welcome, dear madam.
1406Each heart in Rome does love and pity you;
1407Only th’ adulterous Antony, most large
1408In his abominations, turns you off
1409And gives his potent regiment to a trull
1410That noises it against us.

OCTAVIA

That noises it against us. To Caesar Is it so, sir?

CAESAR

1411Most certain. Sister, welcome. Pray you
1412Be ever known to patience. My dear’st sister!

They exit.

Scene 7

Enter Cleopatra and Enobarbus.

CLEOPATRA

1413I will be even with thee, doubt it not.

ENOBARBUS

1414But why, why, why?

CLEOPATRA

1415Thou hast forspoke my being in these wars
1416And say’st it is not fit.

ENOBARBUS

And say’st it is not fit. Well, is it, is it?

CLEOPATRA

1417Is ’t not denounced against us? Why should not we
1418Be there in person?

ENOBARBUS

Be there in person? Well, I could reply:
1419If we should serve with horse and mares together,
1420The horse were merely lost. The mares would bear
1421A soldier and his horse.

CLEOPATRA

A soldier and his horse. What is ’t you say?

ENOBARBUS

1422Your presence needs must puzzle Antony,
1423Take from his heart, take from his brain, from ’s time
1424What should not then be spared. He is already
1425Traduced for levity, and ’tis said in Rome
1426That Photinus, an eunuch, and your maids
1427Manage this war.

CLEOPATRA

Manage this war. Sink Rome, and their tongues rot
1428That speak against us! A charge we bear i’ th’ war,
1429And as the president of my kingdom will
1430Appear there for a man. Speak not against it.
1431I will not stay behind.

Enter Antony and Canidius.

ENOBARBUS

I will not stay behind. Nay, I have done.
1432Here comes the Emperor.

ANTONY

Here comes the Emperor. Is it not strange, Canidius,
1433That from Tarentum and Brundusium
1434He could so quickly cut the Ionian Sea
1435And take in Toryne?—You have heard on ’t, sweet?

CLEOPATRA

1436Celerity is never more admired
1437Than by the negligent.

ANTONY

Than by the negligent. A good rebuke,
1438Which might have well becomed the best of men,
1439To taunt at slackness.—Canidius, we will fight
1440With him by sea.

CLEOPATRA

With him by sea. By sea, what else?

CANIDIUS

With him by sea. By sea, what else? Why will
1441My lord do so?

ANTONY

My lord do so? For that he dares us to ’t.

ENOBARBUS

1442So hath my lord dared him to single fight.

CANIDIUS

1443Ay, and to wage this battle at Pharsalia,
1444Where Caesar fought with Pompey. But these offers,
1445Which serve not for his vantage, he shakes off,
1446And so should you.

ENOBARBUS

And so should you. Your ships are not well manned,
1447Your mariners are muleteers, reapers, people
1448Engrossed by swift impress. In Caesar’s fleet
1449Are those that often have ’gainst Pompey fought.
1450Their ships are yare, yours heavy. No disgrace
1451Shall fall you for refusing him at sea,
1452Being prepared for land.

ANTONY

Being prepared for land. By sea, by sea.

ENOBARBUS

1453Most worthy sir, you therein throw away
1454The absolute soldiership you have by land,
1455Distract your army, which doth most consist
1456Of war-marked footmen, leave unexecuted
1457Your own renownèd knowledge, quite forgo
1458The way which promises assurance, and
1459Give up yourself merely to chance and hazard
1460From firm security.

ANTONY

From firm security. I’ll fight at sea.

CLEOPATRA

1461I have sixty sails, Caesar none better.

ANTONY

1462Our overplus of shipping will we burn,
1463And with the rest full-manned, from th’ head of Actium
1464Beat th’ approaching Caesar. But if we fail,
1465We then can do ’t at land.

Enter a Messenger.

We then can do ’t at land. Thy business?

MESSENGER

1466The news is true, my lord; he is descried.
1467Caesar has taken Toryne.

He exits.

ANTONY

1468Can he be there in person? ’Tis impossible;
1469Strange that his power should be. Canidius,
1470Our nineteen legions thou shalt hold by land,
1471And our twelve thousand horse. We’ll to our ship.—
1472Away, my Thetis.

Enter a Soldier.

Away, my Thetis. How now, worthy soldier?

SOLDIER

1473O noble emperor, do not fight by sea!
1474Trust not to rotten planks. Do you misdoubt
1475This sword and these my wounds? Let th’ Egyptians
1476And the Phoenicians go a-ducking. We
1477Have used to conquer standing on the earth
1478And fighting foot to foot.

ANTONY

And fighting foot to foot. Well, well, away.

Antony, Cleopatra, and Enobarbus exit.

SOLDIER

1479By Hercules, I think I am i’ th’ right.

CANIDIUS

1480Soldier, thou art, but his whole action grows
1481Not in the power on ’t. So our leader’s led,
1482And we are women’s men.

SOLDIER

And we are women’s men. You keep by land
1483The legions and the horse whole, do you not?

CANIDIUS

1484Marcus Octavius, Marcus Justeius,
1485Publicola, and Caelius are for sea,
1486But we keep whole by land. This speed of Caesar’s
1487Carries beyond belief.

SOLDIER

Carries beyond belief. While he was yet in Rome,
1488His power went out in such distractions as
1489Beguiled all spies.

CANIDIUS

Beguiled all spies. Who’s his lieutenant, hear you?

SOLDIER

1490They say one Taurus.

CANIDIUS

They say one Taurus. Well I know the man.

Enter a Messenger.

MESSENGER

1491The Emperor calls Canidius.

CANIDIUS

1492With news the time’s in labor, and throws forth
1493Each minute some.

They exit.

Scene 8

Enter Caesar with his army, and Taurus, marching.

CAESAR

1494Taurus!

TAURUS

1495My lord?

CAESAR

1496Strike not by land, keep whole. Provoke not battle
1497Till we have done at sea. Do not exceed
1498The prescript of this scroll.

Hands him a scroll.

The prescript of this scroll. Our fortune lies
1499Upon this jump.

They exit.

Scene 9

Enter Antony and Enobarbus.

ANTONY

1500Set we our squadrons on yond side o’ th’ hill
1501In eye of Caesar’s battle, from which place
1502We may the number of the ships behold
1503And so proceed accordingly.

They exit.

Scene 10
