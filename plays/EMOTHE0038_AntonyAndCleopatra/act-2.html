Act II

Scene 1

Enter Pompey, Menecrates, and Menas, in warlike manner.

POMPEY

458If the great gods be just, they shall assist
459The deeds of justest men.

MENAS

The deeds of justest men. Know, worthy Pompey,
460That what they do delay they not deny.

POMPEY

461Whiles we are suitors to their throne, decays
462The thing we sue for.

MENAS

The thing we sue for. We, ignorant of ourselves,
463Beg often our own harms, which the wise powers
464Deny us for our good; so find we profit
465By losing of our prayers.

POMPEY

By losing of our prayers. I shall do well.
466The people love me, and the sea is mine;
467My powers are crescent, and my auguring hope
468Says it will come to th’ full. Mark Antony
469In Egypt sits at dinner, and will make
470No wars without doors. Caesar gets money where
471He loses hearts. Lepidus flatters both,
472Of both is flattered; but he neither loves,
473Nor either cares for him.

MENAS

Nor either cares for him. Caesar and Lepidus
474Are in the field. A mighty strength they carry.

POMPEY

475Where have you this? ’Tis false.

MENAS

Where have you this? ’Tis false. From Silvius, sir.

POMPEY

476He dreams. I know they are in Rome together,
477Looking for Antony. But all the charms of love,
478Salt Cleopatra, soften thy wanned lip!
479Let witchcraft join with beauty, lust with both;
480Tie up the libertine in a field of feasts;
481Keep his brain fuming. Epicurean cooks
482Sharpen with cloyless sauce his appetite,
483That sleep and feeding may prorogue his honor
484Even till a Lethe’d dullness—

Enter Varrius.

Even till a Lethe’d dullness— How now, Varrius?

VARRIUS

485This is most certain that I shall deliver:
486Mark Antony is every hour in Rome
487Expected. Since he went from Egypt ’tis
488A space for farther travel.

POMPEY

489I could have given less matter
490A better ear.—Menas, I did not think
491This amorous surfeiter would have donned his helm
492For such a petty war. His soldiership
493Is twice the other twain. But let us rear
494The higher our opinion, that our stirring
495Can from the lap of Egypt’s widow pluck
496The ne’er lust-wearied Antony.

MENAS

The ne’er lust-wearied Antony. I cannot hope
497Caesar and Antony shall well greet together.
498His wife that’s dead did trespasses to Caesar;
499His brother warred upon him, although I think
500Not moved by Antony.

POMPEY

Not moved by Antony. I know not, Menas,
501How lesser enmities may give way to greater.
502Were ’t not that we stand up against them all,
503’Twere pregnant they should square between themselves,
504For they have entertainèd cause enough
505To draw their swords. But how the fear of us
506May cement their divisions and bind up
507The petty difference, we yet not know.
508Be ’t as our gods will have ’t. It only stands
509Our lives upon to use our strongest hands.
510Come, Menas.

They exit.

Scene 2

Enter Enobarbus and Lepidus.

LEPIDUS

511Good Enobarbus, ’tis a worthy deed,
512And shall become you well, to entreat your captain
513To soft and gentle speech.

ENOBARBUS

To soft and gentle speech. I shall entreat him
514To answer like himself. If Caesar move him,
515Let Antony look over Caesar’s head
516And speak as loud as Mars. By Jupiter,
517Were I the wearer of Antonio’s beard,
518I would not shave ’t today.

LEPIDUS

519’Tis not a time for private stomaching.

ENOBARBUS

Every time serves for the matter that is then born in ’t.

LEPIDUS

520But small to greater matters must give way.

ENOBARBUS

521Not if the small come first.

LEPIDUS

522Your speech is passion; but pray you stir
523No embers up. Here comes the noble Antony.

Enter, at one door, Antony and Ventidius.

ENOBARBUS

524And yonder Caesar.

Enter, at another door, Caesar, Maecenas, and Agrippa.

ANTONY

525To Ventidius. If we compose well here, to Parthia.
526Hark, Ventidius.

They talk aside.

CAESAR

527To Maecenas. I do not know, Maecenas. Ask Agrippa.

LEPIDUS

528To Caesar and Antony. Noble friends,
529That which combined us was most great, and let not
530A leaner action rend us. What’s amiss,
531May it be gently heard. When we debate
532Our trivial difference loud, we do commit
533Murder in healing wounds. Then, noble partners,
534The rather for I earnestly beseech,
535Touch you the sourest points with sweetest terms,
536Nor curstness grow to th’ matter.

ANTONY

Nor curstness grow to th’ matter. ’Tis spoken well.
537Were we before our armies, and to fight,
538I should do thus.

Flourish.

CAESAR

539Welcome to Rome.

ANTONY

540Thank you.

CAESAR

541Sit.

ANTONY

542Sit, sir.

CAESAR

543Nay, then.

They sit.

ANTONY

544I learn you take things ill which are not so,
545Or, being, concern you not.

CAESAR

Or, being, concern you not. I must be laughed at
546If or for nothing or a little, I
547Should say myself offended, and with you
548Chiefly i’ th’ world; more laughed at, that I should
549Once name you derogately when to sound your name
550It not concerned me.

ANTONY

551My being in Egypt, Caesar, what was ’t to you?

CAESAR

552No more than my residing here at Rome
553Might be to you in Egypt. Yet if you there
554Did practice on my state, your being in Egypt
555Might be my question.

ANTONY

Might be my question. How intend you, practiced?

CAESAR

556You may be pleased to catch at mine intent
557By what did here befall me. Your wife and brother
558Made wars upon me, and their contestation
559Was theme for you; you were the word of war.

ANTONY

560You do mistake your business. My brother never
561Did urge me in his act. I did inquire it,
562And have my learning from some true reports
563That drew their swords with you. Did he not rather
564Discredit my authority with yours,
565And make the wars alike against my stomach,
566Having alike your cause? Of this my letters
567Before did satisfy you. If you’ll patch a quarrel,
568As matter whole you have to make it with,
569It must not be with this.

CAESAR

It must not be with this. You praise yourself
570By laying defects of judgment to me; but
571You patched up your excuses.

ANTONY

You patched up your excuses. Not so, not so.
572I know you could not lack—I am certain on ’t—
573Very necessity of this thought, that I,
574Your partner in the cause ’gainst which he fought,
575Could not with graceful eyes attend those wars
576Which fronted mine own peace. As for my wife,
577I would you had her spirit in such another.
578The third o’ th’ world is yours, which with a snaffle
579You may pace easy, but not such a wife.

ENOBARBUS

Would we had all such wives, that the men might go to wars with the women!

ANTONY

580So much uncurbable, her garboils, Caesar,
581Made out of her impatience—which not wanted
582Shrewdness of policy too—I grieving grant
583Did you too much disquiet. For that you must
584But say I could not help it.

CAESAR

But say I could not help it. I wrote to you
585When rioting in Alexandria; you
586Did pocket up my letters, and with taunts
587Did gibe my missive out of audience.

ANTONY

Did gibe my missive out of audience. Sir,
588He fell upon me ere admitted, then;
589Three kings I had newly feasted, and did want
590Of what I was i’ th’ morning. But next day
591I told him of myself, which was as much
592As to have asked him pardon. Let this fellow
593Be nothing of our strife; if we contend,
594Out of our question wipe him.

CAESAR

Out of our question wipe him. You have broken
595The article of your oath, which you shall never
596Have tongue to charge me with.

LEPIDUS

597Soft, Caesar!

ANTONY

598No, Lepidus, let him speak.
599The honor is sacred which he talks on now,
600Supposing that I lacked it.—But on, Caesar:
601The article of my oath?

CAESAR

602To lend me arms and aid when I required them,
603The which you both denied.

ANTONY

The which you both denied. Neglected, rather;
604And then when poisoned hours had bound me up
605From mine own knowledge. As nearly as I may
606I’ll play the penitent to you. But mine honesty
607Shall not make poor my greatness, nor my power
608Work without it. Truth is that Fulvia,
609To have me out of Egypt, made wars here,
610For which myself, the ignorant motive, do
611So far ask pardon as befits mine honor
612To stoop in such a case.

LEPIDUS

To stoop in such a case. ’Tis noble spoken.

MAECENAS

613If it might please you to enforce no further
614The griefs between you, to forget them quite
615Were to remember that the present need
616Speaks to atone you.

LEPIDUS

Speaks to atone you. Worthily spoken, Maecenas.

ENOBARBUS

Or, if you borrow one another’s love for the instant, you may, when you hear no more words of Pompey, return it again. You shall have time to wrangle in when you have nothing else to do.

ANTONY

617Thou art a soldier only. Speak no more.

ENOBARBUS

That truth should be silent I had almost forgot.

ANTONY

618You wrong this presence; therefore speak no more.

ENOBARBUS

619Go to, then. Your considerate stone.

CAESAR

620I do not much dislike the matter, but
621The manner of his speech; for ’t cannot be
622We shall remain in friendship, our conditions
623So diff’ring in their acts. Yet if I knew
624What hoop should hold us staunch, from edge to edge
625O’ th’ world I would pursue it.

AGRIPPA

626Give me leave, Caesar.

CAESAR

627Speak, Agrippa.

AGRIPPA

628Thou hast a sister by the mother’s side,
629Admired Octavia. Great Mark Antony
630Is now a widower.

CAESAR

Is now a widower. Say not so, Agrippa.
631If Cleopatra heard you, your reproof
632Were well deserved of rashness.

ANTONY

633I am not married, Caesar. Let me hear

Agrippa further speak.

AGRIPPA

634To hold you in perpetual amity,
635To make you brothers, and to knit your hearts
636With an unslipping knot, take Antony
637Octavia to his wife, whose beauty claims
638No worse a husband than the best of men;
639Whose virtue and whose general graces speak
640That which none else can utter. By this marriage
641All little jealousies, which now seem great,
642And all great fears, which now import their dangers,
643Would then be nothing. Truths would be tales,
644Where now half-tales be truths. Her love to both
645Would each to other and all loves to both
646Draw after her. Pardon what I have spoke,
647For ’tis a studied, not a present thought,
648By duty ruminated.

ANTONY

By duty ruminated. Will Caesar speak?

CAESAR

649Not till he hears how Antony is touched
650With what is spoke already.

ANTONY

651What power is in Agrippa,
652If I would say “Agrippa, be it so,”
653To make this good?

CAESAR

To make this good? The power of Caesar, and
654His power unto Octavia.

ANTONY

His power unto Octavia. May I never
655To this good purpose, that so fairly shows,
656Dream of impediment. Let me have thy hand.
657Further this act of grace; and from this hour
658The heart of brothers govern in our loves
659And sway our great designs.

CAESAR

And sway our great designs. There’s my hand.

They clasp hands.

660A sister I bequeath you whom no brother
661Did ever love so dearly. Let her live
662To join our kingdoms and our hearts; and never
663Fly off our loves again.

LEPIDUS

Fly off our loves again. Happily, amen!

ANTONY

664I did not think to draw my sword ’gainst Pompey,
665For he hath laid strange courtesies and great
666Of late upon me. I must thank him only,
667Lest my remembrance suffer ill report;
668At heel of that, defy him.

LEPIDUS

At heel of that, defy him. Time calls upon ’s.
669Of us must Pompey presently be sought,
670Or else he seeks out us.

ANTONY

671Where lies he?

CAESAR

672About the Mount Misena.

ANTONY

673What is his strength by land?

CAESAR

674Great and increasing;
675But by sea he is an absolute master.

ANTONY

676So is the fame.
677Would we had spoke together. Haste we for it.
678Yet, ere we put ourselves in arms, dispatch we
679The business we have talked of.

CAESAR

The business we have talked of. With most gladness,
680And do invite you to my sister’s view,
681Whither straight I’ll lead you.

ANTONY

682Let us, Lepidus, not lack your company.

LEPIDUS

683Noble Antony, not sickness should detain me.

Flourish. All but Enobarbus, Agrippa, and Maecenas exit.

MAECENAS

684To Enobarbus. Welcome from Egypt, sir.

ENOBARBUS

Half the heart of Caesar, worthy Maecenas!—My honorable friend Agrippa!

AGRIPPA

Good Enobarbus!

MAECENAS

We have cause to be glad that matters are so well digested. You stayed well by ’t in Egypt.

ENOBARBUS

Ay, sir, we did sleep day out of countenance and made the night light with drinking.

MAECENAS

Eight wild boars roasted whole at a breakfast, and but twelve persons there. Is this true?

ENOBARBUS

This was but as a fly by an eagle. We had much more monstrous matter of feast, which worthily deserved noting.

MAECENAS

She’s a most triumphant lady, if report be square to her.

ENOBARBUS

When she first met Mark Antony, she pursed up his heart upon the river of Cydnus.

AGRIPPA

There she appeared indeed, or my reporter devised well for her.

ENOBARBUS

685I will tell you.
686The barge she sat in like a burnished throne
687Burned on the water. The poop was beaten gold,
688Purple the sails, and so perfumed that
689The winds were lovesick with them. The oars were silver,
690Which to the tune of flutes kept stroke, and made
691The water which they beat to follow faster,
692As amorous of their strokes. For her own person,
693It beggared all description: she did lie
694In her pavilion—cloth-of-gold, of tissue—
695O’erpicturing that Venus where we see
696The fancy outwork nature. On each side her
697Stood pretty dimpled boys, like smiling Cupids,
698With divers-colored fans, whose wind did seem
699To glow the delicate cheeks which they did cool,
700And what they undid did.

AGRIPPA

And what they undid did. O, rare for Antony!

ENOBARBUS

701Her gentlewomen, like the Nereides,
702So many mermaids, tended her i’ th’ eyes,
703And made their bends adornings. At the helm
704A seeming mermaid steers. The silken tackle
705Swell with the touches of those flower-soft hands
706That yarely frame the office. From the barge
707A strange invisible perfume hits the sense
708Of the adjacent wharfs. The city cast
709Her people out upon her; and Antony,
710Enthroned i’ th’ market-place, did sit alone,
711Whistling to th’ air, which but for vacancy
712Had gone to gaze on Cleopatra too
713And made a gap in nature.

AGRIPPA

And made a gap in nature. Rare Egyptian!

ENOBARBUS

714Upon her landing, Antony sent to her,
715Invited her to supper. She replied
716It should be better he became her guest,
717Which she entreated. Our courteous Antony,
718Whom ne’er the word of “No” woman heard speak,
719Being barbered ten times o’er, goes to the feast,
720And for his ordinary pays his heart
721For what his eyes eat only.

AGRIPPA

For what his eyes eat only. Royal wench!
722She made great Caesar lay his sword to bed;
723He ploughed her, and she cropped.

ENOBARBUS

He ploughed her, and she cropped. I saw her once
724Hop forty paces through the public street,
725And having lost her breath, she spoke and panted,
726That she did make defect perfection,
727And breathless pour breath forth.

MAECENAS

728Now Antony must leave her utterly.

ENOBARBUS

729Never. He will not.
730Age cannot wither her, nor custom stale
731Her infinite variety. Other women cloy
732The appetites they feed, but she makes hungry
733Where most she satisfies. For vilest things
734Become themselves in her, that the holy priests
735Bless her when she is riggish.

MAECENAS

736If beauty, wisdom, modesty can settle
737The heart of Antony, Octavia is
738A blessèd lottery to him.

AGRIPPA

A blessèd lottery to him. Let us go.
739Good Enobarbus, make yourself my guest
740Whilst you abide here.

ENOBARBUS

Whilst you abide here. Humbly, sir, I thank you.

They exit.

Scene 3

Enter Antony, Caesar; Octavia between them.

ANTONY

741The world and my great office will sometimes
742Divide me from your bosom.

OCTAVIA

Divide me from your bosom. All which time
743Before the gods my knee shall bow my prayers
744To them for you.

ANTONY

To them for you. To Caesar. Goodnight, sir.—My Octavia,
745Read not my blemishes in the world’s report.
746I have not kept my square, but that to come
747Shall all be done by th’ rule. Good night, dear lady.—
748Good night, sir.

CAESAR

749Goodnight.

Caesar and Octavia exit.

Enter Soothsayer.

ANTONY

750Now, sirrah, you do wish yourself in Egypt?

SOOTHSAYER

751Would I had never come from thence, nor you thither.

ANTONY

752If you can, your reason?

SOOTHSAYER

I see it in my motion, have it not in my tongue. But yet hie you to Egypt again.

ANTONY

753Say to me, whose fortunes shall rise higher,
754Caesar’s or mine?

SOOTHSAYER

755Caesar’s.
756Therefore, O Antony, stay not by his side.
757Thy dæmon—that thy spirit which keeps thee—is
758Noble, courageous, high, unmatchable,
759Where Caesar’s is not. But near him, thy angel
760Becomes afeard, as being o’erpowered. Therefore
761Make space enough between you.

ANTONY

Make space enough between you. Speak this no more.

SOOTHSAYER

762To none but thee; no more but when to thee.
763If thou dost play with him at any game,
764Thou art sure to lose; and of that natural luck
765He beats thee ’gainst the odds. Thy luster thickens
766When he shines by. I say again, thy spirit
767Is all afraid to govern thee near him;
768But he away, ’tis noble.

ANTONY

But he away, ’tis noble. Get thee gone.
769Say to Ventidius I would speak with him.

Soothsayer exits.

770He shall to Parthia. Be it art or hap,
771He hath spoken true. The very dice obey him,
772And in our sports my better cunning faints
773Under his chance. If we draw lots, he speeds;
774His cocks do win the battle still of mine
775When it is all to naught, and his quails ever
776Beat mine, inhooped, at odds. I will to Egypt.
777And though I make this marriage for my peace,
778I’ th’ East my pleasure lies.

Enter Ventidius.

779O, come, Ventidius.
780You must to Parthia; your commission’s ready.
781Follow me and receive ’t.

They exit.

Scene 4

Enter Lepidus, Maecenas, and Agrippa.

LEPIDUS

782Trouble yourselves no further. Pray you hasten
783Your generals after.

AGRIPPA

Your generals after. Sir, Mark Antony
784Will e’en but kiss Octavia, and we’ll follow.

LEPIDUS

785Till I shall see you in your soldiers’ dress,
786Which will become you both, farewell.

MAECENAS

Which will become you both, farewell. We shall,
787As I conceive the journey, be at the Mount
788Before you, Lepidus.

LEPIDUS

Before you, Lepidus. Your way is shorter;
789My purposes do draw me much about.
790You’ll win two days upon me.

BOTH

You’ll win two days upon me. Sir, good success.

LEPIDUS

791Farewell.

They exit.

Scene 5

Enter Cleopatra, Charmian, Iras, and Alexas.

CLEOPATRA

792Give me some music—music, moody food
793Of us that trade in love.

ALL

Of us that trade in love. The music, ho!

Enter Mardian the eunuch.

CLEOPATRA

794Let it alone. Let’s to billiards. Come, Charmian.

CHARMIAN

795My arm is sore. Best play with Mardian.

CLEOPATRA

796As well a woman with an eunuch played
797As with a woman.—Come, you’ll play with me, sir?

MARDIAN

798As well as I can, madam.

CLEOPATRA

799And when good will is showed, though ’t come too short,
800The actor may plead pardon. I’ll none now.
801Give me mine angle; we’ll to th’ river. There,
802My music playing far off, I will betray
803Tawny-finned fishes. My bended hook shall pierce
804Their slimy jaws, and as I draw them up
805I’ll think them every one an Antony
806And say “Aha! You’re caught.”

CHARMIAN

And say “Aha! You’re caught.” ’Twas merry when
807You wagered on your angling; when your diver
808Did hang a salt fish on his hook, which he
809With fervency drew up.

CLEOPATRA

With fervency drew up. That time?—O, times!—
810I laughed him out of patience; and that night
811I laughed him into patience; and next morn,
812Ere the ninth hour, I drunk him to his bed,
813Then put my tires and mantles on him, whilst
814I wore his sword Philippan.

Enter a Messenger.

I wore his sword Philippan. O, from Italy!
815Ram thou thy fruitful tidings in mine ears,
816That long time have been barren.

MESSENGER

That long time have been barren. Madam, madam—

CLEOPATRA

817Antonio’s dead! If thou say so, villain,
818Thou kill’st thy mistress. But well and free,
819If thou so yield him, there is gold, and here
820My bluest veins to kiss, a hand that kings
821Have lipped and trembled kissing.

MESSENGER

822First, madam, he is well.

CLEOPATRA

823Why, there’s more gold. But sirrah, mark, we use
824To say the dead are well. Bring it to that,
825The gold I give thee will I melt and pour
826Down thy ill-uttering throat.

MESSENGER

827Good madam, hear me.

CLEOPATRA

828Well, go to, I will.
829But there’s no goodness in thy face—if Antony
830Be free and healthful, so tart a favor
831To trumpet such good tidings! If not well,
832Thou shouldst come like a Fury crowned with snakes,
833Not like a formal man.

MESSENGER

Not like a formal man. Will ’t please you hear me?

CLEOPATRA

834I have a mind to strike thee ere thou speak’st
835Yet if thou say Antony lives, is well,
836Or friends with Caesar or not captive to him,
837I’ll set thee in a shower of gold and hail
838Rich pearls upon thee.

MESSENGER

Rich pearls upon thee. Madam, he’s well.

CLEOPATRA

Rich pearls upon thee. Madam, he’s well. Well said.

MESSENGER

839And friends with Caesar.

CLEOPATRA

And friends with Caesar. Th’ art an honest man.

MESSENGER

840Caesar and he are greater friends than ever.

CLEOPATRA

841Make thee a fortune from me.

MESSENGER

Make thee a fortune from me. But yet, madam—

CLEOPATRA

842I do not like “But yet.” It does allay
843The good precedence. Fie upon “But yet.”
844“But yet” is as a jailer to bring forth
845Some monstrous malefactor. Prithee, friend,
846Pour out the pack of matter to mine ear,
847The good and bad together: he’s friends with Caesar,
848In state of health, thou say’st, and, thou say’st, free.

MESSENGER

849Free, madam, no. I made no such report.
850He’s bound unto Octavia.

CLEOPATRA

He’s bound unto Octavia. For what good turn?

MESSENGER

851For the best turn i’ th’ bed.

CLEOPATRA

For the best turn i’ th’ bed. I am pale, Charmian.

MESSENGER

852Madam, he’s married to Octavia.

CLEOPATRA

853The most infectious pestilence upon thee!

Strikes him down.

MESSENGER

854Good madam, patience!

CLEOPATRA

855What say you?

Strikes him.

856Hence, horrible villain, or I’ll spurn thine eyes
857Like balls before me! I’ll unhair thy head!

She hales him up and down.

858Thou shalt be whipped with wire and stewed in brine,
859Smarting in ling’ring pickle.

MESSENGER

Smarting in ling’ring pickle. Gracious madam,
860I that do bring the news made not the match.

CLEOPATRA

861Say ’tis not so, a province I will give thee
862And make thy fortunes proud. The blow thou hadst
863Shall make thy peace for moving me to rage,
864And I will boot thee with what gift beside
865Thy modesty can beg.

MESSENGER

Thy modesty can beg. He’s married, madam.

CLEOPATRA

866Rogue, thou hast lived too long.

Draw a knife.

MESSENGER

Rogue, thou hast lived too long. Nay then, I’ll run.
867What mean you, madam? I have made no fault.

He exits.

CHARMIAN

868Good madam, keep yourself within yourself.
869The man is innocent.

CLEOPATRA

870Some innocents ’scape not the thunderbolt.
871Melt Egypt into Nile, and kindly creatures
872Turn all to serpents! Call the slave again.
873Though I am mad, I will not bite him. Call!

CHARMIAN

874He is afeard to come.

CLEOPATRA

875I will not hurt him.
876These hands do lack nobility that they strike
877A meaner than myself, since I myself
878Have given myself the cause.

Enter the Messenger again.

Have given myself the cause. Come hither, sir.
879Though it be honest, it is never good
880To bring bad news. Give to a gracious message
881An host of tongues, but let ill tidings tell
882Themselves when they be felt.

MESSENGER

883I have done my duty.

CLEOPATRA

884Is he married?
885I cannot hate thee worser than I do
886If thou again say “yes.”

MESSENGER

If thou again say “yes.” He’s married, madam.

CLEOPATRA

887The gods confound thee! Dost thou hold there still?

MESSENGER

888Should I lie, madam?

CLEOPATRA

Should I lie, madam? O, I would thou didst,
889So half my Egypt were submerged and made
