Act IV

Actus Quartus, Scaena Prima

[Enter] Tellus.

TELLUS

0001I marvel Corsites giveth me so much liberty –all the 0002world knowing his charge to be so high and his nature to 0003be most strange, who hath so ill entreated ladies of great 0004honour that he hath not suffered them to look out of 0005windows, much less to walk abroad. It may be he is in 0006love with me, for, Endymion, hardhearted Endymion, 0007excepted, what is he that is not enamoured of my beauty? 0008But what respectest thou the love of all the world? 0009Endymion hates thee. Alas, poor Endymion, my malice 0010hath exceeded my love, and thy faith to Cynthia 0011quenched my affections. Quenched, Tellus? Nay, kindled 0012them afresh, insomuch that I find scorching flames for 0013dead embers, and cruel encounters of war in my thoughts 0014instead of sweet parleys. Ah, that I might once again see 0015Endymion! Accursed girl, what hope hast thou to see 0016Endymion, on whose head already are grown grey hairs, 0017and whose life must yield to nature before Cynthia end 0018her displeasure? Wicked Dipsas, and most devilish 0019Tellus, the one for cunning too exquisite, the other for 0020hate too intolerable! Thou vast commanded to weave the 0021stories and poetries wherein were showed both examples 0022and punishments of tattling tongues, and thou hast only 0023embroidered the sweet face of Endymion, devices of love, 0024melancholy imaginations, and what not out of thy work, 0025that thou shouldst study to pick out of thy mind. But here 0026cometh Corsites. I must seem yielding and stout, full of 0027mildness yet tempered with a majesty. For if I be too 0028flexible I shall give him more hope than I mean; if too 0029froward, enjoy less liberty than I would. Love him I 0030cannot, and therefore will practise that which is most 0031contrary to our sex, to dissemble.

Enter Corsites.

CORSITES

0032Fair Tellus, I perceive you rise with the lark, and to 0033yourself sing with the nightingale.

TELLUS

0034My lord, I have no playfellow but fancy. Being barred 0035of all company, I must question with myself and make my 0036thoughts my friends.

CORSITES

0037I would you would account my thoughts also your 0038friends, for they be such as are only busied in wondering 0039at your beauty and wisdom, and some such as have 0040esteemed your fortune too hard, and divers of that kind 0041that offer to set you free if you will set them free.

TELLUS

0042There are no colours so contrary as white and 0043black, nor elements so disagreeing as fire and water, 0044nor anything so opposite as men's thoughts and their 0045words.

CORSITES

0046He that gave Cassandra the gift of prophesying, with 0047the curse that, spake she never so true, she should never 0048be believed, hath, I think, poisoned the fortune of men, 0049that, uttering the extremities of their inward passions, are 0050always suspected of outward perjuries.

TELLUS

0051Well, Corsites, I will flatter myself and believe you. 0052What would you do to enjoy my love?

CORSITES

0053Set all the ladies of the castle free and make you the 0054pleasure of my life. More I cannot do; less I will not.

TELLUS

0055These be great words, and fit your calling, for captains 0056must promise things impossible. But will you do one 0057thing for all?

CORSITES

0058Anything, sweet Tellus, that am ready for all.

TELLUS

0059You know that on the lunary bank sleepeth Endymion.

CORSITES

0060I know it.

TELLUS

0061If you will remove him from that place by force and 0062convey him into some obscure cave by policy, I give you 0063here the faith of an unspotted virgin that you only shall 0064possess me as a lover and, in spite of malice, have me for 0065a wife.

CORSITES

0066Remove him, Tellus? Yes, Tellus, he shall be removed, 0067and that so soon as thou shalt as much commend 0068my diligence as my force. I go.

[He starts to leave.]

TELLUS

0069Stay. Will yourself attempt it?

CORSITES

0070Ay, Tellus. As I would have none partaker of my 0071sweet love, so shall none be partners of my labours. But 0072I pray thee go at your best leisure, for Cynthia beginneth 0073to rise, and if she discover our love we both perish, for 0074nothing pleaseth her but the fairness of virginity. All 0075things must be not only without lust but without suspicion 0076of lightness.

TELLUS

0077I will depart, and go you to Endymion.

CORSITES

0078I fly, Tellus, being of all men the most fortunate.

Exit.

TELLUS

0079Simple Corsites! I have set thee about a task, being but 0080a man, that the gods themselves cannot perform. For 0081little dost thou know how heavy his head lies, how hard 0082his fortune. But such shifts must women have to deceive 0083men, and, under colour of things easy, entreat that which 0084is impossible. Otherwise we should be cumbered with 0085importunities, oaths, sighs, letters, and all implements of 0086love, which, to one resolved to the contrary, are most 0087loathsome. I will in and laugh with the other ladies at 0088Corsites' sweating.

Exit.

Actus Quartus, Scaena Secunda

[Enter] Samias [and] Dares.

SAMIAS

0001Will thy master never wake?

DARES

0002No, I think he sleeps for a wager. But how shall we 0003spend the time? Sir Tophas is so far in love that he pineth 0004in his bed and cometh not abroad.

SAMIAS

0005But here cometh Epi, in a pelting chafe.

[Enter] Epiton.

EPITON

0006A pox of all false proverbs! And, were a proverb a 0007page, I would have him by the ears.

SAMIAS

0008Why art thou angry?

EPITON

0009Why? You know it is said, the tide tarrieth no man.

SAMIAS

0010True.

EPITON

0011A monstrous lie; for I was tied two hours, and tarried 0012for one to unloose me.

DARES

0013Alas, poor Epi!

EPITON

0014Poor? No, no, you base-conceited slaves, I am a most 0015complete gentlemen, although I be in disgrace with Sir 0016Tophas.

DARES

0017Art thou out with him?

EPITON

0018Ay, because I cannot get him a lodging with 0019Endymion. He would fain take a nap for forty or fifty 0020years.

DARES

0021A short sleep, considering our long life.

SAMIAS

0022Is he still in love?

EPITON

0023In love? Why, he doth nothing but make sonnets.

SAMIAS

0024Canst thou remember any one of his poems?

EPITON

0025Ay, this is one:

26The beggar Love that knows not where to lodge,
27At last within my heart when I slept,
28He crept.
29I waked, and so my fancies began to fodge.

SAMIAS

0030That's a very long verse.

EPITON

0031Why, the other was short. The first is called from the 0032thumb to the little finger, the second from the little finger 0033to the elbow, and some he hath made to reach to the 0034crown of his head and down again to the sole of his foot. 0035It is set to the tune of the Black Saunce, ratio est, because 0036Dipsas is a black saint.

DARES

0037Very wisely. But pray thee, Epi, how art thou complete? 0038And, being from thy master, what occupation wilt thou take?

EPITON

0039Know, my hearts, I am an absolute microcosmos, a 0040petty world of myself. My library is my head, for I have no 0041other books but my brains; my wardrobe on my back, for 0042I have no more apparel than is on my body; my armoury 0043at my fingers' ends, for I use no other artillery than my 0044nails; my treasure in my purse. Sic omnia mea mecum porto.

DARES

0045Good.

EPITON

0046Now, sirs, my palace is paved with grass and tiled with 0047stars; for caelo tegitur qui non habet urnam, he that hath no 0048house must lie in the yard.

SAMIAS

0049A brave resolution. But how wilt thou spend thy 0050time?

EPITON

0051Not in any melancholy sort. For mine exercise I will 0052walk horses.

DARES

0053Too bad.

EPITON

0054Why, is it not said, 'It is good walking when one hath 0055his horse in his hand'?

SAMIAS

0056Worse and worse. But how wilt thou live?

EPITON

0057By angling. O, 'tis a stately occupation to stand four 0058hours in a cold morning and to have his nose bitten with 0059frost before his bait be mumbled with a fish.

DARES

0060A rare attempt. But wilt thou never travel?

EPITON

0061Yes, in a western barge, when, with a good wind and 0062lusty pugs, one may go ten miles in two days.

SAMIAS

0063Thou art excellent at thy choice. But what pastime 0064wilt thou use? None?

EPITON

0065Yes, the quickest of all.

SAMIAS

0066What, dice?

EPITON

0067No. When I am in a haste, one-and-twenty games at 0068chess to pass a few minutes.

DARES

0069A life for a little lord, and full of quickness.

EPITON

0070Tush, let me alone. But I must needs see if I can find 0071where Endymion lieth, and then go to a certain fountain 0072hard by, where they say faithful lovers shall have all things 0073they will ask. If I can find out any of these, ego et magister 0074meus erimus in tuto, I and my master shall be friends. He 0075is resolved to weep some three or four pailfuls to avoid 0076the rheum of love that wambleth in his stomach.

Enter the watch [two Watchmen and the Constable].

SAMIAS

0077Shall we never see thy master, Dares?

DARES

0078Yet, let us go now, for tomorrow Cynthia will be there.

EPITON

0079I will go with you. But how shall we see for the watch?

SAMIAS

0080Tush, let me alone. I'll begin to them. –Masters, 0081God speed you.

FIRST WATCHMAN

0082Sir boy, we are all sped ready.

EPITON

0083So methinks, for they 0084smell all of drink like a beggar's beard.

DARES

0085But I pray, sirs, may we see Endymion?

SECOND WATCHMAN

0086No, we are commanded in Cynthia's 0087name that no man shall see him.

SAMIAS

0088No man? Why, we are but boys.

FIRST WATCHMAN

0089[To his fellow watchmen] Mass, neighbours, 0090he says true. For if I swear I will never drink my liquor by 0091the quart, and yet call for two pints, I think with a safe 0092conscience I may carouse both.

DARES

0093Pithily, and to the purpose.

SECOND WATCHMAN

0094[To his fellow watchmen] Tush, tush, 0095neighbours, take me with you.

SAMIAS

0096This will grow hot.

DARES

0097Let them alone.

SECOND WATCHMAN

0098[To his fellow watchmen] If I say to my wife, 0099'Wife, I will have no raisins in my pudding', she puts in 0100currants. Small raisins are raisins, and boys are men. 0101Even as my wife should have put no raisins in my pudding, 0102so shall there no boys see Endymion.

DARES

0103Learnedly.

EPITON

0104Let Master Constable speak; I think he is the wisest 0105among you.

CONSTABLE

0106You know, neighbours, 'tis an old said saw, 'Children 0107and fools speak true.'

ALL

0108True.

CONSTABLE

0109Well, there you see the men be the fools, because it 0110is provided from the children.

DARES

0111Good.

CONSTABLE

0112Then say I, neighbours, that children must not see 0113Endymion, because children and fools speak true.

EPITON

0114O, wicked application!

SAMIAS

0115Scurvily brought about.

FIRST WATCHMAN

0116Nay, he says true; and therefore till Cynthia 0117have been here he shall not be uncovered. Therefore 0118away.

DARES

0119[Aside to Samias and Epiton] A watch, quoth you? A 0120man may watch seven years for a wise word and yet go 0121without it. Their wits are all as rusty as their bills. –But 0122come on, Master Constable, shall we have a song before 0123we go?

CONSTABLE

0124With all my heart.

Song.

WATCH

125Stand. Who goes there?
126We charge you appear
127'Fore our constable here.
128In the name of the Man in the Moon,
129To us billmen relate
130Why you stagger so late,
131And how you come drunk so soon.

PAGES

132What are ye, scabs?

WATCH

What are ye, scabs? The watch;
133This is the constable.

PAGES

This is the constable. A patch.

CONSTABLE

134Knock 'em down unless they all stand.
135If any run away,
136'Tis the old watchman's play
137To reach him a bill of his hand.

PAGES

138O gentlemen, hold.
139Your gowns freeze with cold,
140And your rotten teeth dance in your head.

EPITON

141Wine nothing shall cost ye,

SAMIAS

142Nor huge fires to roast ye.

DARES

143Then soberly let us be led.

CONSTABLE

144Come, my brown bills, we'll roar,
145Bounce loud at tavern door,

OMNES

146And I'th'morning steal all to bed.

Exeunt.

Actus Quartus, Scaena Tertia

[Enter] Corsites solus. [Endymion lies asleep on the lunary bank.]

CORSITES

0001I am come in sight of the lunary bank. Without doubt 0002Tellus doteth upon me; and cunningly, that I might not 0003perceive her love, she hath set me to a task that is done 0004before it is begun. Endymion, you must change your 0005pillow, and, if you be not weary of sleep, I will carry you 0006where at ease you shall sleep your fill. It were good that 0007without more ceremonies I took him, lest, being espied, I 0008be entrapped and so incur the displeasure of Cynthia, 0009who commonly setteth watch that Endymion have no 0010wrong. He lifts. 0011What now, is your mastership so heavy? Or are you 0012nailed to the ground? Not stir one whit? –Then use all 0013thy force, though he feel it and wake. –What, stone still? 0014Turned, I think, to earth, with lying so long on the earth. 0015Didst not thou, Corsites, before Cynthia pull up a tree 0016that forty years was fastened with roots and wreathed in 0017knots to the ground? Didst not thou with main force pull 0018open the iron gates which no ram or engine could move? 0019Have my weak thoughts made brawnfallen my strong 0020arms? Or is it the nature of love or the quintessence of the 0021mind to breed numbness, or litherness, or I know not 0022what languishing in my joints and sinews, being but the 0023base strings of my body? Or doth the remembrance of 0024Tellus so refine my spirits into a matter so subtle and 0025divine that the other fleshy parts cannot work whilst they 0026muse? Rest thyself, rest thyself; nay, rend thyself in 0027pieces, Corsites, and strive, in spite of love, fortune, and 0028nature, to lift up this dulled body, heavier than dead and 0029more senseless than death. Enter Fairies. 0030But what are these so fair fiends that cause my hairs to 0031stand upright and spirits to fall down? Hags –out, alas! 0032Nymphs, I crave pardon. Ay me, out! What do I here?

The fairies dance, and with a song pinch him, and he falleth asleep.

[Song.]

ALL

33Pinch him, pinch him, black and blue.
34Saucy mortals must not view
35What the Queen of Stars is doing,
36Nor pry into our fairy wooing.

FIRST FAIRY

37Pinch him blue

SECOND FAIRY

38And pinch him black.

THIRD FAIRY

39Let him not lack
40Sharp nails to pinch him blue and red,
41Till sleep has rocked his addle head.

FOURTH FAIRY

42For the trespass he hath done,
43Spots o'er all his flesh shall run.
44Kiss Endymion, kiss his eyes;
45Then to our midnight hay-de-guise.

They kiss Endymion and depart[, leaving him and the spotted Corsites asleep].

[Enter] Cynthia, Floscula, Semele, Panelion, Zontes, Pythagoras, [and] Gyptes.

Cynthia

0046You see, Pythagoras, what ridiculous opinions you 0047hold, and I doubt not but you are now of another mind.

PYTHAGORAS

0048Madam, I plainly receive that the perfection of 0049your brightness hath pierced through the thickness that 0050covered my mind, insomuch that I am no less glad to be 0051reformed than ashamed to remember my grossness.

GYPTES

0052They are thrice fortunate that live in your palace, 0053where truth is not in colours but life, virtues not in 0054imagination but execution.

CYNTHIA

0055I have always studied to have rather living virtues 0056than painted gods, the body of truth than the tomb. But 0057let us walk to Endymion; it may be it lieth in your arts to 0058deliver him. As for Eumenides, I fear he is dead.

PYTHAGORAS

0059I have alleged all the natural reasons I can for 0060such a long sleep.

GYPTES

0061I can do nothing till I see him.

CYNTHIA

0062Come, Floscula, I am sure you are glad that you 0063shall behold Endymion.

FLOSCULA

0064I were blessed if I might have him recovered.

CYNTHIA

0065Are you in love with his person?

FLOSCULA

0066No, but with his virtue.

CYNTHIA

0067What say you, Semele?

SEMELE

0068Madam, I dare say nothing for fear I offend.

CYNTHIA

0069Belike you cannot speak except you be spiteful. But 0070as good be silent as saucy. Panelion, what punishment 0071were fit for Semele, in whose speech and thoughts is only 0072contempt and sourness?

PANELION

0073I love not, madam, to give any judgement. Yet sith 0074Your Highness commandeth: I think, to commit her 0075tongue close prisoner to her mouth.

CYNTHIA

0076Agreed. Semele, if thou speak this twelvemonth, 0077thou shalt forfeit thy tongue. –Behold Endymion. Alas, 0078poor gentleman, hast thou spent thy youth in sleep, that 0079once vowed all to my service? Hollow eyes? Grey hairs? 0080Wrinkled cheeks? And decayed limbs? Is it destiny or 0081deceit that hath brought this to pass? If the first, who 0082could prevent thy wretched stars? If the latter, I would I 0083might know thy cruel enemy. I favoured thee, Endymion, 0084for thy honour, thy virtues, thy affections; but to bring thy 0085thoughts within the compass of thy fortunes, I have 0086seemed strange, that I might have thee stayed. And now 0087are thy days ended before my favour begin. But whom 0088have we here? Is it not Corsites?

ZONTES

0089It is, but more like a leopard than a man.

CYNTHIA

0090Awake him. [Corsites is awakened.] 0091How now, Corsites, what make you here? How came you 0092deformed? Look on thy hands, and then thou seest the 0093picture of thy face.

CORSITES

0094Miserable wretch, and accursed! How am I deluded? 0095Madam, I ask pardon for my offence, and you see my 0096fortune deserveth pity.

CYNTHIA

0097Speak on. Thy offence cannot deserve greater punishment; 0098but see thou rehearse the truth, else shalt thou 0099not find me as thou wishest me.

CORSITES

0100Madam, as it is no offence to be in love, being a man 0101mortal, so I hope can it be no shame to tell with whom, 0102my lady being heavenly. Your Majesty committed to my 0103charge fair Tellus, whose beauty in the same moment 0104took my heart captive that I undertook to carry her body 0105prisoner. Since that time have I found such combats in 0106my thoughts between love and duty, reverence and affection, 0107that I could neither endure the conflict nor hope for 0108the conquest.

CYNTHIA

0109In love? A thing far unfitting the name of a captain, 0110and, as I thought, the tough and unsmoothed nature of 0111Corsites. But forth.

CORSITES

0112Feeling this continual war, I thought rather by parley 0113to yield than by certain danger to perish. I unfolded to 0114Tellus the depth of my affections, and framed my tongue 0115to utter a sweet tale of love, that was wont to sound 0116nothing but threats of love. She, too fair to be true and too 0117false for one so fair, after a nice denial practised a notable 0118deceit, commanding me to remove Endymion from this 0119cabin and carry him to some dark cave, which I, seeking 0120to accomplish, found impossible, and so by fairies or 0121fiends have been thus handled.

CYNTHIA

0122How say you, my lords, is not Tellus always practising 0123of some deceits? –In sooth, Corsites, thy face is now 0124too foul for a lover and thine heart too fond for a soldier. 0125You may see, when warriors become wantons, how their 0126manners alter with their faces. Is it not a shame, Corsites, 0127that, having lived so long in Mars his camp, thou shouldst 0128now be rocked in Venus' cradle? Dost thou wear Cupid's 0129quiver at thy girdle, and make lances of looks? Well, 0130Corsites, rouse thyself and be as thou hast been, and let 0131Tellus, who is made all of love, melt herself in her own 0132looseness.

CORSITES

0133Madam, I doubt not but to recover my former state, 0134for Tellus' beauty never wrought such love in my mind as 0135now her deceit hath despite; and yet to be revenged of a 0136woman were a thing than love itself more womanish.

GYPTES

0137These spots, gentleman, are to be worn out if you rub 0138them over with this lunary, so that in place where you 0139received this maim you shall find a medicine.

CORSITES

0140I thank you for that. The gods bless me from love 0141and these pretty ladies that haunt this green!

FLOSCULA

0142Corsites, I would Tellus saw your amiable face.

[Corsites rubs out his spots with lunary from the bank.

Semele laughs.]

ZONTES

0143How spitefully Semele laugheth, that dare not speak!

CYNTHIA

0144Could you not stir Endymion with that doubled 0145strength of yours?

CORSITES

0146Not so much as his finger with all my force.

CYNTHIA

0147Pythagoras and Gyptes, what think you of 0148Endymion? What reason is to be given, what remedy?

PYTHAGORAS

0149Madam, it is impossible to yield reason for things 0150that happen not in compass of nature. It is most certain 0151that some strange enchantment hath bound all his senses.

CYNTHIA

0152What say you, Gyptes?

GYPTES

0153With Pythagoras, that it is enchantment, and that so 0154strange that no art can undo it, for that heaviness argueth 0155a malice unremovable in the enchantress, and that no 0156power can end it till she die that did it or the heavens 0157show some means more than miraculous.

FLOSCULA

0158O Endymion, could spite itself a mischief so 0159monstrous as to make thee dead with life, and living 0160being altogether dead? Where others number their years, 0161their hours, their minutes, and step to age by stairs, thou 0162only hast thy years and times in a cluster, being old before 0163thou rememberest thou wast young.

CYNTHIA

0164No more, Floscula; pity doth him no good. I would 0165anything else might, and I vow by the unspotted honour 0166of a lady he should not miss it. But is this all, Gyptes, that 0167is to be done?

GYPTES

0168All as yet. It may be that either the enchantress shall 0169die or else be discovered. If either happen I will then 0170practise the utmost of my art. In the mean season, about 0171this grove would I have a watch, and the first living thing 0172that toucheth Endymion to be taken.

CYNTHIA

0173Corsites, what say you, will you undertake this?

CORSITES

0174Good madam, pardon me; I was overtaken too late. 0175I should rather break into the midst of a main battle than 0176again fall into the hands of those fair babies.

CYNTHIA

0177Well, I will provide others. Pythagoras and Gyptes, 0178you shall yet remain in my court till I hear what may be 0179done in this matter.

PYTHAGORAS

0180We attend.

CYNTHIA

0181Let us go in.

Exeunt. [Endymion continues asleep on his lunary bed, near a tree, but perhaps curtained off during the entr'acte music.]
