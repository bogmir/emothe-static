John Lyly

Endymion

Act I

Actus Primus, Scaena Prima

[Enter] Endymion [and] Eumenides.

ENDYMION

0001I find, Eumenides, in all things both variety to 0002content and satiety to glut, saving only in my affections, 0003which are so stayed, and withal so stately, that I can 0004neither satisfy my heart with love nor mine eyes with 0005wonder. My thoughts, Eumenides, are stitched to the 0006stars, which, being as high as I can see, thou mayst 0007imagine how much higher they are than I can reach.

EUMENIDES

0008If you be enamoured of anything above the moon, 0009your thoughts are ridiculous, for that things immortal are 0010not subject to affections; if allured or enchanted with 0011these transitory things under the moon, you show yourself 0012senseless to attribute such lofty titles to such low 0013trifles.

ENDYMION

0014My love is placed neither under the moon nor 0015above.

EUMENIDES

0016I hope you be not sotted upon the man in the 0017moon.

ENDYMION

0018No, but settled either to die or possess the moon 0019herself.

EUMENIDES

0020Is Endymion mad, or do I mistake? Do you love 0021the moon, Endymion?

ENDYMION

0022Eumenides, the moon.

EUMENIDES

0023There was never any so peevish to imagine the 0024moon either capable of affection or shape of a mistress; 0025for as impossible it is to make love fit to her humour, 0026which no man knoweth, as a coat to her form, which 0027continueth not in one bigness whilst she is measuring. 0028Cease off, Endymion, to feed so much upon fancies. That 0029melancholy blood must be purged which draweth you to 0030a dotage no less miserable than monstrous.

ENDYMION

0031My thoughts have no veins, and yet, unless they be 0032let blood, I shall perish.

EUMENIDES

0033But they have vanities, which being reformed, you 0034may be restored.

ENDYMION

0035O fair Cynthia, why do others term thee unconstant 0036whom I have ever found unmovable? Injurious 0037time, corrupt manners, unkind men, who, finding a constancy 0038not to be matched in my sweet mistress, have 0039christened her with the name of wavering, waxing, and 0040waning! Is she inconstant that keepeth a settled course, 0041which since her first creation altereth not one minute in 0042her moving? There is nothing thought more admirable or 0043commendable in the sea than the ebbing and flowing; and 0044shall the moon, from whom the sea taketh this virtue, be 0045accounted fickle for increasing and decreasing? Flowers 0046in their buds are nothing worth till they be blown, nor 0047blossoms accounted till they be ripe fruit; and shall we 0048then say they be changeable for that they grow from seeds 0049to leaves, from leaves to buds, from buds to their perfection? 0050Then why be not twigs that become trees, children 0051that become men, and mornings that grow to evenings 0052termed wavering, for that they continue not at one stay? 0053Ay, but Cynthia, being in her fullness, decayeth, as not 0054delighting in her greatest beauty, or withering when she 0055should be most honoured. When malice cannot object 0056anything, folly will, making that a vice which is the greatest 0057virtue. What thing, my mistress excepted, being in the 0058pride of her beauty and latter minute of her age, that 0059waxeth young again? Tell me, Eumenides, what is he 0060that, having a mistress of ripe years and infinite virtues, 0061great honours and unspeakable beauty, but would wish 0062that she might grow tender again, getting youth by years 0063and never-decaying beauty by time, whose fair face 0064neither the summer's blaze can scorch nor winter's blast 0065chap, nor the numbering of years breed altering of 0066colours? Such is my sweet Cynthia, whom time cannot 0067touch because she is divine, nor will offend because she is 0068delicate. O Cynthia, if thou shouldst always continue at 0069thy fullness, both gods and men would conspire to ravish 0070thee. But thou, to abate the pride of our affections, dost 0071detract from thy perfections, thinking it sufficient if once 0072in a month we enjoy a glimpse of thy majesty; and then, 0073to increase our griefs, thou dost decrease thy gleams, 0074coming out of thy royal robes wherewith thou dazzlest 0075our eyes, down into thy swath clouts, beguiling our eyes. 0076And then–

EUMENIDES

0077Stay there, Endymion. Thou that committest 0078idolatry wilt straight blaspheme if thou be suffered. Sleep 0079would do thee more good than speech. The moon 0080heareth thee not, or, if she do, regardeth thee not.

ENDYMION

0081Vain Eumenides, whose thoughts never grow 0082higher than the crown of thy head! Why troublest thou 0083me, having neither head to conceive the cause of my love 0084or a heart to receive the impressions? Follow thou thine 0085own fortunes, which creep on the earth, and suffer me to 0086fly to mine, whose fall, though it be desperate, yet shall it 0087come by daring. Farewell.

[Exit.]

EUMENIDES

0088Without doubt Endymion is bewitched; 0089otherwise in a man of such rare virtues there could not harbour a 0090mind of such extreme madness. I will follow him, lest in 0091this fancy of the moon he deprive himself of the sight of 0092the sun.

Exit.

Actus Primus, Scaena Secunda

[Enter] Tellus [and] Floscula

TELLUS

0001Treacherous and most perjured Endymion, is Cynthia 0002the sweetness of thy life and the bitterness of my death? 0003What revenge may be devised so full of shame as my 0004thoughts are replenished with malice? Tell me, Floscula, 0005if falseness in love can possibly be punished with extremity 0006of hate. As long as sword, fire, or poison may be 0007hired, no traitor to my love shall live unrevenged. Were 0008thy oaths without number, thy kisses without measure, 0009thy sighs without end, forged to deceive a poor credulous 0010virgin, whose simplicity had been worth thy favour and 0011better fortune? If the gods sit unequal beholders of injuries, 0012or laughers at lovers' deceits, then let mischief be as 0013well forgiven in women as perjury winked at in men.

FLOSCULA

0014Madam, if you would compare the state of Cynthia 0015with your own, and the height of Endymion his thoughts 0016with the meanness of your fortune, you would rather 0017yield than contend, being between you and her no comparison, 0018and rather wonder than rage at the greatness of 0019his mind, being affected with a thing more than mortal.

TELLUS

0020No comparison, Floscula? And why so? Is not my 0021beauty divine, whose body is decked with fair flowers, 0022and veins are vines, yielding sweet liquor to the dullest 0023spirits, whose ears are corn to bring strength, and whose 0024hairs are grass to bring abundance? Doth not frankincense 0025and myrrh breathe out of my nostrils, and all the 0026sacrifice of the gods breed in my bowels? Infinite are my 0027creatures, without which neither thou nor Endymion nor 0028any could love or live.

FLOSCULA

0029But know you not, fair lady, that Cynthia governeth 0030all things? Your grapes would be but dry husks, your corn 0031but chaff, and all your virtues vain, were it not Cynthia 0032that preserveth the one in the bud and nourisheth the 0033other in the blade, and by her influence both comforteth 0034all things and by her authority commandeth all creatures. 0035Suffer then Endymion to follow his affections, though to 0036obtain her be impossible, and let him flatter himself in his 0037own imaginations, because they are immortal.

TELLUS

0038Loath I am, Endymion, thou shouldst die, because I 0039love thee well, and that thou shouldst live it grieveth me, 0040because thou lovest Cynthia too well. In these extremities 0041what shall I do? Floscula, no more words. I am resolved: 0042he shall neither live or die.

FLOSCULA

0043A strange practice, if it be possible.

TELLUS

0044Yes, I will entangle him in such a sweet net that he 0045shall neither find the means to come out, nor desire it. All 0046allurements of pleasure will I cast before his eyes, insomuch 0047that he shall slake that love which he now woweth 0048to Cynthia, and burn in mine, of which he seemeth 0049careless. In this languishing between my amorous devices 0050and his own loose desires, there shall such dissolute 0051thoughts take root in his head, and over his heart grow so 0052thick a skin, that neither hope or preferment, nor fear of 0053punishment, nor counsel of the wisest, nor company of 0054the worthiest shall alter his humour nor make him once to 0055think of his honour.

FLOSCULA

0056A revenge incredible, and if it may be, unnatural.

TELLUS

0057He shall know the malice of a woman to have neither 0058mean nor end, and of a woman deluded in love to have 0059neither rule nor reason. I can do it, I must, I will. All his 0060virtues will I shadow with vices; his person –ah, sweet 0061person!– shall he deck with such rich robes as he shall 0062forget it is own person; his sharp wit –ah, wit too 0063sharp, that hath cut off all my joys!– shall he use in 0064flattering of my face and devising sonnets in my favour. 0065The prime of his youth and pride of his time shall be 0066spent in melancholy passions, careless behaviour, untamed 0067thoughts, and unbridled affections.

FLOSCULA

0068When this is done, what then? Shall it continue till 0069his death, or shall he dote for ever in this delight?

TELLUS

0070Ah, Floscula, thou rendest my heart in sunder, in 0071putting me in remembrance of the end.

FLOSCULA

0072Why, if this be not the end, all the rest is to no end.

TELLUS

0073Yet suffer me to imitate Juno, who would turn 0074Jupiter's lovers to beasts on the earth, though she knew 0075afterwards they should be stars in heaven.

FLOSCULA

0076Affection that is bred by enchantment is like a flower 0077that is wrought in silk: in colour and form most like, but 0078nothing at all in substance or savour.

TELLUS

0079It shall suffice me, if the world talk, that I am favoured 0080of Endymion.

FLOSCULA

0081Well, use your own will, but you shall find that love 0082gotten with witchcraft is as unpleasant as fish taken with 0083medicines unwholesome.

TELLUS

0084Floscula, they that be so poor that they have neither 0085net nor hook will rather poison dough than pine with 0086hunger; and she that is so oppressed with love that she is 0087neither able with beauty nor wit to obtain her friend will 0088rather use unlawful means than try untolerable pains. I 0089will do it.

Exit.

FLOSCULA

0090Then about it. Poor Endymion, what traps are laid 0091for thee because thou honourest one that all the world 0092wondereth at! And what plots are cast to make thee 0093unfortunate that studiest of all men to be the faithfullest!

Exit.

Actus Primus, Scaena Tertia

[Enter] Dares [and] Samias.

DARES

0001Now our masters are in love up to the ears, what have 0002we to do but to be in knavery up to the crowns?

SAMIAS

0003O, that we had Sir Tophas, that brave squire, in the 0004midst of our mirth –and ecce autem, will you see the devil?

Enter Sir Tophas, [ridiculously armed and accoutred, and] Epiton.

TOPHAS

0005Epi!

EPITON

0006Here, sir.

TOPHAS

0007I brook not this idle humour of love. It tickleth not 0008my liver, from whence the love-mongers in former age 0009seemed to infer they should proceed.

EPITON

0010Love, sir, may lie in your lungs, and I think it doth, 0011and that is the cause you blow and are so pursy.

TOPHAS

0012Tush, boy, I think it but some device of the poet to 0013get money.

EPITON

0014A poet? What's that?

TOPHAS

0015Dost thou not know what a poet is?

EPITON

0016No.

TOPHAS

0017Why, fool, a poet is as much as one should say, a 0018poet. [Discovering Dares and Samias] But soft, yonder be 0019two wrens. Shall I shoot at them?

EPITON

0020They are two lads.

TOPHAS

0021Larks or wrens, I will kill them.

EPITON

0022Larks? Are you blind? They are two little boys.

TOPHAS

0023Birds or boys, they are both but a pittance for my 0024breakfast. Therefore have at them, for their brains must, 0025as it were, embroider my bolts.

[He takes aim at Samias and Dares.]

SAMIAS

0026[To Sir Tophas] Stay your courage, valiant knight, for 0027your wisdom is so weary that it stayeth itself.

DARES

0028Why, Sir Tophas, have you forgotten your old friends?

TOPHAS

0029Friends? Nego argumentum.

SAMIAS

0030And why not friends?

TOPHAS

0031Because amicitia, as in old annuals we find, is inter 0032pares. Now, my pretty companions, you shall see how 0033unequal you be to me. But I will not cut you quite off; 0034you shall be my half friends, for, reaching to my middle, 0035so far as from the ground to the waist I will be your 0036friend.

DARES

0037Learnedly. But what shall become of the rest of your 0038body, from the waist to the crown?

TOPHAS

0039My children, quod supra vos nihil ad vos, you must 0040think the rest immortal because you cannot reach it.

EPITON

0041[To Samias and Dares] Nay, I tell ye, my master is 0042more than a man.

DARES

0043[To Epiton] And thou less than a mouse.

TOPHAS

0044But what be you two?

SAMIAS

0045I am Samias, page to Eumenides.

DARES

0046And I Dares, page to Endymion.

TOPHAS

0047Of what occupation are your masters?

DARES

0048Occupation, you clown? Why, they are honourable, 0049and warriors.

TOPHAS

0050Then are they my prentices.

DARES

0051Thine? And why so?

TOPHAS

0052I was the first to that ever desired war, and therefore by 0053Mars himself given me for my arms a whole armoury, and 0054thus I go as you see, clothed with artillery. It is not silks 0055(milksops), nor tissues, not the fine wool of Seres, but 0056iron, steel, swords, flame, shot, terror, clamour, blood, 0057and ruin, that rocks asleep my thoughts, which never had 0058any other cradle but cruelty. Let me see, do you not 0059bleed?

DARES

0060Why so?

TOPHAS

0061Commonly my words wound.

SAMIAS

0062What then do your blows?

TOPHAS

0063Not only wound, but also confound.

SAMIAS

0064[To Epiton] How dar'st thou come so near thy master, 0065Epi? –Sir Tophas, spare us.

TOPHAS

0066You shall live. You, Samias, because you are little; 0067you Dares, because you are no bigger; and both of you, 0068because you are but two; for commonly I kill by the 0069dozen, and have for every particular adversary a peculiar 0070weapon.

[He displays his armoury.]

SAMIAS

0071May we know the use, for our better skill in war?

TOPHAS

0072You shall. He is a birdbolt for the ugly beast, the 0073blackbird.

DARES

0074A cruel sight.

TOPHAS

0075Here is the musket for the untamed, or, as the vulgar 0076sort term it, the wild mallard.

[He demonstrates, not heeding their talk.]

SAMIAS

0077O desperate attempt!

EPITON

0078Nay, my master will match them.

DARES

0079Ay, if he catch them.

TOPHAS

0080Here is spear and shield, and both necessary, the one 0081to conquer, the other to subdue or overcome the terrible 0082trout, which, although he be under the water, yet, tying a 0083string to the top of my spear and an engine of iron to the 0084end of my line, I overthrow him, and then herein I put 0085him.

[He shows his gear and struts about, oblivious to their talk.]

SAMIAS

0086O wonderful war! Dares, didst thou ever hear such a 0087dolt?

DARES

0088All the better. We shall have good sport hereafter if we 0089can get leisure.

SAMIAS

0090Leisure? I will rather lose my master's service than his 0091company. Look how he struts. [To Tophas] But what is 0092this? Call you it your sword?

TOPHAS

0093No, it is my scimitar, which I, by construction often 0094studying to be compendious, call my smiter.

DARES

0095What, are you also learned, sir?

TOPHAS

0096Learned? I am all Mars and Ars.

SAMIAS

0097Nay, you are all mass and ass.

TOPHAS

0098Mock you me? You shall both suffer, yet with such 0099weapons as you shall make choice of the weapon wherewith 0100you shall perish. Am I all a mass or lump? Is there no 0101proportion in me? Am I all ass? Is there no wit in me? – 0102Epi, prepare them to the slaughter.

SAMIAS

0103I pray, sir, hear us speak. We call you 'mass', which 0104your learning doth well understand is all 'man', for mas, 0105maris is a man. Then 'as', as you know, is a weight, and 0106we for your virtues account you a weight.

TOPHAS

0107The Latin hath saved your lives, the which a world of 0108silver could not have ransomed. I understand you 0109and pardon you.

DARES

0110Well, Sir Tophas, we bid you farewell, and at our next 0111meeting we will be ready to do you service.

TOPHAS

0112Samias, I thank you. Dares, I thank you. But 0113especially I thank you both.

SAMIAS

0114Wisely! Come, next time we'll have 0115some pretty gentlewomen with us to walk, for without 0116doubt with them he will be very dainty.

DARES

0117Come, let us see what our masters do. It 0118is high time.

Exeunt [Dares and Samias].

TOPHAS

0119Now will I march into the field, where, if I cannot 0120encounter with my foul enemies, I will withdraw myself 0121to the river and there fortify for fish; for there resteth 0122no minute free from fight.

Exit [Sir Tophas with Epiton].

Actus Primus, Scaena Quarta

[Enter] Tellus [and] Floscula [at one door, and] Dipsas [at another].

TELLUS

0001Behold, Floscula, we have met with the woman by 0002chance that we sought for by travail. I will break my mind 0003to her without ceremony or circumstance, lest we lose 0004that time in advice that should be spent in execution.

FLOSCULA

0005Use your discretion. I will in this case neither give 0006counsel nor consent, for there cannot be a thing more 0007monstrous than to force affection by sorcery, neither do I 0008imagine anything more impossible.

TELLUS

0009Tush, Floscula, in obtaining of love what impossibilities 0010will I not try? And for the winning of 0011Endymion what impieties will I not practise? –Dipsas, 0012whom as many honour for age as wonder at for cunning, 0013listen in few words to my tale and answer in one word to 0014the purpose, for that neither my burning desire can afford 0015long speech nor the short time I have to stay many delays. 0016Is it possible by herbs, stones, spells, incantation, enchantment, 0017exorcism, fire, metals, planets, or any practice, 0018to plant affection where it is not and to supplant it 0019where it is?

DIPSAS

0020Fair lady, you may imagine that there hoary hairs are 0021not void of experience, nor the great name that goeth of 0022my cunning to be without cause. I can darken the sun by 0023my skill and remove the moon out of her course; I can 0024restore youth to the aged and make hills without bottoms. 0025There is nothing that I cannot do but that only which you 0026would have me do, and therein I differ from the gods, 0027that I am not able to rule hearts; for, were it in my power 0028to place affection by appointment, I would make such evil 0029appetites, such inordinate lusts, such cursed desires, as all 0030the world should be filled both with superstitious heats 0031and extreme love.

TELLUS

0032Unhappy Tellus, whose desires are so desperate that 0033they are neither to be conceived of any creature nor to be 0034cured by any art!

DIPSAS

0035This I can: breed slackness in love, though never root 0036it out. What is he whom you love, and what she that he 0037honoureth?

TELLUS

0038Endymion, sweet Endymion, is he that hath my heart; 0039and Cynthia, too too fair Cynthia, the miracle of nature, 0040of time, of fortune, is the lady that he delights in, and 0041dotes on every day, and dies for ten thousand times a day.

DIPSAS

0042Would you have his love, either by absence or sickness, 0043aslaked? Would you that Cynthia should mistrust 0044him, or be jealous of him without colour?

TELLUS

0045It is the only thing I crave, that, seeing my love to 0046Endymion, unspotted, cannot be accepted, his truth to 0047Cynthia, though it be unspeakable, may be suspected.

DIPSAS

0048I will undertake it and overtake him, that all his love 0049shall be doubted of and therefore become desperate. But 0050this will wear out with time, that treadeth all things down 0051but truth.

TELLUS

0052Let us go.

DIPSAS

0053I follow.

Exeunt.

Act II

Actus Secundus, Scaena Prima

[Enter] Endymion

ENDYMION

0001O fair Cynthia, O unfortunate Endymion! Why 0002was not thy birth as high as thy thoughts, or her beauty 0003less than heavenly? Or why are not thine honours as rare 0004as her beauty? Or thy fortunes as great as thy deserts? 0005Sweet Cynthia, how wouldst thou be pleased, how possessed? 0006Will labours, patient of all extremities, obtain thy 0007love? There is no mountain so steep that I will not climb, 0008no monster so cruel that I will not tame, no action so 0009desperate that I will not attempt. Desirest thou the 0010passions of love, the sad and melancholy moods of perplexed 0011minds, the not-to-be-expressed torments of 0012racked thoughts? Behold my sad tears, my deep sighs, my 0013hollow eyes, my broken sleeps, my heavy countenance. 0014Wouldst thou have me vowed only to thy beauty and 0015consume every minute of time in thy service? Remember 0016my solitary life, almost these seven years. Whom have I 0017entertained but mine own thoughts and thy virtues? What 0018company have I used but contemplations? Whom have I 0019wondered at but thee? Nay, whom have I not contemned 0020for thee? Have I not crept to those on whom I might have 0021trodden, only because thou didst shine upon them? Have 0022not injuries been sweet to me if thou vouchsafedst I 0023should bear them? Have I not spent my golden years in 0024hopes, waxing old with wishing, yet wishing nothing but 0025thy love? With Tellus, fair Tellus, have I dissembled, 0026using her but as a cloak for mine affections, that others, 0027seeing my mangled and disordered mind, might think it 0028were for one that loveth me, not for Cynthia, whose 0029perfection alloweth no companion nor comparison. 0030In the midst of these distempered thoughts of mine, 0031thou art not only jealous of my truth, but careless, suspicious, 0032and secure, which strange humour maketh my 0033mind as desperate as thy conceits are doubtful. I am none 0034of those wolves that bark most when thou shinest brightest, 0035but that fish –thy fish, Cynthia, in the flood Araris– 0036which at thy waxing is as white as the driven snow and at 0037thy waning as black as deepest darkness. I am that 0038Endymion, sweet Cynthia, that have carried my thoughts 0039in equal balance with my actions, being always as free 0040from imagining ill as enterprising; that Endymion whose 0041eyes never esteemed anything fair but thy face, whose 0042tongue termed nothing rare but thy virtues, and whose 0043heart imagined nothing miraculous but thy government; 0044yea, that Endymion who, divorcing himself from the 0045amiableness of all ladies, the bravery of all courts, the 0046company of all men, hath chosen in a solitary cell to live 0047only by feeding on thy favour, accounting in the world 0048(but thyself) nothing excellent, nothing immortal. Thus 0049mayst thou see every vein, sinew, muscle, and artery of 0050my love, in which there is no flattery nor deceit, error nor 0051art. But soft, here cometh Tellus. I must turn my other 0052face to her like Janus, lest she be as suspicious as Juno.

Enter Tellus, [Floscula, and Dipsas].

TELLUS

0053Yonder I espy Endymion. I will seem to suspect 0054nothing, but soothe him, that, seeing I cannot obtain the 0055depth of his love, I may learn the height of his dissembling. 0056Floscula and Dipsas, withdraw yourselves out 0057of our sight, yet be within the hearing of our saluting. [Floscula and Dipsas withdraw.] 0058How now, Endymion, always solitary? No company but 0059your own thoughts? No friend but melancholy fancies?

ENDYMION

0060You know, fair Tellus, that the sweet remembrance 0061of your love is the only companion of my life, and 0062thy presence my paradise, so that I am not alone when 0063nobody is with me, and in heaven itself when thou art 0064with me.

TELLUS

0065Then you love me, Endymion?

ENDYMION

0066Or else I live not, Tellus.

TELLUS

0067Is it not possible for you, Endymion, to dissemble?

ENDYMION

0068Not, Tellus, unless I could make me a woman.

TELLUS

0069Why, is dissembling joined to their sex inseparable, as 0070heat to fire, heaviness to earth, moisture to water, thinness 0071to air?

ENDYMION

0072No, but found in their sex as common as spots 0073upon doves, moles upon faces, caterpillars upon sweet 0074apples, cobwebs upon fair windows.

TELLUS

0075Do they all dissemble?

ENDYMION

0076All but one.

TELLUS

0077Who is that?

ENDYMION

0078I dare not tell. For If I should say you, then would 0079you imagine my flattery to be extreme; if another, then 0080would you think my love to be but indifferent.

TELLUS

0081You will be sure I shall take no vantage of your words. 0082But in sooth, Endymion, without more ceremonies, is it 0083not Cynthia?

ENDYMION

0084You know, Tellus, that of the gods we are 0085forbidden to dispute, because their deities come not 0086within the compass of our reasons; and of Cynthia we are 0087allowed not to talk but to wonder, because her virtues are 0088not within the reach of our capacities.

TELLUS

0089Why, she is but a woman.

ENDYMION

0090No more was Venus.

TELLUS

0091She is but a virgin.

ENDYMION

0092No more was Vesta.

TELLUS

0093She shall have an end.

ENDYMION

0094So shall the world.

TELLUS

0095Is not her beauty subject to time?

ENDYMION

0096No more than time is to standing still.

TELLUS

0097Wilt thou make her immortal?

ENDYMION

0098No, but incomparable.

TELLUS

0099Take heed, Endymion, lest, like the wrestler in 0100Olympia that, striving to lift an impossible weight, 0101catched an incurable strain, thou, by fixing thy thoughts 0102above thy reach, fall into a disease without all recure. But 0103I see thou art now in love with Cynthia.

ENDYMION

0104No, Tellus. Thou knowest that the stately cedar, 0105whose top reacheth unto the clouds, never boweth his 0106head to the shrubs that grow in the valley; nor ivy that 0107climbeth up by the elm can ever get hold of the beams of 0108the sun. Cynthia I honour in all humility, whom none 0109ought or dare adventure to love, whose affections are 0110immortal and virtues infinite. Suffer me therefore to gaze 0111on the moon, at whom, were it not for thyself, I would die 0112with wondering.

Exeunt.

Actus Secundus, Scaena Secunda

[Enter] Dares, Samias, Scintilla, [and] Favilla.

DARES

0001Come, Samias, didst thou ever hear such a sighing, the 0002one for Cynthia, the other for Semele, and both for 0003moonshine in the water?

SAMIAS

0004Let them sigh, and let us sing. –How say you, gentlewomen, 0005are not our masters too far in love?

SCINTILLA

0006Their tongues haply are dipped to the root in amorous 0007words and sweet discourses, but I think their hearts 0008are scarce tipped on the side with constant desires.

DARES

0009How say you, Favilla, is not love a lurcher, that taketh 0010men's stomachs away that they cannot eat, their spleen 0011that they cannot laugh, their hearts that they cannot fight, 0012their eyes that they cannot sleep, and leaveth nothing but 0013livers to make nothing but lovers?

FAVILLA

0014Away, peevish boy! A rod were better under thy 0015girdle than love in thy mouth. It will be a forward cock 0016that croweth in the shell.
