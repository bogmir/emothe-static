Actus Quintus, Scaena Prima

[Enter] Samias [and] Dares.

SAMIAS

0001Eumenides hath told such strange tales as I may well 0002wonder at them but never believe them.

DARES

0003The other old man, what a sad speech used he, that 0004caused us almost all to weep! Cynthia is so desirous to 0005know the experiment of her own virtue, and so willing to 0006ease Endymion's hard fortune, that she no sooner heard 0007the discourse but she made herself in a readiness to try 0008the event.

SAMIAS

0009We will also see the event. But whist! Here cometh 0010Cynthia with all her train. Let us sneak in amongst them.

Enter Cynthia, Floscula, Semele, Panelion etc

[Eumenides, Zontes, Gyptes, and Pythagoras. Samias and Dares join the throng.]

CYNTHIA

0011Eumenides, it cannot sink into my head that I 0012should be signified by that sacred fountain, for many 0013things are there in the world to which those words may be 0014applied.

EUMENIDES

0015Good madam, vouchsafe but to try, else shall I 0016think myself most unhappy that I asked not my sweet 0017mistress.

CYNTHIA

0018Will you not yet tell me her name?

EUMENIDES

0019Pardon me, good madam, for if Endymion awake 0020he shall. Myself have sworn never to reveal it.

CYNTHIA

0021Well, let us to Endymion. [They approach the sleeping Endymion.] 0022I will not be so stately, good Endymion, not 0023to stoop to do thee good; and if thy liberty consist in a kiss 0024from me, thou shalt have it. And although my mouth 0025hath been heretofore as untouched as my thoughts, yet 0026now to recover thy life (though to restore thy youth it be 0027impossible) I will do that to Endymion which yet never 0028mortal man could boast of heretofore, nor shall ever hope 0029for hereafter.

She kisseth him.

EUMENIDES

0030Madam, he beginneth to stir.

CYNTHIA

0031Soft, Eumenides. Stand still.

EUMENIDES

0032Ah, I see his eyes almost open.

CYNTHIA

0033I command thee once again, stir not. I will stand 0034behind him.

[She stands where Endymion will not see her at first.]

PANELION

0035What do I see, Endymion almost awake?

EUMENIDES

0036Endymion, Endymion, art thou deaf or dumb? Or 0037hath this long sleep taken away thy memory? Ah, my 0038sweet Endymion, seest thou not Eumenides, thy faithful 0039friend, thy faithful Eumenides, who for thy safety hath 0040been careless of his own content? Speak, Endymion, 0041Endymion, Endymion!

ENDYMION

0042Endymion? I call to mind such a name.

EUMENIDES

0043Hast thou forgotten thyself, Endymion? Then 0044do I not marvel thou rememberest not thy friend. I tell 0045thee thou art Endymion and I Eumenides. Behold also 0046Cynthia, by whose favour thou art awaked, and by whose 0047virtue thou shalt continue thy natural course.

CYNTHIA

0048Endymion, speak, sweet Endymion. Knowest thou 0049not Cynthia?

ENDYMION

0050O heavens, whom do I behold? Fair Cynthia, divine 0051Cynthia?

CYNTHIA

0052I am Cynthia, and thou Endymion.

ENDYMION

0053Endymion? What do I hear? What, a grey beard? 0054Hollow eyes? Withered body? Decayed limbs? And all in 0055one night?

EUMENIDES

0056One night? Thou hast here slept forty years, by 0057what enchantress as yet it is not known. And behold, the 0058twig to which thou laidst thy head is now become a tree. 0059Callest thou not Eumenides to remembrance?

ENDYMION

0060Thy name I do remember by the sound, but thy 0061favour I do not yet call to mind. Only divine Cynthia, to 0062whom time, fortune, destiny, and death are subject, I 0063see and remember, and in all humility I regard and reverence.

CYNTHIA

0064You have good cause to remember Eumenides, who 0065hath for thy safety forsaken his own solace.

ENDYMION

0066Am I that Endymion who was wont in court to 0067lead my life, and in jousts, tourneys, and arms to exercise 0068my youth? Am I that Endymion?

EUMENIDES

0069Thou art that Endymion and I Eumenides. Wilt 0070thou not yet call me to remembrance?

ENDYMION

0071Ah, sweet Eumenides, I now perceive thou art he, 0072and that myself have the name of Endymion. But that this 0073should be my body I doubt; for how could my curled 0074locks be turned to grey hairs and my strong body to a 0075dying weakness, having waxed old and not knowing it?

CYNTHIA

0076Well, Endymion, arise. A while sit down, for that thy 0077limbs are stiff and not able to stay thee, and tell what hast 0078thou seen in thy sleep all this while? What dreams, visions, 0079thoughts, and fortunes? For it is impossible but in 0080so long time thou shouldst see things strange.

ENDYMION

0081Fair Cynthia, I will rehearse what I have seen, 0082humbly desiring that when I exceed in length, you give 0083me warning, that I may end. For to utter all I have to 0084speak would be troublesome, although haply the strangeness 0085may somewhat abate the tediousness.

CYNTHIA

0086Well, Endymion, begin.

ENDYMION

0087Methought I saw a lady passing fair but very mischievous, 0088who in the one hand carried a knife with which 0089she offered to cut my throat, and in the other a looking 0090glass, wherein, seeing how ill anger became ladies, she 0091refrained from intended violence. She was accompanied 0092with other damsels, one of which, with a stern countenance, 0093and as it were with a settled malice engraven in her 0094eyes, provoked her to execute mischief. Another with 0095visage sad, and constant only in sorrow, with her arms 0096crossed, and watery eyes, seemed to lament my fortune, 0097but durst not offer to prevent the force. I started in my 0098sleep, feeling my very veins to swell and my sinews to 0099stretch with fear, and such a cold sweat bedewed all my 0100body that death itself could not be so terrible as the 0101vision.

CYNTHIA

0102A strange sight. Gyptes at our better leisure shall 0103expound it.

ENDYMION

0104After long debating with herself, mercy overcame 0105anger, and there appeared in her heavenly face such a 0106divine majesty, mingled with a sweet mildness, that I was 0107ravished with the sight above measure, and wished that I 0108might have enjoyed the sight without end. And so she 0109departed with the other ladies, of which the one retained 0110still an unmovable cruelty, the other a constant pity.

CYNTHIA

0111Poor Endymion, how wast thou affrighted! What 0112else?

ENDYMION

0113After her immediately appeared an aged man with 0114a beard as white as snow, carrying in his hand a book with 0115three leaves, and speaking, as I remember, these words: 0116'Endymion, receive this book with three leaves, in which 0117are contained counsels, policies, and pictures.' And with 0118that, he offered me the book, which I rejected; wherewith 0119moved with a disdainful pity, he rent the first leaf in a 0120thousand shivers. The second time he offered it, which I 0121refused also; at which, bending his brows and pitching his 0122eyes fast to the ground as though they were fixed to the 0123earth and not again to be removed, then suddenly casting 0124them up to the heavens, he tore in a rage the second leaf 0125and offered the book only with one leaf. I know not 0126whether fear to offend or desire to know some strange 0127thing moved me; I took the book, and so the old man 0128vanished.

CYNTHIA

0129What didst thou imagine was in the last leaf?

ENDYMION

0130There –ay, portrayed to life– with a cold quaking 0131in every joint, I beheld many wolves barking at thee, 0132Cynthia, who, having ground their teeth to bite, did with 0133striving bleed themselves to death. There might I see 0134Ingratitude with an hundred eyes, gazing for benefits, and 0135with a thousand teeth gnawing on the bowels wherein she 0136was bred. Treachery stood all clothed in white, with a 0137smiling countenance but both her hands bathed in blood. 0138Envy, with a pale and meagre face, whose body was so 0139lean that one might tell all her bones, and whose garment 0140was so tattered that it was easy to number every thread, 0141stood shooting at stars, whose darts fell down again on 0142her own face. There might I behold drones, or beetles, I 0143know not how to term them, creeping under the wings of 0144a princely eagle, who, being carried into her nest, sought 0145there to suck that vein that would have killed the eagle. I 0146mused that things so base should attempt a fact so barbarous 0147or durst imagine a thing so bloody. And many other 0148things, madam, the repetition whereof may at your better 0149leisure seem more pleasing; for bees surfeit sometimes 0150with honey, and the gods are glutted with harmony, and 0151Your Highness may be dulled with delight.

CYNTHIA

0152I am content to be dieted; therefore let us in. 0153Eumenides, see that Endymion be well tended, lest, either 0154eating immoderately or sleeping again too long, he 0155fall into a deadly surfeit or into his former sleep. See this 0156also be proclaimed: that whosoever will discover this 0157practice shall have of Cynthia infinite thanks and no small 0158rewards.

Exit, [attended by her courtly entourage. Floscula, Endymion, and Eumenides remain.]

FLOSCULA

0159Ah, Endymion, none so joyful as Floscula of thy 0160restoring!

EUMENIDES

0161Yes, Floscula, let Eumenides be somewhat gladder, 0162and do not that wrong to the settled friendship of a 0163man as to compare it with the light affection of a 0164woman. –Ah, my dear friend Endymion, suffer me to die 0165with gazing at thee!

ENDYMION

0166Eumenides, thy friendship is immortal and not to 0167be conceived, and thy good will, Floscula, better than I 0168have deserved. But let us all wait on Cynthia. I marvel 0169Semele speaketh not a word.

EUMENIDES

0170Because if she do she loseth her tongue.

ENDYMION

0171But how prospereth your love?

EUMENIDES

0172I never yet spake word since your sleep.

ENDYMION

0173I doubt not but your affection is old and your 0174appetite cold.

EUMENIDES

0175No, Endymion, thine hath made it stronger, and 0176now are my sparks grown to flames and my fancies almost 0177to frenzies. But let us follow, and within we will debate all 0178this matter at large.

Exeunt.

Actus Quintus, Scaena Secunda

[Enter] Sir Tophas [and] Epiton.

TOPHAS

0001Epi, love hath jostled my liberty from the wall and 0002taken the upper hand of my reason.

EPITON

0003Let me then trip up the heels of your affection and 0004thrust your good will into the gutter.

TOPHAS

0005No, Epi, love is a lord of misrule, and keepeth Christmas 0006in my corpse.

EPITON

0007No doubt there is good cheer. What dishes of delight 0008doth his lordship feast you withal?

TOPHAS

0009First, with a great platter of plum-porridge of pleasure, 0010wherein is stewed the mutton of mistrust.

EPITON

0011Excellent love-lap!

TOPHAS

0012Then cometh a pie of patience, a hen of honey, a 0013goose of gall, a capon of care, and many other viands, 0014some sweet and some sour, which proveth love to be as it 0015was said of in old years: dulce venenum.

EPITON

0016A brave banquet!

TOPHAS

0017But Epi, I pray thee feel on my chin; something 0018pricketh me. What dost thou feel or see?

EPITON

0019[Examining his chin] There are three or four little 0020hairs.

TOPHAS

0021I pray thee call it my beard. How shall I be troubled 0022when this young spring shall grow to a great wood!

EPITON

0023O, sir, your chin is but a quiller yet. You will be most 0024majestical when it is full fledge. But I marvel that you love 0025Dipsas, that old crone.

TOPHAS

0026Agnosco veteris vestigia flamma, I love the smoke of an 0027old fire.

EPITON

0028Why, she is so cold that no fire can thaw her thoughts.

TOPHAS

0029It is an old goose, Epi, that will eat no oats; old kine 0030will kick, old rats gnaw cheese, and old sacks will have 0031much patching. I prefer an old cony before a rabbit-sucker, 0032and an ancient hen before a young chicken 0033peeper.

EPITON

0034Argumentum ab antiquitate. [Aside] My master loveth 0035antique work.

TOPHAS

0036Give me a pippin that is withered like an old wife.

EPITON

0037Good, sir.

TOPHAS

0038Then a contrario sequitur argumentum. Give me a wife 0039that looks like an old pippin.

EPITON

0040Nothing hath made my master a fool but flat 0041scholarship.

TOPHAS

0042Knowest thou not that old wine is best?

EPITON

0043Yes.

TOPHAS

0044And thou knowest that like will to like?

EPITON

0045Ay.

TOPHAS

0046And thou knowest that Venus loved the best wine?

EPITON

0047So.

TOPHAS

0048Then I conclude that Venus was an old woman in an 0049old cup of wine. For, est Venus in vinis, ignis in igne fruit.

EPITON

0050O lepidum caput, O madcap master! You were worthy 0051to win Dipsas, where she as old again, for in your love you 0052have worn the nap of your wit quite off and made it 0053threadbare. But soft, who comes here?

[Enter Samias and Dares.]

TOPHAS

0054My solicitors.

SAMIAS

0055All hail, Sir Tophas! How feel you yourself?

TOPHAS

0056Stately in every joint, which the common people term 0057stiffness. Doth Dipsas stoop? Will she yield? Will she 0058bend?

DARES

0059O, sir, as much as you would wish, for her chin almost 0060toucheth her knees.

EPITON

0061Master, she is bent, I warrant you.

TOPHAS

0062What conditions doth she ask?

SAMIAS

0063She hath vowed she will never love any that hath not 0064a tooth in his head less than she.

TOPHAS

0065How many hath she?

DARES

0066One.

EPITON

0067That goeth hard, master, for then you must have 0068none.

TOPHAS

0069A small request, and agreeable to the gravity of her 0070years. What should a wise man do with his mouth full of 0071bones like a charnel house? The turtle true hath ne'er a 0072tooth.

SAMIAS

0073Thy master is in a notable vein, that 0074will lose his teeth to be like a turtle.

EPITON

0075Let him lose his tongue too, I care 0076not.

DARES

0077Nay, you must also have no nails, for she long since 0078hath cast hers.

TOPHAS

0079That I yield to. What a quiet life shall Dipsas and I 0080lead, when we can neither bite nor scratch! You may see, 0081youths, how age provides for peace.

SAMIAS

0082How shall we do to make 0083him leave his love? For we never spake to her.

DARES

0084[Aside to Samias] Let me alone. [To Sir Tophas] She is 0085a notable witch, and hath turned her maid Bagoa to an 0086aspen tree for bewraying her secrets.

TOPHAS

0087I honour her for her cunning, for now, when I am 0088weary of walking on two legs, what a pleasure may she do 0089me to turn me to some goodly ass and help me to four!

DARES

0090Nay then, I must tell you the truth: her husband Geron 0091is come home, who this fifty years hath had her to wife.

TOPHAS

0092What do I hear? Hath she an husband? Go to the 0093sexton and tell him Desire is dead, and will him to dig his 0094grave. Oh heavens, an husband? What death is agreeable 0095to my fortune?

SAMIAS

0096Be not desperate, and we will help you to find a 0097young lady.

TOPHAS

0098I love no Grissels; they are so brittle they will crack 0099like glass, or so dainty that if they be touched they are 0100straight of the fashion of wax. Animus maioribus instat; I 0101desire old matrons. What a sight would it be to embrace 0102one whose hair were as orient as the pearl, whose teeth 0103shall be so pure a watchet that they shall stain the truest 0104turquoise, whose nose shall throw more beams from it 0105than the fiery carbuncle, whose eyes shall be environed 0106about with redness exceeding the deepest coral, and 0107whose lips might compare with silver for the paleness! 0108Such a one if you can help me to, I will by piecemeal 0109curtail my affections toward Dipsas and walk my swelling 0110thoughts till they be cold.

EPITON

0111Wisely provided. How say you, my friends, will you 0112angle for my master's cause?

SAMIAS

0113Most willingly.

DARES

0114If we speed him not shortly, I will burn my cap. We 0115will serve him of the spades, and dig and old wife out of the 0116grave that shall be answerable to his gravity.

TOPHAS

0117Youths, adieu. He that bringeth me first news shall 0118possess mine inheritance.

[Exit.]

DARES

0119[To Epiton] What, is thy master landed?

EPITON

0120Know you not that my master is liber tenens?

SAMIAS

0121What's that?

EPITON

0122A freeholder. But I will after him.

SAMIAS

0123And we to hear what news of Endymion for the 0124conclusion.

Exeunt.

Actus Quintus, Scaena Tertia

[Enter] Panelion [and] Zontes.

PANELION

0001Who would have thought that Tellus, being so fair 0002by nature, so honourable by birth, so wise by education, 0003would have entered into a mischief to the gods so odious, 0004to men so detestable, and to her friend so malicious?

ZONTES

0005If Bagoa had not bewrayed it, how then should it have 0006come to light? But we see that gold and fair words are of 0007force to corrupt the strongest men, and therefore able to 0008work silly women like wax.

PANELION

0009I marvel what Cynthia will determine in this cause.

ZONTES

0010I fear as in all causes: hear of it in justice and then 0011judge of it in mercy. For how can it be that she that is 0012unwilling to punish her deadliest foes with disgrace will 0013revenge injuries of her train with death?

PANELION

0014That old witch Dipsas, in a rage, having understood 0015her practice to be discovered, turned poor Bagoa to an 0016aspen tree. But let us make haste and bring Tellus before 0017Cynthia, for she was coming out after us.

ZONTES

0018Let us go.

Exeunt.

[SCENE 4]

[Enter] Cynthia, Semele, Floscula, Dipsas, Endymion,

Eumenides[, Geron, Pythagoras, Gyptes, and Sir Tophas.

A tree stands by the lunary bank, as in IV.iii and V.i].

CYNTHIA

0001Dipsas, thy years are not so many as thy vices, yet 0002more in number than commonly nature doth afford or 0003justice should permit. Hast thou almost these fifty years 0004practised that detested wickedness of witchcraft? Wast 0005thou so simple as for to know the nature of simples, of all 0006creatures to be most sinful? Thou hast threatened to turn 0007my course awry and alter by thy damnable art the government 0008that I now possess by the eternal gods. But know 0009thou, Dipsas, and let all the enchanters know, that 0010Cynthia, being placed for light on earth, is also protected 0011by the powers of heaven. Breathe out thou mayst words, 0012gather thou mayst herbs, find out thou mayst stones 0013agreeable to thine art, yet of no force to appal my heart, 0014in which courage is so rooted, and constant persuasion of 0015the mercy of the gods so grounded, that all thy witchcraft 0016I esteem as weak as the world doth thy case wretched. 0017This noble gentleman Geron, once thy husband but now 0018thy mortal hate, didst thou procure to live in a desert, 0019almost desperate. Endymion, the flower of my court and 0020the hope of succeeding time, hast thou bewitched by art 0021before thou wouldst suffer him to flourish by nature.

DIPSAS

0022Madam, things past may be repented, not recalled. 0023There is nothing so wicked that I have not done, nor 0024anything so wished for as death. Yet among all the things 0025that I committed, there is nothing so much tormenteth 0026my rented and ransacked thoughts as that in the prime of 0027my husband's youth I divorced him by my devilish art, for 0028which, if to die might be amends, I would not live till 0029tomorrow. If to live and still be more miserable would 0030better content him, I would wish of all creatures to be 0031oldest and ugliest.

GERON

0032Dipsas, thou hast made this difference between me 0033and Endymion, that, being both young, thou hast caused 0034me to wake in melancholy, losing the joys of my youth, 0035and him to sleep, not remembering youth.

CYNTHIA

0036Stay, here cometh Tellus. We shall now know all.

Enter Corsites [and] Tellus, [escorted by] Panelion

[and Zontes].

CORSITES

0037[To Tellus] I would to Cynthia thou couldst make as 0038good an excuse in truth as to me thou hast done by wit.

TELLUS

0039Truth shall be mine answer, and therefore I will not 0040study for an excuse.

CYNTHIA

0041Is it possible, Tellus, that so few years should harbour 0042so many mischiefs? Thy swelling pride have I borne 0043because it is a thing that beauty maketh blameless, which, 0044the more it exceedeth fairness in measure, the more it 0045stretcheth itself in disdain. Thy devices against Corsites I 0046smile at, for that wits the sharper they are, the shrewder 0047they are. But this unacquainted and most unnatural practice 0048with a vile enchantress against so noble a gentleman 0049as Endymion I abhor as a thing most malicious, and will 0050revenge as a deed most monstrous. And as for you, 0051Dipsas, I will send you into the desert amongst wild 0052beasts, and try whether you can cast lions, tigers, boars, 0053and bears into as dead a sleep as you did Endymion, or 0054turn them to trees as you have done Bagoa. But tell me, 0055Tellus, what was the cause of this cruel part, far unfitting 0056thy sex, in which nothing should be but simpleness, and 0057much disagreeing from thy face, in which nothing seemed 0058to be but softness?

TELLUS

0059Divine Cynthia, by whom I receive my life and am 0060content to end it, I can neither excuse my fault without 0061lying nor confess it without shame. Yet were it possible 0062that in so heavenly thoughts as yours there could fall such 0063earthly motions as mine, I would then hope, if not to be 0064pardoned without extreme punishment, yet to be heard 0065without great marvel.

CYNTHIA

0066Say on, Tellus. I cannot imagine anything that can 0067colour such a cruelty.

TELLUS

0068Endymion, that Endymion, in the prime of his youth 0069so ravished my heart with love that to obtain my desires 0070I could not find means, nor to resist them reason. What 0071was she that favoured not Endymion, being young, wise, 0072honourable, and virtuous? Besides, what metal was she 0073made of, be she mortal, that is not affected with the spice, 0074nay infected with the poison, of that not-to-be-expressed 0075yet always-to-be-felt love, which breaketh the brains and 0076never bruiseth the brow, consumeth the heart and never 0077toucheth the skin, and maketh a deep wound to be felt 0078before any scar at all be seen? My heart, too tender to 0079withstand such a divine fury, yielded to love –madam, I 0080not without blushing confess, yielded to love.

CYNTHIA

0081A strange effect of love, to work such an extreme 0082hate. How say you, Endymion, all this was for love?

ENDYMION

0083I say, madam, then the gods send me a woman's 0084hate.

CYNTHIA

0085That were as bad, for then by contrary you should 0086never sleep. But on, Tellus, let us hear the end.

TELLUS

0087Feeling a continual burning in all my bowels and a 0088bursting almost in every vein, I could not smother the 0089inward fire, but it must needs be perceived by the outward 0090smoke; and, by the flying abroad of divers sparks, 0091divers judged of my scalding flames. Endymion, as full of 0092art as wit, marking mine eyes (in which he might see 0093almost his own), my sighs (by which he might ever hear 0094his name sounded), aimed at my heart (in which he was 0095assured his person was imprinted), and by questions 0096wrung out that which was ready to burst out. When he 0097saw the depth of my affections, he swore that mine in 0098respect of his were as fumes to Etna, valleys to Alps, ants 0099to eagles, and nothing could be compared to my beauty 0100but his love and eternity. Thus drawing a smooth shoe 0101upon a crooked foot, he made me believe that (which all 0102of our sex willingly acknowledge) I was beautiful, and to 0103wonder (which indeed is a thing miraculous) that any of 0104his sex should be faithful.

CYNTHIA

0105Endymion, how will you clear yourself?

ENDYMION

0106Madam, by mine own accuser.

CYNTHIA

0107Well, Tellus, proceed, but briefly, lest, taking delight 0108in uttering thy love, thou offend us with the length of it.

TELLUS

0109I will, madam, quickly make an end of my love and my 0110tale. Finding continual increase of my tormenting 0111thoughts, and that the enjoying of my love made deeper 0112wounds than the entering into it, I could find no means to 0113ease my grief but to follow Endymion, and continually to 0114have him in the object of mine eyes, who had me slave 0115and subject to his love. But in the moment that I feared 0116his falsehood, and fried myself most in mine affections, I 0117found (ah, grief! even then I lost myself), I found him in 0118most melancholy and desperate terms, cursing his stars, 0119his state, the earth, the heavens, the world, an all for the 0120love of–

CYNTHIA

0121Of whom? Tellus, speak boldly.

TELLUS

0122Madam, I dare not utter for fear to offend.

CYNTHIA

0123Speak, I say. Who dare take offence if thou be commanded 0124by Cynthia?

TELLUS

0125For the love of Cynthia.

CYNTHIA

0126For my love, Tellus? That were strange. Endymion, 0127is it true?

ENDYMION

0128In all things, madam. Tellus doth not speak false.

CYNTHIA

0129What will this breed to in the end? Well, Endymion, 0130we shall hear all.

TELLUS

0131I, seeing my hopes turned to mishaps, and a settled 0132dissembling towards me, and an unmovable desire to 0133Cynthia, forgetting both myself and my sex, fell unto this 0134unnatural hate. For knowing your virtues, Cynthia, to be 0135immortal, I could not have an imagination to withdraw 0136him; and finding mine own affections unquenchable, I 0137could not carry the mind that any else should possess 0138what I had pursued. For though in majesty, beauty, virtue, 0139and dignity I always humbled and yielded myself to 0140Cynthia, yet in affections I esteemed myself equal with 0141the goddesses, and all other creatures, according to their 0142states, with myself. For stars to their bigness have their 0143lights, and the sun hath no more. And little pitchers, 0144when they can hold no more, are as full as great vessels 0145that run over. Thus, madam, in all truth have I uttered 0146the unhappiness of my love and the cause of my hate, 0147yielding wholly to that divine judgement which never 0148erred for want of wisdom or envied for too much 0149partiality.

CYNTHIA

0150How say you, my lords, to this matter? But what say 0151you, Endymion, hath Tellus told truth?

ENDYMION

0152Madam, in all things, but in that she said I loved 0153her and swore to honour her.

CYNTHIA

0154Was there such a time whenas for my love thou didst 0155vow thyself to death, and in respect of it loathed thy life? 0156Speak, Endymion. I will not revenge it with hate.

ENDYMION

0157The time was, madam, and is, and ever shall be, 0158that I honoured Your Highness above all the world; but 0159to stretch it so far as to call it love, I never durst. There 0160hath none pleased mine eye but Cynthia, none delighted 0161mine ears but Cynthia, none possessed my heart but 0162Cynthia. I have forsaken all other fortunes to follow 0163Cynthia, and here I stand ready to die if it please Cynthia. 0164Such a difference hath the gods set between our states 0165that all must be duty, loyalty, and reverence; nothing, 0166without it vouchsafe Your Highness, be termed love. My 0167unspotted thoughts, my languishing body, my discontented 0168life, let them obtain by princely favour that which 0169to challenge they must not presume, only wishing of 0170impossibilities; with imagination of which I will spend my 0171spirits, and to myself, that no creature may hear, softly 0172call it love. And if any urge to utter what I whisper, then 0173will I name it honour. From this sweet contemplation if I 0174be not driven, I shall live of all men the most content, 0175taking more pleasure in mine aged thoughts than ever I 0176did in my youthful actions.

CYNTHIA

0177Endymion, this honourable respect of thine shall be 0178christened 'love' in thee, and my reward for it 'favour'. 0179Persevere, Endymion, in loving me, and I account more 0180strength in a true heart than in a walled city. I have 0181laboured to win all, and study to keep such as I have won; 0182but those that neither my favour can move to continue 0183constant, nor my offered benefits get to be faithful, the 0184gods shall either reduce to truth or revenge their treacheries 0185with justice. Endymion, continue as thou hast begun, 0186and thou shalt find that Cynthia shineth not on thee 0187in vain.

[Endymion's youthful looks are restored to him.]

ENDYMION

0188Your highness hath blessed me, and your words 0189have again restored my youth. Methinks I feel my joints 0190strong, and these mouldy hairs to moult, and all by your 0191virtue, Cynthia, into whose hands the balance that 0192weigheth time and fortune are committed.

CYNTHIA

0193What, young again? Then it is pity to punish Tellus.

TELLUS

0194Ah, Endymion, now I know thee and ask pardon of 0195thee. Suffer me still to wish thee well.

ENDYMION

0196Tellus, Cynthia must command what she will.

FLOSCULA

0197Endymion, I rejoice to see thee in thy former estate.

ENDYMION

0198Good Floscula, to thee also am I in my former 0199affections.

EUMENIDES

0200Endymion, the comfort of my life, how am I ravished 0201with a joy matchless, saving only the enjoying of my 0202mistress!

CYNTHIA

0203Endymion, you must now tell who Eumenides 0204shrineth for his saint.

ENDYMION

0205Semele, madam.

CYNTHIA

0206Semele, Eumenides? Is it Semele? The very wasp of 0207all women, whose tongue stingeth as much as an adder's 0208tooth?

EUMENIDES

0209It is Semele, Cynthia, the possessing of whose 0210love must only prolong my life.

CYNTHIA

0211Nay, sith Endymion is restored, we will have all 0212parties pleased. Semele, are you content after so long trial 0213of his faith, such rare secrecy, such unspotted love, to 0214take Eumenides? –Why speak you not? Not a word?

ENDYMION

0215Silence, madam, consents. That is most true.

CYNTHIA

0216It is true, Endymion. Eumenides, take Semele. Take 0217her, I say.

EUMENIDES

0218Humble thanks, madam. Now only do I begin to 0219live.

SEMELE

0220A hard choice, madam, either to be married if I say 0221nothing or to lose my tongue if I speak a word. Yet do I 0222rather choose to have my tongue cut out than my heart 0223distempered. I will not have him.

CYNTHIA

0224Speaks the parrot? She shall nod hereafter with 0225signs. Cut off her tongue, nay, her head, that, having a 0226servant of honourable birth, honest manners, and true 0227love, will not be persuaded!

SEMELE

0228He is no faithful lover, madam, for then would he 0229have asked his mistress.

GERON

0230Had he not been faithful, he had never seen into the 0231fountain, and so lost his friend and mistress.

EUMENIDES

0232Thine own thoughts, sweet Semele, witness 0233against thy words, for what hast thou found in my life but 0234love? And as yet what have I found in my love but 0235bitterness? Madam, pardon Semele, and let my tongue 0236ransom hers.

CYNTHIA

0237Thy tongue, Eumenides? What shouldst thou live, 0238wanting a tongue to blaze the beauty of Semele? Well, 0239Semele, I will not command love, for it cannot be enforced. 0240Let me entreat it.

SEMELE

0241I am content Your Highness shall command, for now 0242only do I think Eumenides faithful, that is willing to lose 0243his tongue for my sake; yet loath, because it should do me 0244better service. Madam, I accept of Eumenides.

CYNTHIA

0245I thank you, Semele.

EUMENIDES

0246Ah, happy Eumenides, that hast a friend so faithful 0247and a mistress so fair! With what sudden mischief will 0248the gods daunt this excess of joy? Sweet Semele, I live or 0249die as thou wilt.

CYNTHIA

0250What shall become of Tellus? Tellus, you know 0251Endymion is vowed to a service from which death cannot 0252remove him. Corsites casteth still a lovely look towards 0253you. How say you, will you have your Corsites and so 0254receive pardon for all that is past?

TELLUS

0255Madam, most willingly.

CYNTHIA

0256But I cannot tell whether Corsites be agreed.

CORSITES

0257Ay, madam, more happy to enjoy Tellus than the 0258monarchy of the world.

EUMENIDES

0259Why, she caused you to be pinched with fairies.

CORSITES

0260Ay, but her fairness hath pinched my heart more 0261deeply.

CYNTHIA

0262Well, enjoy thy love. But what have you wrought in 0263the castle, Tellus?

TELLUS

0264Only the picture of Endymion.

CYNTHIA

0265Then so much of Endymion as his picture cometh 0266to, possess and play withal.

CORSITES

0267Ah, my sweet Tellus, my love shall be as thy beauty 0268is: matchless.

CYNTHIA

0269Now it resteth, Dipsas, that if thou wilt forswear that 0270vile art of enchanting, Geron hath promised again to 0271receive thee; otherwise, If thou be wedded to that wickedness, 0272I must and will see it punished to the uttermost.

DIPSAS

0273Madam, I renounce both substance and shadow of 0274that most horrible and hateful trade, vowing to the gods 0275continual penance and to Your Highness obedience.

CYNTHIA

0276How say you, Geron, will you admit her to your 0277wife?

GERON

0278Ay, with more joy than I did the first day, for nothing 0279could happen to make me happy but only her forsaking 0280that lewd and detestable course. Dipsas, I embrace thee.

DIPSAS

0281And I thee, Geron, to whom I will hereafter recite the 0282cause of these my first follies.

[They embrace.]

CYNTHIA

0283Well, Endymion, nothing resteth now but that we 0284depart. Thou hast my favour, Tellus her friend, 0285Eumenides in paradise with his Semele, Geron contented 0286with Dipsas.

TOPHAS

0287Nay, soft. I cannot handsomely go to bed without 0288Bagoa.

CYNTHIA

0289Well, Sir Tophas, it may be there are more virtues in 0290me than myself knoweth of, for Endymion I awaked, and 0291at my words he waxed young. I will try whether I can turn 0292this tree again to thy true love.

TOPHAS

0293Turn her to a true love or false, so she be a wench I 0294care not.

CYNTHIA

0295Bagoa, Cynthia putteth an end to thy hard fortunes, 0296for, being turned to a tree for revealing a truth, I will 0297recover thee again if in my power be the effect of truth.

[Bagoa regains her human shape.]

TOPHAS

0298Bagoa? A bots upon thee!

CYNTHIA

0299Come my lords, let us in. You, Gyptes and Pythagoras, 0300if you cannot content yourselves in our court to fall 0301from vain follies of philosophers to such virtues as are 0302here practised, you shall be entertained according to your 0303deserts, for Cynthia is no stepmother to strangers.

PYTHAGORAS

0304I had rather in Cynthia's court spend ten years 0305than in Greece one hour.

GYPTES

0306And I choose rather to live by the sight of Cynthia 0307than by the possessing of all Egypt.

CYNTHIA

0308Then follow.

EUMENIDES

0309We all attend.

Exeunt.

The Epilogue

0001A man walking abroad, the wind and sun strove for 0002sovereignty; the one with his blast, the other with his 0003beams. The wind blew hard; the man wrapped his garment 0004about him harder. It blustered more strongly; he 0005then girt it fast to him. 'I cannot prevail', said the wind. 0006The sun casting her crystal beams began to warm the 0007man; he unloosed his gown. Yet it shined brighter; he 0008then put it off. 'I yield', said the wind, 'for if thou continue 0009shining he will also put off his coat'. 0010Dread sovereign, the malicious that seek to overthrow 0011us with threats do but stiffen our thoughts and make 0012them sturdier in storms.
