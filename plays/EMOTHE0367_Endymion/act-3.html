Act III

Actus Tertius, Scaena Prima

[Enter] Cynthia, three lords [Corsites, Zontes, and

Panelion,] Tellus, [Semele, and Eumenides].

CYNTHIA

0001Is the report true that Endymion is stricken into 0002such a dead sleep that nothing can either wake him or 0003move him?

EUMENIDES

0004Too true, madam, and as much to be pitied as 0005wondered at.

TELLUS

0006As good sleep and do no harm as wake and do no 0007good.

CYNTHIA

0008What maketh you, Tellus, to be so short? The time 0009was, Endymion only was.

EUMENIDES

0010It is an old saying, madam, that a waking dog doth 0011afar off bark at a sleeping lion.

SEMELE

0012It were good, Eumenides, that you took a nap with 0013your friend, for you speech beginneth to be heavy.

EUMENIDES

0014Contrary to your nature, Semele, which hath been 0015always accounted light.

CYNTHIA

0016What, have we here before my face these unseemly 0017and malapert overthwarts? I will tame your tongues and 0018your thoughts, and make your speeches answerable to 0019your duties and your conceits fit for my dignity; else will 0020I banish you both my person and the world.

EUMENIDES

0021Pardon, I humbly ask; but such is my unspotted 0022faith to Endymion that whatsoever seemeth a needle to 0023prick his finger is a dagger to wound my heart.

CYNTHIA

0024If you be so dear to him, how happeneth it you 0025neither go to see him nor search for remedy for him?

EUMENIDES

0026I have seen him, to my grief, and sought recure 0027with despair, for that I cannot imagine who should restore 0028him that is the wonder to all men. Your Highness, 0029on whose hands the compass of the earth is at command 0030(though not in possession), may show yourself both worthy 0031your sex, your nature, and your favour, if you redeem 0032that honourable Endymion, whose ripe years foretell rare 0033virtues and whose unmellowed conceits promise ripe 0034counsel.

CYNTHIA

0035I have had trial of Endymion, and conceive greater 0036assurance of his age than I could hope of his youth.

TELLUS

0037But timely, madam, crooks that tree that will be a 0038cammock, and young it pricks that will be a thorn; and 0039therefore he that began without care to settle his life, it is 0040a sign without amendment he will end it.

CYNTHIA

0041Presumptuous girl, I will make thy tongue an example 0042of unrecoverable displeasure. –Corsites, carry her to 0043the castle in the desert, there to remain and weave.

CORSITES

0044Shall she work stories or poetries?

CYNTHIA

0045It skillet not which. Go to, in both; for she shall 0046find examples infinite in either, what punishment long 0047tongues have. [Exeunt Corsites and Tellus.] 0048Eumenides, if either the soothsayers in Egypt, or the 0049enchanters in Thessaly, or the philosophers in Greece, or 0050all the sages of the world can find remedy, I will procure 0051it. Therefore dispatch with all speed: you, Eumenides, 0052into Thessaly; you, Zontes, into Greece (because you 0053are acquainted in Athens); you, Panelion, to Egypt, 0054saying that Cynthia sendeth and, if you will, 0055commandeth.

EUMENIDES

0056On bowed knee I give thanks, and with wings on 0057my legs I fly for remedy.

ZONTES

0058We are ready at Your Highness' command, and hope 0059to return to your full content.

CYNTHIA

0060It shall never be said that Cynthia, whose mercy and 0061goodness filleth the heavens with joys and the world with 0062marvels, will suffer either Endymion or any to perish if he 0063may be protected.

EUMENIDES

0064Your Majesty's words have been always deeds, 0065and your deeds virtues.

Exeunt.

Actus Tertius, Scaena Secunda

[Enter] Corsites [and] Tellus.

CORSITES

0001Here is the candle, fair Tellus, in which you must 0002weave, till either time end your days or Cynthia her 0003displeasure. I am sorry so fair a face should be subject to 0004so hard a fortune, and that the flower of beauty, which is 0005honoured in courts, should here wither in prison.

TELLUS

0006Corsites, Cynthia may restrain the liberty of my body; 0007of my thoughts she cannot. And therefore do I esteem 0008myself most free, though I am in greatest bondage.

CORSITES

0009Can you then feed on fancy, and subdue the malice 0010of envy by the sweetness of imagination?

TELLUS

0011Corsites, there is no sweeter music to the miserable 0012than despair; and therefore the more bitterness I feel, the 0013more sweetness I find. For so vain were liberty, and so 0014unwelcome the following of higher fortune, that I choose 0015rather to pine in this castle than to be a prince in any 0016other court.

CORSITES

0017A humour contrary to your years and nothing agreeable 0018to your sex, the one commonly allured with delights, 0019the other always with sovereignty.

TELLUS

0020I marvel, Corsites, that you, being a captain, who 0021should sound nothing but terror and suck nothing but 0022blood, can find in your heart to talk such smooth words, 0023for that it agreeth not with your calling to use words so 0024soft as that of love.

CORSITES

0025Lady, it were unfit of wars to discourse with women, 0026into whose minds nothing can sink but smoothness. Besides, 0027you must not think that soldiers be so rough-hewn 0028or of such knotty metal that beauty cannot allure, and 0029you, being beyond perfection, enchant.

TELLUS

0030Good Corsites, talk not of love, but let me to my 0031labour. The little beauty I have shall be bestowed on my 0032loom, which I now mean to make my lover.

CORSITES

0033Let us in, and what favour Corsites can show, Tellus 0034shall command.

TELLUS

0035The only favour I desire is now and then to walk.

Exeunt.

Actus Tertius, Scaena Tertia

[Enter] Sir Tophas [armed as before], and Epiton[, with a

gown and other paraphernalia].

TOPHAS

0001Epi!

EPITON

0002Here, sir.

TOPHAS

0003Unrig me. Heighho!

EPITON

0004What's that?

TOPHAS

0005An interjection, whereof some are of mourning, as 0006eho, vah.

EPITON

0007I understand you not.

TOPHAS

0008Thou seest me.

EPITON

0009Ay.

TOPHAS

0010Thou hearst me.

EPITON

0011Ay.

TOPHAS

0012Thou feelest me.

EPITON

0013Ay.

TOPHAS

0014And not understandst me?

EPITON

0015No.

TOPHAS

0016Then am I but three quarters of a noun substantive. 0017But alas, Epi, to tell thee the troth I am a noun adjective.

EPITON

0018Why?

TOPHAS

0019Because I cannot stand without another.

EPITON

0020Who is that?

TOPHAS

0021Dipsas.

EPITON

0022Are you in love?

TOPHAS

0023No, but love hath, as it were, milked my thoughts and 0024drained from my heart the very substance of my accustomed 0025courage. It worketh in my head like new wine, so 0026as I must hoop my sconce with iron, lest my head break 0027and so I bewray my brains. But I pray thee, first discover 0028me in all parts, that I may be like a lover, and then will I 0029sigh and die. Take my gun, and give me a gown. Cedant 0030arma togae.

EPITON

0031[Helping Sir Tophas to disarm] Here.

TOPHAS

0032Take my sword and shield, and give me beard-brush 0033and scissors. Bella gerant alii; tu, Pari, semper ama.

EPITON

0034Will you be trimmed, sir?

TOPHAS

0035Not yet, for I feel a contention within me whether I 0036shall frame the bodkin beard or the bush. But take my 0037pike and give me pen. Dicere quae puduit, scribere jussit 0038amor.

EPITON

0039I will furnish you, sir.

TOPHAS

0040Now for my bow and bolts, give me ink and paper; 0041for my smiter, a penknife. For scalpellum, calami, 0042atramentum, charta, libelli, sint semper studiis arma parata meis.

EPITON

0043Sir, will you give over wars and play with that bauble 0044called love?

TOPHAS

0045Give over wars? No, Epi. Militat omnis amans, et habet 0046sua castra Cupido.

EPITON

0047Love hath made you very eloquent, but your face is 0048nothing fair.

TOPHAS

0049Non formosus erat, sed erat facundus Ulysses.

EPITON

0050Nay, I must seek a new master if you can speak 0051nothing but verses.

TOPHAS

0052Quicquid conabar dicere versus erat. Epi, I feel all Ovid 0053de Arte Amandi lie as heavy at my heart as a load of logs. 0054O, what a fine thin hair hath Dipsas! What a pretty low 0055forehead! What a tall and stately nose! What little hollow 0056eyes! What great and goodly lips! How harmless she is, 0057being toothless! Her fingers fat and short, adorned with 0058long nails like a bittern! In how sweet a proportion her 0059cheeks hang down to her breast like dugs, and her paps 0060to her waist like bags! What a low stature she is, and yet 0061what a great foot she carrieth! How thrifty must she be in 0062whom there is no waste! How virtuous is she like to be, 0063over whom no man can be jealous!

EPITON

0064Stay, master, you forget yourself.

TOPHAS

0065O Epi, even as a dish melteth by the fire, so doth my 0066wit increase by love.

EPITON

0067Pithily, and to the purpose. But what, begin you to 0068nod?

TOPHAS

0069Good Epi, let me take a nap. For as some man may 0070better steal a horse than another look over the hedge, so 0071divers shall be sleepy when they would fainest take rest.

He sleeps.

EPITON

0072Who ever saw such a woodcock? Love Dipsas? Without 0073doubt all the world will now account him valiant, that 0074ventureth on her whom none durst undertake. But here 0075cometh two wags.

Enter Dares and Samias.

SAMIAS

0076[To Dares] Thy master hath slept his share.

DARES

0077[To Samias] I think he doth it because he would not 0078pay me my board wages.

SAMIAS

0079It is a thing most strange, and I think mine will never 0080return; so that we must both seek new masters, for we 0081shall never live by our manners.

EPITON

0082[To Samias and Dares] If you want masters, join with 0083me and serve Sir Tophas, who must needs keep more 0084men because he is toward marriage.

SAMIAS

0085What, Epi, where's thy master?

EPITON

0086Yonder sleeping in love.

DARES

0087Is it possible?

EPITON

0088He hath taken his thoughts a hole lower, and saith, 0089seeing it is the fashion of the world, he will vail bonnet to 0090beauty.

SAMIAS

0091How is he attired?

EPITON

0092Lovely.

DARES

0093Whom loveth this amorous knight?

EPITON

0094Dipsas.

SAMIAS

0095That ugly creature? Why, she is a fool, a scold, fat, 0096without fashion, and quite without favour.

EPITON

0097Tush, you be simple. My master hath a good 0098marriage.

DARES

0099Good? As how?

EPITON

0100Why, in marrying Dipsas, he shall have every day 0101twelve dishes of meat to his dinner, though there be none 0102but Dipsas with him. Four of flesh, four of fish, four of 0103fruit.

SAMIAS

0104As how, Epi?

EPITON

0105For flesh, these: woodcock, goose, bittern, and rail.

DARES

0106Indeed, he shall not miss if Dipsas be there.

EPITON

0107For fish, these: crab, carp, lump, and pouting.

SAMIAS

0108Excellent! For, of my word, she is both crabbish, 0109lumpish, and carping.

EPITON

0110For fruit, these: fretters, medlers, hart-i-chokes, and 0111lady-longings. Thus you see he shall fare like a king, 0112though he be but a beggar.

DARES

0113Well, Epi, dine thou with him, for I had rather fast 0114than see her face. But see, thy master is asleep. Let us 0115have a song to wake this amorous knight.

EPITON

0116Agreed.

SAMIAS

0117Content.

Song.

EPITON

118Here snores Tophas,
119That amorous ass,
120Who loves Dipsas,
121With face so sweet,
122Nose and chin meet.

ALL THREE

123At sight of her each Fury skips
124And fling into her lap their whips.

DARES

125Holla, holla in his ear.

SAMIAS

126The witch sure thrust her fingers there.

EPITON

127Cramp him, or wring the fool by th'nose.

DARES

128Or clap some burning flax to his toes.

SAMIAS

129What music's best to wake him?

EPITON

130Bow-wow! Let bandogs shake him.

DARES

131Let adders hiss in's ear.

SAMIAS

132Else earwigs wriggle there.

EPITON

133No, let him batten; when his tongue
134Once goes, a cat is not worse strung.

ALL THREE

135But if he ope nor mouth nor eyes,
136He may in time sleep himself wise.

TOPHAS

0137Sleep is a binding of the 0138senses, love a loosing.

EPITON

0139Let us hear him awhile.

TOPHAS

0140There appeared in my sleep a goodly owl, who, 0141sitting upon my shoulder, cried 'Twit, twit', and before 0142mine eyes presented herself the express image of Dipsas. 0143I marvelled what the owl said, till at the last I perceived 0144'Twit, twit', 'To it, to it', only by contraction admonished 0145by this vision to make account of my sweet Venus.

SAMIAS

0146[Loudly] Sir Tophas, you have overslept yourself.

TOPHAS

0147No, youth, I have but slept over my love.

DARES

0148Love? Why, it is impossible that into so noble and 0149unconquered a courage, love should creep, having first a 0150head as hard to pierce as steel, then to pass to a heart 0151armed with a shirt of mail.

EPITON

0152Ay, but my master yawning 0153one day in the sun, love crept into his mouth before 0154he could close it, and there kept such a tumbling in his 0155body that he was glad to untruss the points of his heart 0156and entertain Love as a stranger.

TOPHAS

0157If there remain any pity in you, plead for me to 0158Dipsas.

DARES

0159Plead? Nay, we will press her to it. 0160[Aside to Samias] Let us go with him to Dipsas, and there shall we have 0161good sport. –But Sir Tophas, when shall we go? For I 0162find my tongue voluble, and my heart venturous, and all 0163myself like myself.

SAMIAS

0164Come, Dares, let us not lose him till 0165we find our masters, for as long as he liveth, we shall lack 0166neither mirth nor meat.

EPITON

0167We will traverse. –Will you go, sir?

TOPHAS

0168I prae, sequar.

Exeunt.

Actus Tertius, Scaena Quarta

[Enter] Eumenides [and] Geron [near the fountain.

Geron sings.]

EUMENIDES

0001Father, your sad music, being tuned on the same 0002key that my hard fortune is, hath so melted my mind that 0003I wish to hang at your mouth's end till my life end.

GERON

0004These tunes, gentleman, have I been accustomed with 0005these fifty winters, having no other house to shroud myself 0006but the broad heavens; and so familiar with me hath 0007use made misery that I esteem sorrow my chiefest solace. 0008And welcomest is that guest to me that can rehearse the 0009saddest tale or the bloodiest tragedy.

EUMENIDES

0010A strange humour. Might I enquire the cause?

GERON

0011You mast pardon me if I deny to tell it, for, knowing 0012that the revealing of griefs is, as it were, a renewing of 0013sorrow, I have vowed therefore to conceal them, that I 0014might not only feel the depth of everlasting discontentment, 0015but despair of remedy. But whence are you? What 0016fortune hath thrust you to this distress?

EUMENIDES

0017I am going to Thessaly to seek remedy for 0018Endymion, my dearest friend, who hath been cast into a 0019dead sleep almost these twenty years, waxing old and 0020ready for the grave, being almost but newly come forth of 0021the cradle.

GERON

0022You need not for recure travel far, for whoso can 0023clearly see the bottom of this fountain shall have remedy 0024for anything.

EUMENIDES

0025That, methinketh, is unpossible. Why, what virtue 0026can there be in water?

GERON

0027Yes, whosoever can shed the tears of a faithful lover 0028shall obtain anything he would. Read these words 0029engraven about the brim.

EUMENIDES

0030[Reading] Have you known this by experience, or 0031is it placed here of purpose to delude men?

GERON

0032I only would have experience of it, and then should 0033there be an end of my misery. And then would I tell the 0034strangest discourse that ever yet was heard.

EUMENIDES

0035Ah, Eumenides!

GERON

0036What lack you, gentleman? Are you not well?

EUMENIDES

0037Yes, father, but a qualm that often cometh over 0038my heart doth now take hold of me. But did never any 0039lovers come hither?

GERON

0040Lusters, but not lovers. For often have I seen them 0041weep, but never could I hear they saw the bottom.

EUMENIDES

0042Came there women also?

GERON

0043Some.

EUMENIDES

0044What did they see?

GERON

0045They all wept, that the fountain overflowed with tears, 0046but so thick became the water with their tears that I could 0047scarce discern the brim, much less behold the bottom.

EUMENIDES

0048Be faithful lovers so scant?

GERON

0049It seemeth so, for yet heard I never of any.

EUMENIDES

0050Ah, Eumenides, how art thou perplexed! Call to 0051mind the beauty of thy sweet mistress and the depth of 0052thy never-dying affections. How oft hast thou honoured 0053her, not only without spot, but suspicion of falsehood! 0054And how hardly hath she rewarded thee without cause or 0055colour of despite! How secret hast thou been these seven 0056years, that hast not, nor once darest not, to name her for 0057discontenting her! How unfaithful, that hast offered to die 0058for her to please her! Unhappy Eumenides!

GERON

0059Why, gentleman, did you once love?

EUMENIDES

0060Once? Ay, father, and ever shall.

GERON

0061Was she unkind, and you faithful?

EUMENIDES

0062She of all women the most froward, and I of all 0063creatures the most fond.

GERON

0064You doted, then, not loved. For affection is grounded 0065on virtue, and virtue is never peevish; or on beauty, and 0066beauty loveth to be praised.

EUMENIDES

0067Ay, but if all virtuous ladies should yield to all that 0068be loving, or all amiable gentlewomen entertain all that 0069be amorous, their virtues would be accounted vices and 0070their beauties deformities, for that love can be but between 0071two, and that not proceeding of him that is most 0072faithful, but most fortunate.

GERON

0073I would you were so faithful that your tears might 0074make you fortunate.

EUMENIDES

0075Yea, father, if that my tears clear not this fountain, 0076then may swear it is but a mere mockery.

GERON

0077So, 'faith, everyone yet that wept.

EUMENIDES

0078[Looking into the fountain] Ah, I faint, I die! Ah, 0079sweet Semele, let me alone, and dissolve by weeping into 0080water!

GERON

0081This affection seemeth strange. If he see nothing, 0082without doubt this dissembling passeth, for nothing 0083shall draw me from the belief.

EUMENIDES

0084Father, I plainly see the bottom, and there in 0085white marble engraven these words: 'Ask one for all, and 0086but one thing at all.'

GERON

0087O fortunate Eumenides (for so have I heard thee call 0088[He looks into the fountain.] thyself), let me see. 0089I cannot discern any such thing. I think thou dreamest.

EUMENIDES

0090Ah, father, thou art not a faithful lover and therefore 0091canst not behold it.

GERON

0092Then ask, that I may be satisfied by the event, and 0093thyself blessed.

EUMENIDES

0094Ask? So I will. And what shall I do but ask, and 0095whom should I ask but Semele, the possessing of whose 0096person is a pleasure that cannot come within the compass 0097of comparison, whose golden locks seem most curious 0098when they seem most careless, whose sweet looks seem 0099most alluring when they are most chaste, and whose 0100words, the more virtuous they are, the more amorous 0101they be accounted. I pray thee, Fortune, when I shall first 0102meet with fair Semele, dash my delight with some light 0103disgrace, lest, embracing sweetness beyond measure, I 0104take surfeit without recure. Let her practise her accustomed 0105coyness, that I may diet myself upon my desires. 0106Otherwise the fullness of my joys will diminish the sweetness, 0107and I shall perish by them before I possess them. 0108Why do I trifle the time in words? The least minute 0109being spent in the getting of Semele is more worth than 0110the whole world; therefore let me ask. –What now, 0111Eumenides? Whither art thou drawn? Hast thou forgotten 0112both friendship and duty, care of Endymion and the 0113commandment of Cynthia? Shall he die in a leaden sleep 0114because thou sleepest in a golden dream? –Ay, let him 0115sleep over, so I slumber but one minute with Semele. 0116Love knoweth neither friendship nor kindred. 0117Shall I not hazard the loss of a friend, for the obtaining 0118of her for whom I would often lose myself? –Fond 0119Eumenides, shall the enticing beauty of a most disdainful 0120lady be of more force than the rare fidelity of a tried 0121friend? The love of men to women is a thing common, 0122and of course; the friendship of man to man infinite, and 0123immortal. –Tush, Semele doth possess my love. –Ay, 0124but Endymion hath deserved it. I will help Endymion; I 0125found Endymion unspotted in his truth. –Ay, but I shall 0126find Semele constant in her love. I will have Semele. 0127–What shall I do? Father, thy grey hairs are ambassadors of 0128experience. Which shall I ask?

GERON

0129Eumenides, release Endymion; for all things, friendship 0130excepted, are subject to fortune. Love is but an eye-worm, 0131which only tickleth the head with hopes and 0132wishes; friendship the image of eternity, in which there is 0133nothing movable, nothing mischievous. As much difference 0134as there is between beauty and virtue, bodies and 0135shadows, colours and life, so great odds is there between 0136love and friendship. Love is a chameleon, which draweth 0137nothing into the mouth bur air, and nourisheth nothing in 0138the body but lungs. Believe me, Eumenides, desire dies in 0139the same moment that beauty sickens, and beauty fadeth 0140in the same instant that it flourisheth. When adversities 0141flow, then love ebbs, but friendship standeth stiffly in 0142storms. Time draweth wrinkles in a fair face but addeth 0143fresh colours to a fast friend, which neither heat, nor cold, 0144nor misery, nor place, nor destiny can alter or diminish. 0145O friendship, of all things the most rare, and therefore 0146most rare because most excellent, whose comforts in 0147misery is always sweet and whose counsels in prosperity 0148are ever fortunate! Vain love, that only coming near to 0149friendship in name, would seem to be the same, or better, 0150in nature!

EUMENIDES

0151Father, I allow your reasons and will therefore 0152conquer mine own. Virtue shall subdue affections, wisdom 0153lust, friendship beauty. Mistresses are in every place, 0154and as common as hares in Athos, bees in Hybla, fowls in 0155the air; but friends to be found are like the phoenix in 0156Arabia, but one, or the philadelphi in Arays, never above 0157two. I will have Endymion. [He looks into the fountain again.] 0158Sacred fountain, in whose bowels are hidden divine secrets, 0159I have increased your waters with the tears of 0160unspotted thoughts, and therefore let me receive the reward 0161you promise. Endymion, the truest friend to me, 0162and faithfullest lover to Cynthia, is in such a dead sleep 0163that nothing can wake or move him.

GERON

0164Dost thou see anything?

EUMENIDES

0165I see in the same pillar these words: 'When she, 0166whose figure of all is the perfectest and never to be 0167measured, always one yet never the same, still inconstant 0168yet never wavering, shall come and kiss Endymion in his 0169sleep, he shall then rise; else, never.' This is strange.

GERON

0170What see you else?

EUMENIDES

0171There cometh over mine eyes either a dark mist, 0172or upon the fountain a deep thickness, for I can perceive 0173nothing. But how am I deluded? Or what difficult, nay, 0174impossible, thing is this?

GERON

0175Methinketh it easy.

EUMENIDES

0176Good father, and how?

GERON

0177Is not a circle of all figures the perfectest?

EUMENIDES

0178Yes.

GERON

0179And is not Cynthia of all circles the most absolute?

EUMENIDES

0180Yes.

GERON

0181Is it not impossible to measure her, who still worketh 0182by her influence, never standing at one stay?

EUMENIDES

0183Yes.

GERON

0184Is she not always Cynthia, yet seldom in the same 0185bigness, always wavering in her waxing or waning, that 0186our bodies might the better be governed, our seasons the 0187dailier give their increase, yet never to be removed from 0188her course as long as the heavens continue theirs?

EUMENIDES

0189Yes.

GERON

0190Then who can it be but Cynthia, whose virtues, being 0191all divine, must needs bring things to pass that be miraculous? 0192Go humble thyself to Cynthia; tell her the success, 0193of which myself shall be a witness. And this assure thyself: 0194that she that sent to find means for his safety will now 0195work her cunning.

EUMENIDES

0196How fortunate am I, if Cynthia be she that may do 0197it!

GERON

0198How fond art thou, if thou do not believe it!

EUMENIDES

0199I will hasten thither, that I may entreat on my 0200knees for succour, and embrace in mine arms my friend.

GERON

0201I will go with thee, for unto Cynthia must I discover all 0202my sorrows, who also must work in me a contentment.

EUMENIDES

0203May I now know the cause?

GERON

0204That shall be as we walk, and I doubt not but the 0205strangeness of my tale will take away the tediousness of 0206our journey.

EUMENIDES

0207Let us go.

GERON

0208I follow.

Exeunt.
