Act II

Actus Secundus, Scaena Prima

[Enter] Endymion

ENDYMION

0001O fair Cynthia, O unfortunate Endymion! Why 0002was not thy birth as high as thy thoughts, or her beauty 0003less than heavenly? Or why are not thine honours as rare 0004as her beauty? Or thy fortunes as great as thy deserts? 0005Sweet Cynthia, how wouldst thou be pleased, how possessed? 0006Will labours, patient of all extremities, obtain thy 0007love? There is no mountain so steep that I will not climb, 0008no monster so cruel that I will not tame, no action so 0009desperate that I will not attempt. Desirest thou the 0010passions of love, the sad and melancholy moods of perplexed 0011minds, the not-to-be-expressed torments of 0012racked thoughts? Behold my sad tears, my deep sighs, my 0013hollow eyes, my broken sleeps, my heavy countenance. 0014Wouldst thou have me vowed only to thy beauty and 0015consume every minute of time in thy service? Remember 0016my solitary life, almost these seven years. Whom have I 0017entertained but mine own thoughts and thy virtues? What 0018company have I used but contemplations? Whom have I 0019wondered at but thee? Nay, whom have I not contemned 0020for thee? Have I not crept to those on whom I might have 0021trodden, only because thou didst shine upon them? Have 0022not injuries been sweet to me if thou vouchsafedst I 0023should bear them? Have I not spent my golden years in 0024hopes, waxing old with wishing, yet wishing nothing but 0025thy love? With Tellus, fair Tellus, have I dissembled, 0026using her but as a cloak for mine affections, that others, 0027seeing my mangled and disordered mind, might think it 0028were for one that loveth me, not for Cynthia, whose 0029perfection alloweth no companion nor comparison. 0030In the midst of these distempered thoughts of mine, 0031thou art not only jealous of my truth, but careless, suspicious, 0032and secure, which strange humour maketh my 0033mind as desperate as thy conceits are doubtful. I am none 0034of those wolves that bark most when thou shinest brightest, 0035but that fish –thy fish, Cynthia, in the flood Araris– 0036which at thy waxing is as white as the driven snow and at 0037thy waning as black as deepest darkness. I am that 0038Endymion, sweet Cynthia, that have carried my thoughts 0039in equal balance with my actions, being always as free 0040from imagining ill as enterprising; that Endymion whose 0041eyes never esteemed anything fair but thy face, whose 0042tongue termed nothing rare but thy virtues, and whose 0043heart imagined nothing miraculous but thy government; 0044yea, that Endymion who, divorcing himself from the 0045amiableness of all ladies, the bravery of all courts, the 0046company of all men, hath chosen in a solitary cell to live 0047only by feeding on thy favour, accounting in the world 0048(but thyself) nothing excellent, nothing immortal. Thus 0049mayst thou see every vein, sinew, muscle, and artery of 0050my love, in which there is no flattery nor deceit, error nor 0051art. But soft, here cometh Tellus. I must turn my other 0052face to her like Janus, lest she be as suspicious as Juno.

Enter Tellus, [Floscula, and Dipsas].

TELLUS

0053Yonder I espy Endymion. I will seem to suspect 0054nothing, but soothe him, that, seeing I cannot obtain the 0055depth of his love, I may learn the height of his dissembling. 0056Floscula and Dipsas, withdraw yourselves out 0057of our sight, yet be within the hearing of our saluting. [Floscula and Dipsas withdraw.] 0058How now, Endymion, always solitary? No company but 0059your own thoughts? No friend but melancholy fancies?

ENDYMION

0060You know, fair Tellus, that the sweet remembrance 0061of your love is the only companion of my life, and 0062thy presence my paradise, so that I am not alone when 0063nobody is with me, and in heaven itself when thou art 0064with me.

TELLUS

0065Then you love me, Endymion?

ENDYMION

0066Or else I live not, Tellus.

TELLUS

0067Is it not possible for you, Endymion, to dissemble?

ENDYMION

0068Not, Tellus, unless I could make me a woman.

TELLUS

0069Why, is dissembling joined to their sex inseparable, as 0070heat to fire, heaviness to earth, moisture to water, thinness 0071to air?

ENDYMION

0072No, but found in their sex as common as spots 0073upon doves, moles upon faces, caterpillars upon sweet 0074apples, cobwebs upon fair windows.

TELLUS

0075Do they all dissemble?

ENDYMION

0076All but one.

TELLUS

0077Who is that?

ENDYMION

0078I dare not tell. For If I should say you, then would 0079you imagine my flattery to be extreme; if another, then 0080would you think my love to be but indifferent.

TELLUS

0081You will be sure I shall take no vantage of your words. 0082But in sooth, Endymion, without more ceremonies, is it 0083not Cynthia?

ENDYMION

0084You know, Tellus, that of the gods we are 0085forbidden to dispute, because their deities come not 0086within the compass of our reasons; and of Cynthia we are 0087allowed not to talk but to wonder, because her virtues are 0088not within the reach of our capacities.

TELLUS

0089Why, she is but a woman.

ENDYMION

0090No more was Venus.

TELLUS

0091She is but a virgin.

ENDYMION

0092No more was Vesta.

TELLUS

0093She shall have an end.

ENDYMION

0094So shall the world.

TELLUS

0095Is not her beauty subject to time?

ENDYMION

0096No more than time is to standing still.

TELLUS

0097Wilt thou make her immortal?

ENDYMION

0098No, but incomparable.

TELLUS

0099Take heed, Endymion, lest, like the wrestler in 0100Olympia that, striving to lift an impossible weight, 0101catched an incurable strain, thou, by fixing thy thoughts 0102above thy reach, fall into a disease without all recure. But 0103I see thou art now in love with Cynthia.

ENDYMION

0104No, Tellus. Thou knowest that the stately cedar, 0105whose top reacheth unto the clouds, never boweth his 0106head to the shrubs that grow in the valley; nor ivy that 0107climbeth up by the elm can ever get hold of the beams of 0108the sun. Cynthia I honour in all humility, whom none 0109ought or dare adventure to love, whose affections are 0110immortal and virtues infinite. Suffer me therefore to gaze 0111on the moon, at whom, were it not for thyself, I would die 0112with wondering.

Exeunt.

Actus Secundus, Scaena Secunda

[Enter] Dares, Samias, Scintilla, [and] Favilla.

DARES

0001Come, Samias, didst thou ever hear such a sighing, the 0002one for Cynthia, the other for Semele, and both for 0003moonshine in the water?

SAMIAS

0004Let them sigh, and let us sing. –How say you, gentlewomen, 0005are not our masters too far in love?

SCINTILLA

0006Their tongues haply are dipped to the root in amorous 0007words and sweet discourses, but I think their hearts 0008are scarce tipped on the side with constant desires.

DARES

0009How say you, Favilla, is not love a lurcher, that taketh 0010men's stomachs away that they cannot eat, their spleen 0011that they cannot laugh, their hearts that they cannot fight, 0012their eyes that they cannot sleep, and leaveth nothing but 0013livers to make nothing but lovers?

FAVILLA

0014Away, peevish boy! A rod were better under thy 0015girdle than love in thy mouth. It will be a forward cock 0016that croweth in the shell.

DARES

0017Alas, good old gentlewoman, how it becometh you to 0018be grave!

SCINTILLA

0019Favilla, though she be but a spark, yet is she fire.

FAVILLA

0020And you, Scintilla, be not much more than a spark, 0021though you would be esteemed a flame.

SAMIAS

0022It were good sport to see the fight 0023between two sparks.

DARES

0024Let them to it, and we will warm us 0025by their words.

SCINTILLA

0026You are not angry, Favilla?

FAVILLA

0027That is, Scintilla, as you list to take it.

SAMIAS

0028That, that!

SCINTILLA

0029This it is to be matched with girls, who, coming but 0030yesterday from making of babies, would before tomorrow 0031be accounted matrons.

FAVILLA

0032I cry your matronship mercy. Because your pantables 0033be higher with cork, therefore your feet must needs be 0034higher in the insteps. You will be mine elder because you 0035stand upon a stool and I on the floor.

SAMIAS

0036Good, good.

DARES

0037Let them alone, and see with what 0038countenance they will become friends.

SCINTILLA

0039[To Favilla] Nay, you think to be the wiser, because 0040you mean to have the last word.

[The women threaten each other.]

SAMIAS

0041Step between them, lest they scratch. In faith, gentlewomen, 0042seeing we came out to be merry, let not your 0043jarring mar our jests. Be friends. How say you?

SCINTILLA

0044I am not angry, but it spited me to see how short she 0045was.

FAVILLA

0046I meant nothing, till she would needs cross me.

DARES

0047Then so let it rest.

SCINTILLA

0048I am agreed.

FAVILLA

0049[Weeping] And I, yet I never took anything so unkindly 0050in my life.

SCINTILLA

0051[Weeping] 'Tis I have the cause, that never offered 0052the occasion.

DARES

0053Excellent, and right like a woman.

SAMIAS

0054A strange sight, to see water come out of fire.

DARES

0055It is their property to carry in their eyes fire and water, 0056tears and torches, and in their mouths honey and gall.

SCINTILLA

0057You will be a good one if you live. But what is 0058yonder formal fellow?

Enter Sir Tophas [and Epiton].

DARES

0059Sir Tophas, Sir Tophas of whom 0060we told you. If you be good wenches, make as though you 0061love him, and wonder at him.

FAVILLA

0062We will do our parts.

DARES

0063But first let us stand aside and let him use his garb, for 0064all consisteth in his gracing.

[The pages and maids-in-waiting stand aside.]

TOPHAS

0065Epi!

EPITON

0066At hand, sir.

TOPHAS

0067How likest thou this martial life, where nothing but 0068blood besprinkleth our bosoms? Let me see, be our enemies 0069fat?

EPITON

0070Passing fat. And I would not change this life to be a 0071lord; and yourself passeth all comparison, for other captains 0072kill and beat, and there is nothing you kill but you 0073also eat.

TOPHAS

0074I will draw out their guts out of their bellies, and tear 0075the flesh with my teeth, so mortal is my hate and so eager 0076my unstanched stomach.

EPITON

0077My master thinks himself the valiantest man in 0078the world if he kill a wren, so warlike a thing he 0079accounteth to take away life, though it be from a lark.

TOPHAS

0080Epi, I find my thoughts to swell and my spirit to take 0081wings, insomuch that I cannot continue within the compass 0082of so slender combats.

FAVILLA

0083This passeth!

SCINTILLA

0084Why, is he not mad?

SAMIAS

0085No, but a little vainglorious.

TOPHAS

0086Epi!

EPITON

0087Sir?

TOPHAS

0088I will encounter that black and cruel enemy that 0089beareth rough and untewed locks upon his body, whose 0090sire throweth down the strongest walls, whose legs are as 0091many as both ours, on whose head are placed most horrible 0092horns by nature as a defence from all harms.

EPITON

0093What mean you, master, to be so desperate?

TOPHAS

0094Honour inciteth me, and very hunger compelleth me.

EPITON

0095What is that monster?

TOPHAS

0096The monster ovis. I have said; let thy wits work.

EPITON

0097I cannot imagine it. Yet let me see. A black enemy 0098with roughs locks –it may be a sheep, and ovis is a sheep. 0099His sire so strong –a ram is a sheep’s sire, that being also 0100an engine of war. Horns he hath, and four legs –so hath 0101a sheep. Without doubt this monster is a black sheep. Is 0102it not a sheep that you mean?

TOPHAS

0103Thou hast hit it. That monster will I kill and sup 0104with.

SAMIAS

0105[To his friends] Come, let us take him off. [The pages and maids come forward.] 0106Sir Tophas, all hail!

TOPHAS

0107Welcome, children. I seldom cast mine eyes so low as 0108to the crowns of your heads, and therefore pardon me 0109that I spake not all this while.

DARES

0110No harm done. Here be fair ladies come to wonder at 0111your person, your valour, your wit, the report whereof 0112hath made them careless of their own honours, to glut 0113their eyes and hearts upon yours.

TOPHAS

0114Report cannot but injure me, for that, not knowing 0115fully what I am, I fear she hath been a niggard in her 0116praises.

SCINTILLA

0117No, gentle knight. Report hath been prodigal, for 0118she hath left you no equal, nor herself credit. So much 0119hath she told, yet no more than we now see.

DARES

0120A good wench.

FAVILLA

0121If there remain as much pity toward women as there 0122is in you courage against your enemies, then shall we be 0123happy, who, hearing of your person, came to see it, and, 0124seeing it, are now in love with it.

TOPHAS

0125Love me, ladies? I easily believe it, but my tough 0126heart receiveth no impression with sweet words. Mars 0127may pierce it; Venus shall not paint on it.

FAVILLA

0128A cruel saying.

SAMIAS

0129There’s a girl.

DARES

0130[To Sir Tophas] Will you cast these ladies away, and all 0131for a little love? Do but speak kindly.

TOPHAS

0132There cometh no soft syllable within my lips. Custom 0133hath made my words bloody and my heart barbarous. 0134That pelting word 'love', how waterish it is in my 0135mouth! It carrieth no sound. Hate, horror, death are 0136speeches that nourish my spirits. I like honey, but I care 0137not for the bees; I delight in music, but I love not to play 0138on the bagpipes; I can vouchsafe to hear the voice of 0139women, but to touch their bodies I disdain it as a thing 0140childish and fit for such men as can disgest nothing but 0141milk.

SCINTILLA

0142A hard heart. Shall we die for your love and find no 0143remedy?

TOPHAS

0144I have already taken a surfeit.

EPITON

0145Good master, pity them.

TOPHAS

0146Pity them, Epi? No, I do not think that this breast 0147shall be pestered with such a foolish passion. What is that 0148the gentlewoman carrieth in a chain?

EPITON

0149Why, it is a squirrel.

TOPHAS

0150A squirrel? O gods, what things are made for money!

[The pages and maids speak confidentially to one another.]

DARES

0151Is not this gentleman overwise?

FAVILLA

0152I could stay all day with him if I feared not to be 0153shent.

SCINTILLA

0154Is it not possible to meet again?

DARES

0155Yes, at any time.

FAVILLA

0156Then let us hasten home.

SCINTILLA

0157[Aloud] Sir Tophas, the god of war deal better with 0158you than you do with the god of love.

FAVILLA

0159Our love we may dissemble, disgest we cannot; but I 0160doubt not but time will hamper you and help us.

TOPHAS

0161I defy time, who hath no interest in my heart. 0162–Come, Epi, let me to the battle with that hideous beast. 0163Love is pap, and hath no relish in my taste because it is 0164not terrible.

[Exeunt Sir Tophas and Epiton.]

DARES

0165Indeed, a black sheep is a perilous beast. But let us in 0166till another time.

FAVILLA

0167I shall long for that time.

Exeunt.

Actus Secundus, Scaena Tertia

[Enter] Endymion, [near the lunary bank; and, unseen by him,] Dipsas [and] Bagoa.

ENDYMION

0001No rest, Endymion? Still uncertain how to settle 0002thy steps by day or thy thoughts by night? Thy truth is 0003measured by thy fortune, and thou art judged unfaithful 0004because thou art unhappy. I will see if I can beguile 0005myself with sleep; and, if no slumber will take hold in my 0006eyes, yet will I embrace the golden thoughts in my head 0007and wish to melt by musing, that as ebony, which no fire 0008can scorch, is yet consumed with sweet savours, so my 0009heart, which cannot be bent by the hardness of fortune, 0010may be bruised by amorous desires. On yonder bank 0011never grew anything but lunary, and hereafter I will never 0012have any bed but that bank. O Endymion, Tellus was fair! 0013But what availeth beauty without wisdom? Nay, 0014Endymion, she was wise. But what availeth wisdom without 0015honour? She was honourable, Endymion, belie her 0016not. Ay, but how obscure is honour without fortune? Was 0017she not fortunate, whom so many followed? Yes, yes, but 0018base is fortune without majesty. Thy majesty, Cynthia, all 0019the world knoweth and wondereth at, but not one in the 0020world that can imitate it or comprehend it. No more, 0021Endymion! Sleep or die. Nay, die, for to sleep it is 0022impossible; and yet, I know not how it cometh to pass, I 0023feel such a heaviness both in mine eyes and heart that I 0024am suddenly benumbed, yea, in every joint. It may be 0025weariness, for when did I rest? It may be deep melancholy, 0026for when did I not sigh? Cynthia, ay so, I say, Cynthia!

He falls asleep.

DIPSAS

0027[Advancing] Little dost thou know, Endymion, when 0028thou shalt wake; for, hadst thou placed thy heart as low in 0029love as thy head lieth now in sleep, thou mightiest have 0030commanded Tellus, whom now instead of a mistress 0031thou shalt find a tomb. These eyes must I seal up by art, 0032not nature, which are to be opened neither by art nor 0033nature. Thou that layest down with golden locks shalt not 0034awake until they be turned to silver hairs; and that chin, 0035on which scarcely appeareth soft down, shall be filled 0036with bristles as hard as broom. Thou shalt sleep out thy 0037youth and flowering time and become dry hay before 0038thou knewest thyself green grass, and ready by age to step 0039into the grave when thou wakest, that was youthful in the 0040court when thou laidst thee down to sleep. The malice of 0041Tellus hath brought this to pass, which, if she could not 0042have commanded by menacing; for from her gather we all our 0043simples to maintain our sorceries. [To Bagoa] Fan with 0044this hemlock over his face, and sing the enchantment for 0045sleep, whilst I go in and finish those ceremonies that are 0046required in our art. Take heed ye touch not his face, for 0047the fan is so seasoned that whoso it toucheth with a leaf 0048shall presently die, and over whom the wind of it 0049breatheth, he shall sleep for ever.

Exit.

BAGOA

0050Let me alone, I will be careful. [She fans Endymion as she sings.] 0051What hap hadst thou, Endymion, to come 0052under the hands of Dipsas? O fair Endymion, how it 0053grieveth me that that fair face must be turned to a withered 0054skin and taste the pains of death before it feel the 0055reward of love! I fear Tellus will repent that which the 0056heavens themselves seemed to rue. –But I hear Dipsas 0057coming. I dare not repine, lest she make me pine, and 0058rock me into such a deep sleep that I shall not awake to 0059my marriage.

Enter Dipsas.

DIPSAS

0060How now, have you finished?

BAGOA

0061Yea.

DIPSAS

0062Well, then, let us in, and see that you do not so much 0063as whisper that I did this; for if you do, I will turn thy 0064hairs to adders and all thy teeth in thy head to tongues. 0065Come away, come away.

Exeunt[, leaving Endymion asleep].

A Dumb Show

Music sounds. Three Ladies enter, one with a knife and a looking-glass, who, by procurement of one of the other two, offers to stab Endymion as he sleeps, but the third wrings her hands, lamenteth, offering still to prevent it, but dares not. At last, the first lady, looking in the glass, casts down the knife. Exeunt [the Ladies]. Enters an ancient Man with books with three leaves, offers the same twice. Endymion refuseth. He rendeth two and offers the third, where he stands a while, and then Endymion offers to take it. Exit [the old Man. Endymion remains sleeping on the lunary bank, curtained off from view.]
