Act IV

Scene i

[Enter Ferdinand and Bosola]

FERDINAND.

1How doth our sister Duchess bear herself
2In her imprisonment?

BOSOLA.

In her imprisonment? Nobly: I’ll describe her:
3She’s sad, as one long used to’t: and she seems
4Rather to welcome the end of misery
5Than shun it: a behaviour so noble,
6As gives a majesty to adversity:
7You may discern the shape of loveliness
8More perfect in her tears, than in her smiles;
9She will muse four hours together: and her silence,
10Methinks, expresseth more than if she spake.

FERDINAND.

11Her melancholy seems to be fortified
12With a strange disdain.

BOSOLA.

With a strange disdain. ’Tis so: and this restraint
13(Like English mastives, that grow fierce with tying)
14Makes her too passionately apprehend
15Those pleasures she’s kept from.

FERDINAND.

Those pleasures she’s kept from. Curse upon her!
16I will no longer study in the book
17Of another’s heart: inform her what I told you.

Exit.

[Bosola draws the traverse to reveal the Dutchess, Cariola and Servants.]

BOSOLA.

18All comfort to your Grace;―

DUCHESS.

All comfort to your Grace;― I will have none.
19’Pray-thee, why dost thou wrap thy poisoned pills
20In gold and sugar?

BOSOLA.

21Your elder brother the Lord Ferdinand
22Is come to visit you: and sends you word
23’Cause once he rashly made a solemn vow
24Never to see you more; he comes i’th’ night;
25And prays you, gently, neither torch nor taper
26Shine in your chamber: he will kiss your hand;
27And reconcile himself: but, for his vow,
28He dares not see you.

DUCHESS.

He dares not see you. At his pleasure.
29Take hence the lights: he’s come.

[Exeunt servants with lights; enter Ferdinand.]

FERDINAND.

30Where are you?

DUCHESS.

Where are you? Here, sir.

FERDINAND.

31This darkness suits you well.

DUCHESS.

This darkness suits you well. I would ask you pardon.

FERDINAND.

32You have it;
33For I account it the honourabl’st revenge
34Where I may kill, to pardon: where are your cubs?

DUCHESS.

35Whom?

FERDINAND.

36Call them your children;
37For though our national law distinguish bastards
38From true legitimate issue, compassionate nature
39Makes them all equal.

DUCHESS.

Makes them all equal. Do you visit me for this?
40You violate a sacrament o’th’ Church
41Shall make you howl in hell for’t.

FERDINAND.

Shall make you howl in hell for’t. It had been well,
42Could you have lived thus always, for indeed
43You were too much i’th’ light. But no more;
44I come to seal my peace with you: here’s a hand,
45(gives her a dead man’s hand.) To which you have vowed much love: the ring upon’t
46You gave.

DUCHESS.

You gave. I affectionately kiss it.

FERDINAND.

47Pray do: and bury the print of it in your heart.
48I will leave this ring with you, for a love-token:
49And the hand, as sure as the ring: and do not doubt
50But you shall have the heart too. When you need a friend
51Send it to him that owed it: you shall see
52Whether he can aid you.

DUCHESS.

Whether he can aid you. You are very cold.
53I fear you are not well after your travel:
54Ha? Lights: Oh horrible!

FERDINAND.

55Let her have lights enough.

[Exit.

[Enter servants with lights.]

DUCHESS.

56What witchcraft doth he practice, that he hath left
57A dead man’s hand here?―

Here is discovered, behind a traverse, the artificial figures of Antonio and his children; appearing as if they were dead .

BOSOLA.

58Look you: here’s the piece from which ’twas tane;
59He doth present you this sad spectacle,
60That now you know directly they are dead,
61Hereafter you may, wisely, cease to grieve
62For that which cannot be recovered.

DUCHESS.

63There is not between heaven and earth one wish
64I stay for after this: it wastes me more,
65Than were’t my picture, fashioned out of wax,
66Stuck with a magical needle, and then buried
67In some foul dunghill: And yond’s an excellent property
68For a tyrant, which I would account mercy,―

BOSOLA.

69What’s that?

DUCHESS.

70If they would bind me to that lifeless trunk,
71And let me freeze to death.

BOSOLA.

And let me freeze to death. Come, you must live.

DUCHESS.

72That’s the greatest torture souls feel in hell,
73In hell: that they must live, and cannot die.
74Portia, I’ll new kindle thy coals again,
75And revive the rare and almost dead example
76Of a loving wife.

BOSOLA.

Of a loving wife. O fie! despair? remember
77You are a Christian.

DUCHESS.

You are a Christian. The Church enjoins fasting:
78I’ll starve myself to death.

BOSOLA.

I’ll starve myself to death. Leave this vain sorrow;
79Things being at the worst, begin to mend:
80The bee when he hath shot his sting into your hand
81May then play with your eyelid.

DUCHESS.

May then play with your eyelid. Good comfortable fellow
82Persuade a wretch that’s broke upon the wheel
83To have all his bones new set: entreat him live,
84To be executed again. Who must dispatch me?
85I account this world a tedious theatre,
86For I do play a part in’t ’gainst my will.

BOSOLA.

87Come, be of comfort, I will save your life.

DUCHESS.

88Indeed I have not leisure to tend so small a business.

BOSOLA.

89Now, by my life, I pity you.

DUCHESS.

Now, by my life, I pity you. Thou art a fool then,
90To waste thy pity on a thing so wretched
91As cannot pity itself. I am full of daggers.
92Puff! let me blow these vipers from me.

[Enter Servant.]

93What are you?

SERVANT.

What are you? One that wishes you long life.

DUCHESS.

94I would thou wert hanged for the horrible curse
95Thou hast given me: I shall shortly grow one
96Of the miracles of pity. I’ll go pray. No,
97I’ll go curse.

BOSOLA.

I’ll go curse. Oh, fie!

DUCHESS.

98I could curse the stars.

BOSOLA.

I could curse the stars. Oh, fearful!

DUCHESS.

99And those three smiling seasons of the year
100Into a Russian winter: nay the world
101To its first chaos.

BOSOLA.

102Look you, the stars shine still.

DUCHESS.

Look you, the stars shine still. Oh, but you must
103Remember, my curse hath a great way to go:
104Plagues, that make lanes through largest families,
105Consume them.

BOSOLA.

Consume them. Fie lady!

DUCHESS.

Consume them. Fie lady! Let them like tyrants
106Never be remembered, but for the ill they have done:
107Let all the zealous prayers of mortified
108Churchmen forget them,―

BOSOLA.

Churchmen forget them,― O uncharitable!

DUCHESS.

109Let Heaven, a little while, cease crowning martyrs,
110To punish them.
111Go, howl them this: and say I long to bleed.
112It is some mercy when men kill with speed .

Exit [with servants]

[Enter Ferdinand. ]

FERDINAND.

113Excellent; as I would wish: she’s plagued in art.
114These presentations are but framed in wax
115By the curious master in that quality,
116Vincentio Lauriola, and she takes them
117For true substantial bodies.

BOSOLA.

For true substantial bodies. Why do you do this?

FERDINAND.

118To bring her to despair.

BOSOLA.

To bring her to despair. ’Faith, end here;
119And go no farther in your cruelty,
120Send her a penitential garment, to put on
121Next to her delicate skin, and furnish her
122With beads and prayer books.

FERDINAND.

With beads and prayer books. Damn her! that body of hers,
123While that my blood ran pure in’t, was more worth
124Than that which thou wouldst comfort, called a soul.
125I will send her masques of common courtesans,
126Have her meat served up by bawds and ruffians,
127And, ’cause she’ll needs be mad, I am resolved
128To remove forth the common hospital
129All the mad folk, and place them near her lodging:
130There let them practice together, sing, and dance,
131And act their gambols to the full o’th’ moon:
132If she can sleep the better for it, let her.
133Your work is almost ended.

BOSOLA.

Your work is almost ended. Must I see her again?

FERDINAND.

134Yes.

BOSOLA.

Yes. Never.

FERDINAND.

Yes. Never. You must.

BOSOLA.

Yes. Never. You must. Never in mine own shape;
135That’s forfeited by my intelligence,
136And this last cruel lie: when you send me next,
137The business shall be comfort.

FERDINAND.

The business shall be comfort. Very likely:
138Thy pity is nothing of kin to thee. Antonio
139Lurks about Milan; thou shalt shortly thither,
140To feed a fire as great as my revenge,
141Which nev’r will slack, till it have spent his fuel;
142Intemperate agues make physicians cruel

Exeunt.

Scene ii

[Enter Duchess and Cariola.]

DUCHESS.

1What hideous noise was that?

CARIOLA.

What hideous noise was that? ’Tis the wild consort
2Of madmen, lady, which your tyrant brother
3Hath placed about your lodging. This tyranny,
4I think, was never practiced till this hour.

DUCHESS.

5Indeed I thank him: nothing but noise, and folly
6Can keep me in my right wits, whereas reason
7And silence make me stark mad. Sit down,
8Discourse to me some dismal tragedy.

CARIOLA.

9O ’twill increase your melancholy.

DUCHESS.

O ’twill increase your melancholy. Thou art deceived;
10To hear of greater grief would lessen mine.
11This is a prison?

CARIOLA.

This is a prison? Yes, but you shall live
12To shake this durance off.

DUCHESS.

To shake this durance off. Thou art a fool:
13The robin red-breast and the nightingale
14Never live long in cages.

CARIOLA.

Never live long in cages. Pray dry your eyes.
15What think you of Madam?

DUCHESS.

What think you of Madam? Of nothing:
16When I muse thus, I sleep.

CARIOLA.

17Like a madman, with your eyes open?

DUCHESS.

18Dost thou think we shall know one another
19In th’other world?

CARIOLA.

In th’other world? Yes, out of question.

DUCHESS.

20O that it were possible we might
21But hold some two days’ conference with the dead,
22From them I should learn somewhat, I am sure
23I never shall know here. I'll tell thee a miracle,
24I am not mad yet, to my cause of sorrow.
25Th’heaven o'er my head seems made of molten brass,
26The earth of flaming sulphur, yet I am not mad.
27I am acquainted with sad misery,
28As the tanned galley-slave is with his oar.
29Necessity makes me suffer constantly.
30And custom makes it easy. Who do I look like now?

CARIOLA.

31Like to your picture in the gallery,
32A deal of life in show, but none in practice:
33Or rather like some reverend monument
34Whose ruins are even pitied.

DUCHESS.

Whose ruins are even pitied. Very proper:
35And Fortune seems only to have her eyesight,
36To behold my tragedy.
37How now! what noise is that?

[Enter] Servant.

SERVANT.

How now! what noise is that? I am come to tell you,
38Your brother hath intended you some sport.
39A great physician when the Pope was sick
40Of a deep melancholy, presented him
41With several sorts of madmen, which wild object,
42Being full of change and sport, forced him to laugh,
43And so th’impostume broke: the selfsame cure
44The Duke intends on you.

DUCHESS.

The Duke intends on you. Let them come in.

SERVANT.

45There's a mad lawyer, and a secular priest,
46A doctor that hath forfeited his wits
47By jealousy; an astrologian,
48That in his works said such a day o’th’ month
49Should be the day of doom; and, failing of’t,
50Ran mad; an English tailor, crazed i’th’ brain
51With the study of new fashion; a gentleman usher
52Quite beside himself with care to keep in mind
53The number of his lady's salutations,
54Or ‘How do you?’ she employed him in each morning:
55A farmer too, an excellent knave in grain,
56Mad, 'cause he was hindered transportation;
57And let one broker, that’s mad, loose to these,
58You'ld think the devil were among them.

DUCHESS.

59Sit Cariola: let them loose when you please,
60For I am chained to endure all your tyranny.

[Enter Madmen.]

Here, by a madman, this song is sung to a dismal kind of music. / O let us howl, some heavy note, / Some deadly-doggèd howl, / Sounding, as from the threat’ning of beasts, and fatal fowl. / As ravens, screech-owls, bulls, and bears, / We’ll bill, and bawl our parts, / Till yerksome noise, have cloyed your ears, / and corrosived your hearts. / At last when as our choir wants breath, / our bodies being blest, / We’ll sing like swans, to welcome death, / and die in love and rest . throat,

MAD ASTROLOGER.

Doomsday not come yet? I’ll draw it nearer by a perspective, or make a glass, that shall set all the world on fire upon an instant. I cannot sleep, my pillow is stufft with a litter of porcupines.

MAD LAWYER.

Hell is a mere glass-house, where the devils are continually blowing up women’s souls on hollow irons, and the fire never goes out.

MAD PRIEST.

I will lie with every woman in my parish the tenth night: I will tithe them over like haycocks.

MAD DOCTOR.

Shall my pothecary outgo me, because I am a cuckold? I have found out his roguery: he makes allum of his wife’s urine, and sells it to Puritans, that have sore throats with over-straining.

MAD ASTROLOGER.

I have skill in heraldry.

MAD LAWYER.

Hast?

MAD ASTROLOGER.

You do give for your crest a woodcock’s head, with the brains picked out on’t. You are a very ancient gentleman.

MAD PRIEST.

Greek is turned Turk; we are only to be saved by the Helvetian translation.

MAD ASTROLOGER

[to lawyer]. Come on sir, I will lay the law to you.

MAD LAWYER.

Oh, rather lay a corrosive, the law will eat to the bone.

MAD PRIEST.

He that drinks but to satisfy nature is damned.

MAD DOCTOR.

If I had my glass here, I would show a sight should make all the women here call me mad doctor.

MAD ASTROLOGER

[pointing to the Priest]. What’s he, a rope- maker?

MAD LAWYER.

No, no, no, a snuffling knave, that while he shows the tombs, will have his hand in a wench’s placket.

MAD PRIEST.

Woe to the caroche that brought home my wife from the masque, at three o’clock in the morning; it had a large featherbed in it.

MAD DOCTOR.

I have pared the devil’s nails forty times, roasted them in ravens’ eggs, and cured agues with them.

MAD PRIEST.

Get me three hundred milch bats, to make possets to procure sleep.

MAD DOCTOR.

All the college may throw their caps at me, I have made a soap-boiler costive: it was my masterpiece:―

Here the dance, consisting of 8. madmen, with music answerable thereunto, after which Bosola, like an old man, enters.

DUCHESS.

101Is he mad too?

SERVANT.

Is he mad too? Pray question him; I'll leave you.

[Exeunt Servant and Madmen]

BOSOLA.

102I am come to make thy tomb.

DUCHESS.

I am come to make thy tomb. Ha! my tomb?
103Thou speak’st as if I lay upon my death-bed,
104Gasping for breath: dost thou perceive me sick?

BOSOLA.

Yes, and the more dangerously, since thy sickness is insensible.

DUCHESS.

Thou art not mad, sure; dost know me?

BOSOLA.

Yes.

DUCHESS.

Who am I?

BOSOLA.

Thou art a box of worm seed, at best, but a sal- vatory of green mummy: what’s this flesh? a little cruded milk, fantastical puff-paste: our bodies are weaker than those paper prisons boys use to keep flies in: more contemptible; since ours is to preserve earth-worms: didst thou ever see a lark in a cage? such is the soul in the body: this world is like her little turf of grass, and the heaven o’er our heads, like her looking-glass, only gives us a miserable knowledge of the small compass of our prison.

DUCHESS.

Am not I thy Duchess?

BOSOLA.

Thou art some great woman, sure; for riot begins to sit on thy forehead (clad in gray hairs) twenty years sooner than on a merry milkmaid’s. Thou sleep’st worse, than if a mouse should be forced to take up her lodging in a cat’s ear: a little infant, that breeds its teeth, should it lie with thee, would cry out as if thou wert the more unquiet bedfellow.

DUCHESS.

I am Duchess of Malfi still.

BOSOLA.

That makes thy sleeps so broken:

127Glories, like glow-worms, afar off shine bright,
128But looked to near, have neither heat nor light.

DUCHESS.

Thou art very plain.

BOSOLA.

My trade is to flatter the dead, not the living; I am a tomb-maker.

DUCHESS.

And thou com’st to make my tomb?

BOSOLA.

Yes.

DUCHESS.

134Let me be a little merry;
135Of what stuff wilt thou make it?

BOSOLA.

136Nay, resolve me first, of what fashion?

DUCHESS.

Why, do we grow fantastical in our death-bed? Do we affect fashion in the grave?

BOSOLA.

139Most ambitiously. Princes’ images on their tombs
140Do not lie as they were wont, seeming to pray
141Up to Heaven: but with their hands under their cheeks,
142As if they died of the tooth-ache; they are not carved
143With their eyes fixed upon the stars; but as
144Their minds were wholly bent upon the world,
145The self-same way they seem to turn their faces.

DUCHESS.

146Let me know fully therefore the effect
147Of this thy dismal preparation,
148This talk, fit for a charnel.

BOSOLA.

This talk, fit for a charnel. Now I shall;

[Enter Executioners with] a coffin, cords, and a bell.

149Here is a present from your princely brothers,
150And may it arrive welcome, for it brings
151Last benefit, last sorrow.

DUCHESS.

Last benefit, last sorrow. Let me see it.
152I have so much obedience, in my blood,
153I wish it in their veins, to do them good.

BOSOLA.

154This is your last presence chamber.

CARIOLA.

155O my sweet lady!

DUCHESS.

O my sweet lady! Peace; it affrights not me.

BOSOLA.

156I am the common bellman,
157That usually is sent to condemned persons,
158The night before they suffer.

DUCHESS.

The night before they suffer. Even now thou said’st
159Thou wast a tomb-maker?

BOSOLA.

Thou wast a tomb-maker? ’Twas to bring you
160By degrees to mortification. Listen:
161[rings the bell] Hark, now everything is still,
162The screech-owl and the whistler shrill
163Call upon our Dame, aloud,
164And bid her quickly don her shroud.
165Much you had of land and rent,
166Your length in clay’s now competent.
167A long war disturbed your mind,
168Here your perfect peace is signed.
169Of what is’t fools make such vain keeping?
170Sin their conception, their birth, weeping:
171Their life, a general mist of error,
172Their death, a hideous storm of terror.
173Strew your hair with powders sweet:
174Don clean linen, bath your feet,
175And, the foul fiend more to check,
176A crucifix let bless your neck.
177’Tis now full tide ’tween night and day,
178End your groan, and come away.

[Executioners approach.]

CARIOLA.

179Hence villains, tyrants, murderers. Alas!
180What will you do with my lady? Call for help.

DUCHESS.

181To whom, to our next neighbours? They are mad-folks.

BOSOLA.

182Remove that noise.

[Executioners seize Cariola, who struggles.]

DUCHESS.

Remove that noise. Farewell Cariola,
183In my last will I have not much to give;
184A many hungry guests have fed upon me,
185Thine will be a poor reversion.

CARIOLA.

Thine will be a poor reversion. I will die with her.

DUCHESS.

186I pray thee, look thou giv’st my little boy
187Some syrup for his cold, and let the girl
188Say her prayers, ere she sleep.

[Cariola is forced off.]

Say her prayers, ere she sleep. Now, what you please,
189What death?

BOSOLA.

What death? Strangling: here are your executioners.

DUCHESS.

190I forgive them:
191The apoplexy, catarrh, or cough o'th’ lungs
192Would do as much as they do.

BOSOLA.

193Doth not death fright you?

DUCHESS.

Doth not death fright you? Who would be afraid on’t?
194Knowing to meet such excellent company
195In th’other world?

BOSOLA.

In th’other world? Yet, methinks,
196The manner of your death should much afflict you,
197This cord should terrify you?

DUCHESS.

This cord should terrify you? Not a whit:
198What would it pleasure me, to have my throat cut
199With diamonds? or to be smothered
200With cassia? or to be shot to death, with pearls?
201I know death hath ten thousand several doors
202For men to take their Exits : and ’tis found
203They go on such strange geometrical hinges,
204You may open them both ways: any way, for Heaven sake,
205So I were out of your whispering. Tell my brothers
206That I perceive death, now I am well awake,
207Best gift is, they can give, or I can take.
208I would fain put off my last woman's fault,
209I’ll’d not be tedious to you.

EXECUTIONERS.

I’ll’d not be tedious to you. We are ready.

DUCHESS.

210Dispose my breath how please you, but my body
211Bestow upon my women, will you?

EXECUTIONERS.

Bestow upon my women, will you? Yes.

DUCHESS.

212Pull, and pull strongly, for your able strength
213Must pull down heaven upon me:
214Yet stay, heaven gates are not so highly arched
215As princes’ palaces: they that enter there
216Must go upon their knees. Come violent death,
217Serve for mandragora to make me sleep;
218Go tell my brothers, when I am laid out,
219They then may feed in quiet.

They strangle her.

BOSOLA.

They then may feed in quiet. Where's the waiting woman?
220Fetch her. Some other strangle the children.

[Exeunt Executioners. Enter one with Cariola.]

221Look you, there sleeps your mistress.

CARIOLA.

Look you, there sleeps your mistress. O you are damned
222Perpetually for this. My turn is next,
223Is’t not so ordered?

BOSOLA.

Is’t not so ordered? Yes, and I am glad
224You are so well prepared for’t.

CARIOLA.

You are so well prepared for’t. You are deceived sir,
225I am not prepared for't. I will not die,
226I will first come to my answer; and know
227How I have offended.

BOSOLA.

How I have offended. Come, dispatch her.
228You kept her counsel, now you shall keep ours.

CARIOLA.

229I will not die, I must not, I am contracted
230To a young gentleman.

EXECUTIONER

To a young gentleman. [showing the noose]. Here’s your wedding-ring.

CARIOLA.

231Let me but speak with the Duke. I'll discover
232Treason to his person.

BOSOLA.

Treason to his person. Delays: throttle her.

EXECUTIONER.

233She bites: and scratches.

CARIOLA.

She bites: and scratches. If you kill me now
234I am damned. I have not been at confession
235This two years.

BOSOLA.

This two years. When!

CARIOLA.

236I am quick with child.

BOSOLA.

I am quick with child. Why then,
237Your credit’s saved: bear her into th' next room.
238Let this lie still.

[Executioners strangle Cariola and exeunt with her body.

Enter Ferdinand.]

FERDINAND.

239Is she dead?

BOSOLA.

Is she dead? She is what
240You’ll’d have her. But here begin your pity,

[Bosola draws the traverse and] Shows the children strangled.

241Alas, how have these offended?

FERDINAND.

Alas, how have these offended? The death
242Of young wolves is never to be pitied.

BOSOLA.

243Fix your eye here.

FERDINAND.

Fix your eye here. Constantly.

BOSOLA.

Fix your eye here. Constantly. Do you not weep?
244Other sins only speak; murther shrieks out:
245The element of water moistens the earth,
246But blood flies upwards, and bedews the heavens.

FERDINAND.

247Cover her face. Mine eyes dazzle: she died young.

BOSOLA.

248I think not so: her infelicity
249Seemed to have years too many.

FERDINAND.

Seemed to have years too many. She and I were twins:
250And should I die this instant, I had lived
251Her time to a minute.

BOSOLA.

Her time to a minute. It seems she was born first:
252You have bloodily approved the ancient truth,
253That kindred commonly do worse agree
254Than remote strangers.

FERDINAND.

Than remote strangers. Let me see her face again.
255Why didst not thou pity her? What an excellent
256Honest man might’st thou have been
257If thou hadst borne her to some sanctuary!
258Or, bold in a good cause, opposed thyself
259With thy advanced sword above thy head,
260Between her innocence and my revenge!
261I bade thee, when I was distracted of my wits,
262Go kill my dearest friend, and thou hast done’t.
263For let me but examine well the cause;
264What was the meanness of her match to me?
265Only I must confess, I had a hope,
266Had she continued widow, to have gained
267An infinite mass of treasure by her death:
268And that was the main cause; her marriage,
269That drew a stream of gall quite through my heart;
270For thee, (as we observe in tragedies
271That a good actor many times is cursed
272For playing a villain’s part) I hate thee for't:
273And, for my sake, say thou hast done much ill, well.

BOSOLA.

274Let me quicken your memory: for I perceive
275You are falling into ingratitude. I challenge
276The reward due to my service.

FERDINAND.

The reward due to my service. I'll tell thee,
277What I'll give thee—

BOSOLA.

What I'll give thee— Do.

FERDINAND.

What I'll give thee— Do. I'll give thee a pardon
278For this murder.

BOSOLA.

For this murder. Ha?

FERDINAND.

For this murder. Ha? Yes: and ’tis
279The largest bounty I can study to do thee.
280By what authority didst thou execute
281This bloody sentence?

BOSOLA.

This bloody sentence? By yours.

FERDINAND.

This bloody sentence? By yours. Mine? Was I her judge?
282Did any ceremonial form of law
283Doom her to not-being? did a complete jury
284Deliver her conviction up i’th’ court?
285Where shalt thou find this judgement registered
286Unless in hell? See: like a bloody fool
287Th’hast forfeited thy life, and thou shalt die for't.

BOSOLA.

288The office of justice is perverted quite
289When one thief hangs another: who shall dare
290To reveal this?

FERDINAND.

To reveal this? Oh, I'll tell thee:
291The wolf shall find her grave, and scrape it up;
292Not to devour the corpse, but to discover
293The horrid murther.

BOSOLA.

The horrid murther. You; not I shall quake for't.

FERDINAND.

294Leave me.

BOSOLA.

Leave me. I will first receive my pension.

FERDINAND.

295You are a villain.

BOSOLA.

You are a villain. When your ingratitude
296Is judge, I am so―

FERDINAND.

Is judge, I am so― O horror!
297That not the fear of Him which binds the devils
298Can prescribe man obedience.
299Never look upon me more.

BOSOLA.

Never look upon me more. Why fare thee well:
300Your brother and yourself are worthy men;
301You have a pair of hearts are hollow graves,
302Rotten, and rotting others: and your vengeance,
303Like two chained bullets, still goes arm in arm;
304You may be brothers: for treason, like the plague,
305Doth take much in a blood. I stand like one
306That long hath tane a sweet and golden dream.
307I am angry with myself, now that I wake.

FERDINAND.

308Get thee into some unknown part o’th’ world
309That I may never see thee.

BOSOLA.

That I may never see thee. Let me know
310Wherefore I should be thus neglected? Sir,
311I served your tyranny: and rather strove
312To satisfy yourself, than all the world;
313And though I loathed the evil, yet I loved
314You that did counsel it: and rather sought
315To appear a true servant than an honest man.

FERDINAND.

316I'll go hunt the badger by owl-light:
317'Tis a deed of darkness. Exit.

BOSOLA.

318He’s much distracted. Off my painted honour!
319While with vain hopes our faculties we tire,
320We seem to sweat in ice and freeze in fire;
321What would I do, were this to do again?
322I would not change my peace of conscience
323For all the wealth of Europe. She stirs; here’s life.
324Return, fair soul, from darkness, and lead mine
325Out of this sensible hell. She’s warm, she breathes:
326Upon thy pale lips I will melt my heart
327To store them with fresh colour. Who’s there?
328Some cordial drink! Alas! I dare not call:
329So pity would destroy pity: her eye opes,
330And heaven in it seems to ope, that late was shut,
331To take me up to mercy.

DUCHESS.

To take me up to mercy. Antonio!

BOSOLA.

332Yes, Madam, he is living,
333The dead bodies you saw were but feigned statues;
334He’s reconciled to your brothers: the Pope hath wrought
335The atonement.

DUCHESS.

The atonement. Mercy.

she dies.

BOSOLA.

336Oh, she’s gone again: there the cords of life broke.
337Oh sacred innocence, that sweetly sleeps
338On turtles’ feathers: whilst a guilty conscience
339Is a black register, wherein is writ
340All our good deeds and bad; a perspective
341That shows us hell; that we cannot be suffered
342To do good when we have a mind to it!
343This is manly sorrow:
344These tears, I am very certain, never grew
345In my mother’s milk. My estate is sunk
346Below the degree of fear: where were
347These penitent fountains while she was living?
348Oh, they were frozen up: here is a sight
349As direful to my soul as is the sword
350Unto a wretch hath slain his father. Come,
351I’ll bear thee hence,
352And execute thy last will; that’s deliver
353Thy body to the reverend dispose
354Of some good women: that the cruel tyrant
355Shall not deny me. Then I’ll post to Milan,
356Where somewhat I will speedily enact
357Worth my dejection.

Exit [carrying the body].
