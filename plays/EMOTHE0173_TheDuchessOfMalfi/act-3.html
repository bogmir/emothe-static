Act III

Act III, Scene i

[Enter Antonio and Delio]

ANTONIO.

1Our noble friend, my most beloved Delio,
2Oh, you have been a stranger long at court.
3Came you along with the Lord Ferdinand?

DELIO.

4I did, sir. And how fares your noble Duchess?

ANTONIO.

5Right fortunately well. She’s an excellent
6Feeder of pedigrees: since you last saw her,
7She hath had two children more, a son and daughter.

DELIO.

8Methinks ’twas yesterday. Let me but wink,
9And not behold your face, which to mine eye
10Is somewhat leaner: verily I should dream
11It were within this half hour.

ANTONIO.

12You have not been in law, friend Delio,
13Nor in prison, nor a suitor at the court,
14Nor begged the reversion of some great man’s place,
15Nor troubled with an old wife, which doth make
16Your time so insensibly hasten.

DELIO.

Your time so insensibly hasten. Pray sir tell me,
17Hath not this news arrived yet to the ear
18Of the Lord Cardinal?

ANTONIO.

Of the Lord Cardinal? I fear it hath.
19The Lord Ferdinand, that’s newly come to court,
20Doth bear himself right dangerously.

DELIO.

Doth bear himself right dangerously. Pray, why?

ANTONIO.

21He is so quiet, that he seems to sleep
22The tempest out, as dormice do in winter;
23Those houses, that are haunted, are most still,
24Till the devil be up.

DELIO.

Till the devil be up. What say the common people?

ANTONIO.

25The common rabble do directly say
26She is a strumpet.

DELIO.

She is a strumpet. And your graver heads,
27Which would be politic, what censure they?

ANTONIO.

28They do observe I grow to infinite purchase
29The left-hand way, and all suppose the Duchess
30Would amend it, if she could. For, say they,
31Great princes, though they grudge their officers
32Should have such large and unconfined means
33To get wealth under them, will not complain
34Lest thereby they should make them odious
35Unto the people: for other obligation
36Of love, or marriage, between her and me,
37They never dream of.

[Enter Ferdinand, Duchess and Bosola.]

DELIO.

They never dream of. The Lord Ferdinand
38Is going to bed.

FERDINAND.

Is going to bed. I'll instantly to bed,
39For I am weary: I am to bespeak
40A husband for you.

DUCHESS.

A husband for you. For me, sir! pray who is’t?

FERDINAND.

41The great Count Malateste.

DUCHESS.

The great Count Malateste. Fie upon him,
42A count? He’s a mere stick of sugar-candy,
43You may look quite through him: when I choose
44A husband, I will marry for your honour.

FERDINAND.

45You shall do well in’t. How is’t, worthy Antonio?

DUCHESS.

46But, sir, I am to have private conference with you,
47About a scandalous report is spread
48Touching mine honour.

FERDINAND.

Touching mine honour. Let me be ever deaf to’t:
49One of Pasquil’s paper bullets, court calumny,
50A pestilent air, which princes’ palaces
51Are seldom purged of. Yet, say that it were true,
52I pour it in your bosom, my fixed love
53Would strongly excuse, extenuate, nay deny
54Faults were they apparent in you. Go, be safe
55In your own innocency.

DUCHESS.

In your own innocency. Oh blessed comfort,
56This deadly air is purged.

Exeunt [Dutchess, Antonio, Delio.]

FERDINAND.

This deadly air is purged. Her guilt treads on
57Hot burning cultures. Now Bosola,
58How thrives our intelligence?

BOSOLA.

How thrives our intelligence? Sir, uncertainly:
59’Tis rumored she hath had three bastards, but
60By whom, we may go read i’th’ stars.

FERDINAND.

By whom, we may go read i’th’ stars. Why some
61Hold opinion, all things are written there.

BOSOLA.

62Yes, if we could find spectacles to read them;
63I do suspect, there hath been some sorcery
64Used on the Duchess.

FERDINAND.

Used on the Duchess. Sorcery, to what purpose?

BOSOLA.

65To make her dote on some desertless fellow,
66She shames to acknowledge.

FERDINAND.

She shames to acknowledge. Can your faith give way
67To think there’s power in potions, or in charms,
68To make us love, whether we will or no?

BOSOLA.

69Most certainly.

FERDINAND.

70Away, these are mere gulleries, horrid things
71Invented by some cheating mountebanks
72To abuse us. Do you think that herbs, or charms
73Can force the will? Some trials have been made
74In this foolish practice; but the ingredients
75Were lenitive poisons, such as are of force
76To make the patient mad; and straight the witch
77Swears, by equivocation, they are in love.
78The witchcraft lies in her rank blood: this night
79I will force confession from her. You told me
80You had got, within these two days, a false key
81Into her bed-chamber.

BOSOLA.

Into her bed-chamber. I have.

FERDINAND.

82As I would wish.

BOSOLA.

As I would wish. What do you intend to do?

FERDINAND.

83Can you guess?

BOSOLA.

Can you guess? No.

FERDINAND.

Can you guess? No. Do not ask then.
84He that can compass me, and know my drifts,
85May say he hath put a girdle ’bout the world,
86And sounded all her quick-sands.

BOSOLA.

And sounded all her quick-sands. I do not
87Think so.

FERDINAND.

Think so. What do you think then, pray?

BOSOLA.

Think so. What do you think then, pray? That you
88Are your own chronicle too much: and grossly
89Flatter yourself.

FERDINAND.

Flatter yourself. Give me thy hand; I thank thee.
90I never gave pension but to flatterers,
91Till I entertained thee: farewell,
92That friend a great man’s ruin strongly checks,
93Who rails into his belief all his defects.

Exeunt.

Scene ii

[Enter Duchess, Antonio and Cariola]

DUCHESS.

1Bring me the casket hither, and the glass;
2You get no lodging here to-night, my lord.

ANTONIO.

3Indeed, I must persuade one.

DUCHESS.

Indeed, I must persuade one. Very good:
4I hope in time ’twill grow into a custom,
5That noblemen shall come with cap and knee,
6To purchase a night's lodging of their wives.

ANTONIO.

7I must lie here.

DUCHESS.

I must lie here. Must? you are a Lord of mis-rule.

ANTONIO.

8Indeed, my rule is only in the night.

DUCHESS.

9To what use will you put me?

ANTONIO.

To what use will you put me? We'll sleep together.

DUCHESS.

10Alas, what pleasure can two lovers find in sleep?

CARIOLA.

11My Lord, I lie with her often: and I know
12She’ll much disquiet you.

ANTONIO.

She’ll much disquiet you. See, you are complained of.

CARIOLA.

13For she’s the sprawling’st bedfellow.

ANTONIO.

14I shall like her the better for that.

CARIOLA.

15Sir, shall I ask you a question?

ANTONIO.

16I pray thee Cariola.

CARIOLA.

17Wherefore still, when you lie with my lady
18Do you rise so early?

ANTONIO.

Do you rise so early? Labouring men,
19Count the clock oft’nest Cariola,
20Are glad when their task’s ended.

DUCHESS.

Are glad when their task’s ended. I’ll stop your mouth

[kisses him].

[She kisses him.]

ANTONIO.

21Nay, that’s but one, Venus had two soft doves
22To draw her chariot: I must have another
23[kisses her]. When wilt thou marry, Cariola?

CARIOLA.

[kisses her]. When wilt thou marry, Cariola? Never, my lord.

ANTONIO.

24O fie upon this single life: forgo it.
25We read how Daphne, for her peevish flight
26Became a fruitless bay-tree; Sirinx turned
27To the pale empty reed; Anaxarete
28Was frozen into marble: whereas those
29Which married, or proved kind unto their friends
30Were, by a gracious influence, transhaped
31Into the olive, pomegranate, mulberry:
32Became flowers, precious stones, or eminent stars.

CARIOLA.

33This is a vain poetry: but I pray you tell me,
34If there were proposed me wisdom, riches, and beauty,
35In three several young men, which should I choose?

ANTONIO.

36’Tis a hard question. This was Paris’ case
37And he was blind in’t, and there was great cause:
38For how was’t possible he could judge right,
39Having three amorous goddesses in view,
40And they stark naked? ’Twas a motion
41Were able to benight the apprehension
42Of the severest counsellor of Europe.
43Now I look on both your faces, so well formed
44It puts me in mind of a question, I would ask.

CARIOLA.

45What is’t?

ANTONIO.

What is’t? I do wonder why hard favoured ladies
46For the most part, keep worse-favoured waiting-women,
47To attend them, and cannot endure fair ones.

DUCHESS.

48Oh, that’s soon answered.
49Did you ever in your life know an ill painter
50Desire to have his dwelling next door to the shop
51Of an excellent picture-maker? ’Twould disgrace
52His face-making, and undo him. I prithee
53When were we so merry? My hair tangles.

ANTONIO

54Pray thee, Cariola, let’s steal forth the room,aparte
55And let her talk to herself: I have divers timesaparte
56Served her the like, when she hath chafed extremely.aparte
57I love to see her angry: softly Cariola.aparte

Exeunt [Antonio and Cariola.]

DUCHESS.

58Doth not the colour of my hair ’gin to change?
59When I wax gray, I shall have all the court
60Powder their hair with arras, to be like me:
61You have cause to love me, I ent’red you into my heart.
62[Enter Ferdinand, unseen.] Before you would vouchsafe to call for the keys.
63We shall one day have my brothers take you napping.
64Methinks his presence, being now in court,
65Should make you keep your own bed: but you’ll say
66Love mixed with fear is sweetest. I’ll assure you
67You shall get no more children till my brothers
68Consent to be your gossips. Have you lost your tongue?
69[She sees Ferdinand holding a poniard.] ’Tis welcome:
70For know, whether I am doomed to live, or die,
71I can do both like a prince.

Ferdinand gives her a poniard.

FERDINAND.

I can do both like a prince. Die then, quickly.
72Virtue, where art thou hid? What hideous thing
73Is it, that doth eclipse thee?

DUCHESS.

Is it, that doth eclipse thee? Pray sir hear me―

FERDINAND.

74Or is it true, thou art but a bare name,
75And no essential thing?

DUCHESS.

And no essential thing? Sir—

FERDINAND.

76Do not speak.

DUCHESS.

Do not speak. No, sir:
77I will plant my soul in mine ears, to hear you.

FERDINAND.

78Oh most imperfect light of human reason,
79That mak’st us so unhappy, to foresee
80What we can least prevent. Pursue thy wishes:
81And glory in them: there’s in shame no comfort,
82But to be past all bounds and sense of shame.

DUCHESS.

83I pray sir, hear me: I am married―

FERDINAND.

I pray sir, hear me: I am married― So.

DUCHESS.

84Happily, not to your liking: but for that
85Alas: your shears do come untimely now
86To clip the bird’s wings, that’s already flown.
87Will you see my husband?

FERDINAND.

Will you see my husband? Yes, if I could change
88Eyes with a basilisk.

DUCHESS.

Eyes with a basilisk. Sure, you came hither
89By his confederacy.

FERDINAND.

By his confederacy. The howling of a wolf
90Is music to thee, screech-owl; prithee peace.
91Whate’er thou art, that hast enjoyed my sister,
92(For I am sure thou hear’st me), for thine own sake
93Let me not know thee. I came hither prepared
94To work thy discovery: yet am now persuaded
95It would beget such violent effects
96As would damn us both. I would not for ten millions
97I had beheld thee; therefore use all means
98I never may have knowledge of thy name;
99Enjoy thy lust still, and a wretched life,
100On that condition. And for thee, vild woman,
101If thou do wish thy lecher may grow old
102In thy embracements, I would have thee build
103Such a room for him, as our anchorites
104To holier use inhabit. Let not the sun
105Shine on him, till he’s dead. Let dogs and monkeys
106Only converse with him, and such dumb things
107To whom nature denies use to sound his name.
108Do not keep a paraquito, lest she learn it;
109If thou do love him, cut out thine own tongue
110Lest it bewray him.

DUCHESS.

Lest it bewray him. Why might not I marry?
111I have not gone about, in this, to create
112Any new world, or custom.

FERDINAND.

Any new world, or custom. Thou art undone:
113And thou hast tane that massy sheet of lead
114That hid thy husband’s bones, and folded it
115About my heart.

DUCHESS.

About my heart. Mine bleeds for’t.

FERDINAND.

About my heart. Mine bleeds for’t. Thine? thy heart?
116What should I name’t, unless a hollow bullet
117Filled with unquenchable wild-fire?

DUCHESS.

Filled with unquenchable wild-fire? You are in this
118Too strict: and were you not my princely brother
119I would say too willful. My reputation
120Is safe.

FERDINAND.

121Dost thou know what reputation is?
122I’ll tell thee, to small purpose, since th’instruction
123Comes now too late:
124Upon a time Reputation, Love, and Death
125Would travel o’er the world: and it was concluded
126That they should part, and take three several ways.
127Death told them, they should find him in great battles:
128Or cities plagued with plagues. Love gives them counsel
129To inquire for him ’mongst unambitious shepherds,
130Where dowries were not talked of: and sometimes
131’Mongst quiet kindred, that had nothing left
132By their dead parents. ‘Stay’, quoth Reputation,
133‘Do not forsake me: for it is my nature
134If once I part from any man I meet
135I am never found again.’ And so, for you:
136You have shook hands with Reputation,
137And made him invisible. So fare you well.
138I will never see you more.

DUCHESS.

I will never see you more. Why should only I,
139Of all the other princes of the world
140Be cased up, like a holy relic? I have youth,
141And a little beauty.

FERDINAND.

And a little beauty. So you have some virgins,
142That are witches. I will never see thee more.

Exit.

Enter [Cariola and] Antonio with a pistol.

DUCHESS.

143You saw this apparition?

ANTONIO.

You saw this apparition? Yes: we are
144Betrayed; how came he hither? I should turn
145This, to thee, for that.

[points the pistol at Cariola.]

CARIOLA.

This, to thee, for that. Pray sir do: and when
146That you have cleft my heart, you shall read there,
147Mine innocence.

DUCHESS.

Mine innocence. That gallery gave him entrance.

ANTONIO.

148I would this terrible thing would come again,
149That, standing on my guard, I might relate
150My warrantable love. Ha! what means this?

DUCHESS.

151He left this with me.

She shows the poniard.

ANTONIO.

He left this with me. And it seems, did wish
152You would use it on yourself?

DUCHESS.

You would use it on yourself? His action seemed
153To intend so much.

ANTONIO.

To intend so much. This hath a handle to't,
154As well as a point: turn it towards him, and
155So fasten the keen edge in his rank gall. [knocking]
156How now? Who knocks? More earthquakes?

DUCHESS.

How now? Who knocks? More earthquakes? I stand
157As if a mine, beneath my feet, were ready
158To be blown up.

CARIOLA.

To be blown up. 'Tis Bosola.

DUCHESS.

To be blown up. 'Tis Bosola. Away!
159Oh misery! Methinks unjust actions
160Should wear these masks and curtains; and not we.
161You must instantly part hence: I have fashioned it already.

Ex[it] Ant[onio; enter Bosola.]

BOSOLA.

162The Duke your brother is tane up in a whirlwind;
163Hath took horse, and’s rid post to Rome.

DUCHESS.

Hath took horse, and’s rid post to Rome. So late?

BOSOLA.

164He told me, as he mounted into th’ saddle,
165You were undone.

DUCHESS.

You were undone. Indeed, I am very near it.

BOSOLA.

166What’s the matter?

DUCHESS.

167Antonio, the master of our household
168Hath dealt so falsely with me, in’s accounts:
169My brother stood engaged with me for money
170Tane up of certain Neapolitan Jews,
171And Antonio lets the bonds be forfeit.

BOSOLA.

172Strange:
Strange: this is cunning.aparte

DUCHESS.

Strange: this is cunning. And hereupon
173My brother’s bills at Naples are protested
174Against. Call up our officers.

BOSOLA.

Against. Call up our officers. I shall.

Exit.

[Enter Antonio]

DUCHESS.

175The place that you must fly to, is Ancona,
176Hire a house there. I’ll send after you
177My treasure, and my jewels: our weak safety
178Runs upon enginous wheels: short syllables
179Must stand for periods. I must now accuse you
180Of such a feignèd crime, as Tasso calls
181Magnanima mensogna: a noble lie,
182’Cause it must shield our honours: hark, they are coming.

[Enter Bosola and officers.]

ANTONIO.

183Will your Grace hear me?

DUCHESS.

184I have got well by you: you have yielded me
185A million of loss; I am like to inherit
186The people’s curses for your stewardship.
187You had the trick, in audit time to be sick,
188Till I had signed your Quietus ; and that cured you
189Without help of a doctor. Gentlemen,
190I would have this man be an example to you all:
191So shall you hold my favour. I pray let him;
192For h’as done that, alas! you would not think of,
193And, because I intend to be rid of him,
194I mean not to publish. Use your fortune elsewhere.

ANTONIO.

195I am strongly armed to brook my overthrow,
196As commonly men bear with a hard year:
197I will not blame the cause on’t; but do think
198The necessity of my malevolent star
199Procures this, not her humour. O the inconstant
200And rotten ground of service, you may see;
201’Tis ev’n like him that, in a winter night,
202Takes a long slumber, o’er a dying fire
203As loath to part from’t: yet parts thence as cold,
204As when he first sat down.

DUCHESS.

As when he first sat down. We do confiscate,
205Towards the satisfying of your accounts,
206All that you have.

ANTONIO.

All that you have. I am all yours; and ’tis very fit
207All mine should be so.

DUCHESS.

All mine should be so. So, sir; you have your pass.

ANTONIO.

208You may see, gentlemen, what ’tis to serve
209A prince with body and soul.

Exit.

BOSOLA.

Here’s an example for extortion; what moisture is drawn out of the sea, when foul weather comes, pours down, and runs into the sea again.

DUCHESS.

213I would know what are your opinions
214Of this Antonio.

2 OFFICER.

He could not abide to see a pig’s head gaping, I thought your Grace would find him a Jew:

3 OFFICER.

I would you had been his officer, for your own sake.

4 OFFICER.

You would have had more money.

1 OFFICER.

He stopped his ears with black wool: and to those came to him for money said he was thick of hearing.

2 OFFICER.

Some said he was an hermaphrodite, for he could not abide a woman.

4 OFFICER.

How scurvy proud he would look, when the treasury was full. Well, let him go.

1 OFFICER.

Yes, and the chippings of the butt’ry fly after him, to scour his gold chain.

DUCHESS.

227Leave us. What do you think of these?

Exeunt [Officers].

BOSOLA.

228That these are rogues, that in’s prosperity,
229But to have waited on his fortune, could have wished
230His dirty stirrup riveted through their noses:
231And followed after’s mule, like a bear in a ring.
232Would have prostituted their daughters to his lust;
233Made their firstborn intelligencers; thought none happy
234But such as were born under his blessed planet;
235And wore his livery: and do these lice drop off now?
236Well, never look to have the like again;
237He hath left a sort of flatt’ring rogues behind him,
238Their doom must follow. Princes pay flatterers,
239In their own money. Flatterers dissemble their vices,
240And they dissemble their lies, that’s justice.
241Alas, poor gentleman―

DUCHESS.

242Poor! He hath amply filled his coffers.

BOSOLA.

243Sure he was too honest. Pluto the god of riches,
244When he’s sent, by Jupiter, to any man
245He goes limping, to signify that wealth
246That comes on God’s name, comes slowly; but when he’s sent
247On the devil’s errand, he rides post, and comes in by scuttles.
248Let me show you what a most unvalued jewel
249You have, in a wanton humour, thrown away.
250To bless the man shall find him. He was an excellent
251Courtier, and most faithful; a soldier that thought it
252As beastly to know his own value too little,
253As devilish to acknowledge it too much;
254Both his virtue and form deserved a far better fortune:
255His discourse rather delighted to judge itself, than show itself.
256His breast was filled with all perfection,
257And yet it seemed a private whisp’ring room:
258It made so little noise of’t.

DUCHESS.

It made so little noise of’t. But he was basely descended.

BOSOLA.

259Will you make yourself a mercenary herald,
260Rather to examine men’s pedigrees, than virtues?
261You shall want him:
262For know an honest statesman to a prince,
263Is like a cedar, planted by a spring,
264The spring bathes the tree’s root, the grateful tree
265Rewards it with his shadow: you have not done so;
266I would sooner swim to the Bermoothes on
267Two politicians’ rotten bladders, tied
268Together with an intelligencer’s heart string
269Than depend on so changeable a prince’s favour.
270Fare thee well, Antonio, since the malice of the world
271Would needs down with thee, it cannot be said yet
272That any ill happened unto thee,
273Considering thy fall was accompanied with virtue.

DUCHESS.

274Oh, you render me excellent music.

BOSOLA.

Oh, you render me excellent music. Say you?

DUCHESS.

275This good one that you speak of, is my husband.

BOSOLA.

276Do I not dream? Can this ambitious age
277Have so much goodness in’t, as to prefer
278A man merely for worth: without these shadows
279Of wealth, and painted honours? possible?

DUCHESS.

280I have had three children by him.

BOSOLA.

I have had three children by him. Fortunate lady,
281For you have made your private nuptial bed
282The humble and fair seminary of peace.
283No question but many an unbeneficed scholar
284Shall pray for you, for this deed, and rejoice
285That some preferment in the world can yet
286Arise from merit. The virgins of your land,
287That have no dowries, shall hope your example
288Will raise them to rich husbands. Should you want
289Soldiers, ’twould make the very Turks and Moors
290Turn Christians, and serve you for this act.
291Last, the neglected poets of your time,
292In honour of this trophy of a man,
293Raised by that curious engine, your white hand,
294Shall thank you in your grave for’t; and make that
295More reverend than all the cabinets
296Of living princes. For Antonio,
297His fame shall likewise flow from many a pen,
298When heralds shall want coats, to sell to men.

DUCHESS.

299As I taste comfort, in this friendly speech,
300So would I find concealment―

BOSOLA.

So would I find concealment― Oh the secret of my prince,
301Which I will wear on th’inside of my heart.

DUCHESS.

302You shall take charge of all my coin, and jewels,
303And follow him, for he retires himself
304To Ancona.

BOSOLA.

To Ancona. So.

DUCHESS.

To Ancona. So. Whither, within few days,
305I mean to follow thee.

BOSOLA.

I mean to follow thee. Let me think:
306I would wish your Grace to feign a pilgrimage
307To Our Lady of Loretto, scarce seven leagues
308From fair Ancona, so may you depart
309Your country with more honour, and your flight
310Will seem a princely progress, retaining
311Your usual train about you.

DUCHESS.

Your usual train about you. Sir, your direction
312Shall lead me, by the hand.

CARIOLA.

Shall lead me, by the hand. In my opinion,
313She were better progress to the baths at Lucca,
314Or go visit the Spa
315In Germany: for, if you will believe me,
316I do not like this jesting with religion,
317This feigned pilgrimage.

DUCHESS.

This feigned pilgrimage. Thou art a superstitious fool:
318Prepare us instantly for our departure.
319Past sorrows, let us moderately lament them,
320For those to come, seek wisely to prevent them.

Exit [Dutchess with Cariola].

BOSOLA.

321A politician is the devil’s quilted anvil,
322He fashions all sins on him, and the blows
323Are never heard; he may work in a lady’s chamber,
324As here for proof. What rests, but I reveal
325All to my lord? Oh, this base quality
326Of intelligencer! Why, every quality i’th’ world
327Prefers but gain, or commendation:
328Now for this act, I am certain to be raised,
329And men that paint weeds, to the life, are praised .

Exit.

Scene iii

[Enter] Cardinal, Ferdinand, Malateste, Pescara, Silvio, Delio.

CARDINAL.

1Must we turn soldier, then?

MALATESTE.

Must we turn soldier, then? The Emperor,
2Hearing your worth that way, ere you attained
3This reverend garment, joins you in commission
4With the right fortunate soldier, the Marquis of Pescara
5And the famous Lannoy.

CARDINAL.

And the famous Lannoy. He that had the honour
6Of taking the French king prisoner?

MALATESTE.

Of taking the French king prisoner? The same.
7Here’s a plot drawn for a new fortification
8At Naples.

FERDINAND.

At Naples. This great Count Malateste, I perceive
9Hath got employment?

DELIO.

Hath got employment? No employment, my lord,
10A marginal note in the muster book, that he is
11A voluntary lord.

FERDINAND.

A voluntary lord. He’s no soldier?

DELIO.

12He has worn gunpowder, in’s hollow tooth,
13For the tooth-ache.

SILVIO.

14He comes to the leaguer with a full intent
15To eat fresh beef, and garlic; means to stay
16Till the scent be gone, and straight return to court.

DELIO.

17He hath read all the late service,
18As the City chronicle relates it,
19And keeps two painters going, only to express
20Battles in model.

SILVIO.

Battles in model. Then he’ll fight by the book.

DELIO.

Battles in model. Then he’ll fight by the book. By the almanac, I think,
21To choose good days, and shun the critical.
22That’s his mistress’ scarf.

SILVIO.

That’s his mistress’ scarf. Yes, he protests
23He would do much for that taffeta,―

DELIO.

24I think he would run away from a battle
25To save it from taking prisoner.

SILVIO.

To save it from taking prisoner. He is horribly afraid
26Gunpowder will spoil the perfume on’t,―

DELIO.

27I saw a Dutchman break his pate once
28For calling him pot-gun; he made his head
29Have a bore in’t, like a musket.

SILVIO.

30I would he had made a touch-hole to’t.
31He is indeed a guarded sumpter-cloth
32Only for the remove of the court.

[Enter Bosola.]

PESCARA.

33Bosola arrived? What should be the business?
34Some falling out amongst the cardinals?
35These factions amongst great men, they are like
36Foxes, when their heads are divided:
37They carry fire in their tails, and all the country
38About them goes to wrack for’t.

SILVIO.

About them goes to wrack for’t. What’s that Bosola?

DELIO.

I knew him in Padua, a fantastical scholar, like such who study to know how many knots was in Hercules’ club; of what colour Achilles’ beard was, or whether Hector were not troubled with the toothache. He hath studied himself half blear-eyed, to know the true symmetry of Caesar’s nose by a shoeing-horn: and this he did to gain the name of a speculative man.

PESCARA.

46Mark Prince Ferdinand,
47A very salamander lives in’s eye,
48To mock the eager violence of fire.

SILVIO.

That cardinal hath made more bad faces with his oppression than ever Michael Angelo made good ones: he lifts up’s nose, like a foul porpoise before a storm,―

PESCARA.

52The Lord Ferdinand laughs.

DELIO.

53Like a deadly cannon, that lightens ere it smokes.

PESCARA.

54These are your true pangs of death,
55The pangs of life, that struggle with great statesmen,―

DELIO.

In such a deformed silence, witches whisper their charms.

CARDINAL.

58Doth she make religion her riding hood
59To keep her from the sun and tempest?

FERDINAND.

To keep her from the sun and tempest? That:
60That damns her. Methinks her fault and beauty
61Blended together, show like leprosy,
62The whiter, the fouler. I make it a question
63Whether her beggarly brats were ever christened.

CARDINAL.

64I will instantly solicit the state of Ancona
65To have them banished.

FERDINAND.

To have them banished. You are for Loretto?
66I shall not be at your ceremony; fare you well:
67Write to the Duke of Malfi, my young nephew
68She had by her first husband, and acquaint him
69With's mother's honesty.

BOSOLA.

With's mother's honesty. I will.

FERDINAND.

With's mother's honesty. I will. Antonio!
70A slave, that only smelled of ink and counters,
71And nev’r in’s life looked like a gentleman,
72But in the audit time: go, go presently,
73Draw me out an hundred and fifty of our horse,
74And meet me at the fort-bridge.

Exeunt.

Scene iv

[Enter] two Pilgrims to the Shrine of Our Lady of Loretto.

1 PILGRIM.

1I have not seen a goodlier shrine than this,
2Yet I have visited many.

2 PILGRIM.

Yet I have visited many. The Cardinal of Aragon
3Is this day to resign his cardinal’s hat;
4His sister duchess likewise is arrived
5To pay her vow of pilgrimage. I expect
6A noble ceremony.

1 PILGRIM.

7No question.—They come.

Here the ceremony of the Cardinal's instalment in the habit of a soldier: performed in delivering up his cross, hat, robes, and ring at the shrine; and investing him with sword, helmet, shield, and spurs. Then Antonio, the Duchess, and their children, having presented themselves at the shrine, are (by a form of banishment in dumb-show expressed towards them by the Cardinal, and the state of Ancona) banished. During all which ceremony this ditty is sung to very solemn music, by divers churchmen; and then

Exeunt.

Arms and honours deck thy story, / To thy fame’s eternal glory, / Adverse fortune ever fly thee, / No disastrous fate come nigh thee. / I alone will sing thy praises, / Whom to honour virtue raises; / And thy study, that divine is, / Bent to martial discipline is: / Lay aside all those robes lie by thee, / Crown thy arts with arms: they’ll beautify thee. / O worthy of worthiest name, adorned in this manner, / Lead bravely thy forces on, under war’s warlike banner: / O mayst thou prove fortunate in all martial courses, / Guide thou still by skill, in arts and forces: / Victory attend thee nigh, whilst fame sings loud thy powers, / Triumphant conquest crown thy head, and blessings pour down showers.

1 PILGRIM.

8Here’s a strange turn of state: who would have thought
9So great a lady would have matched herself
10Unto so mean a person? Yet the Cardinal
11Bears himself much too cruel.

2 PILGRIM.

Bears himself much too cruel. They are banished.

1 PILGRIM.

12But I would ask, what power hath this state
13Of Ancona, to determine of a free prince?

2 PILGRIM.

14They are a free state sir, and her brother showed
15How that the Pope, forehearing of her looseness,
16Hath seized into th’ protection of the Church
17The dukedom which she held as dowager.

1 PILGRIM.

18But by what justice?

2 PILGRIM.

But by what justice? Sure I think by none,
19Only her brother’s instigation.

1 PILGRIM.

20What was it, with such violence he took
21Off from her finger?

2 PILGRIM.

Off from her finger? ’Twas her wedding-ring,
22Which he vowed shortly he would sacrifice
23To his revenge.

1 PILGRIM.

To his revenge. Alas Antonio!
24If that a man be thrust into a well,
25No matter who sets hand to’t, his own weight
26Will bring him sooner to th’ bottom. Come, let’s hence
27Fortune makes this conclusion general,
28All things do help th’unhappy man to fall .

Exeunt.

Scene v

[Enter] Antonio, Duchess, Children, Cariola, Servants.

DUCHESS.

1Banished Ancona?

ANTONIO.

Banished Ancona? Yes, you see what power
2Lightens in great men’s breath.

DUCHESS.

Lightens in great men’s breath. Is all our train
3Shrunk to this poor remainder?

ANTONIO.

Shrunk to this poor remainder? These poor men,
4Which have got little in your service, vow
5To take your fortune. But your wiser buntings,
6Now they are fledged, are gone.

DUCHESS.

Now they are fledged, are gone. They have done wisely;
7This puts me in mind of death: physicians thus,
8With their hands full of money, use to give o’er
9Their patients.

ANTONIO.

Their patients. Right the fashion of the world:
10From decayed fortunes every flatterer shrinks,
11Men cease to build where the foundation sinks.

DUCHESS.

12I had a very strange dream tonight.

ANTONIO.

I had a very strange dream tonight. What was’t?

DUCHESS.

13Methought I wore my coronet of state,
14And on a sudden all the diamonds
15Were changed to pearls.

ANTONIO.

Were changed to pearls. My interpretation
16Is, you’ll weep shortly; for to me, the pearls
17Do signify your tears.

DUCHESS.

Do signify your tears. The birds, that live i’th’ field
18On the wild benefit of nature, live
19Happier than we; for they may choose their mates,
20And carol their sweet pleasures to the spring.

[Enter Bosola with a letter which he presents to the Dutchess.]

BOSOLA.

21You are happily o’ertane.

DUCHESS.

You are happily o’ertane. From my brother?

BOSOLA.

22Yes, from the Lord Ferdinand; your brother,
23All love, and safety―

DUCHESS.

All love, and safety― Thou dost blanch mischief;
24Wouldst make it white. See, see; like to calm weather
25At sea before a tempest, false hearts speak fair
26To those they intend most mischief.
27[She reads] A Letter: Send Antonio to me; I want his head in a business.
28(A politic equivocation)
29He doth not want your counsel, but your head;
30That is, he cannot sleep till you be dead.
31And here’s another pitfall, that’s strewed o’er
32With roses: mark it, ’tis a cunning one:

I stand engaged for your husband for several debts at Naples: let not that trouble him, I had rather have his heart than his money.

36And I believe so too.

BOSOLA.

And I believe so too. What do you believe?

DUCHESS.

37That he so much distrusts my husband’s love,
38He will by no means believe his heart is with him
39Until he see it. The devil is not cunning enough
40To circumvent us in riddles.

BOSOLA.

41Will you reject that noble and free league
42Of amity and love which I present you?

DUCHESS.

43Their league is like that of some politic kings,
44Only to make themselves of strength and power
45To be our after-ruin: tell them so.

BOSOLA.

46And what from you?

ANTONIO.

And what from you? Thus tell him: I will not come.

BOSOLA.

47And what of this?

ANTONIO.

And what of this? My brothers have dispersed
48Bloodhounds abroad; which till I hear are muzzled,
49No truce, though hatched with ne’er such politic skill,
50Is safe, that hangs upon our enemies’ will.
51I’ll not come at them.

BOSOLA.

I’ll not come at them. This proclaims your breeding.
52Every small thing draws a base mind to fear;
53As the adamant draws iron: fare you well sir,
54You shall shortly hear from’s.

Exit.

DUCHESS.

You shall shortly hear from’s. I suspect some ambush:
55Therefore by all my love; I do conjure you
56To take your eldest son, and fly towards Milan;
57Let us not venture all this poor remainder
58In one unlucky bottom.

ANTONIO.

In one unlucky bottom. You counsel safely.
59Best of my life, farewell. Since we must part,
60Heaven hath a hand in’t: but no otherwise
61Than as some curious artist takes in sunder
62A clock, or watch, when it is out of frame,
63To bring’t in better order.

DUCHESS.

To bring’t in better order. I know not which is best,
64To see you dead, or part with you. Farewell boy,
65Thou art happy, that thou hast not understanding
66To know thy misery. For all our wit
67And reading brings us to a truer sense
68Of sorrow. In the eternal Church, sir,
69I do hope we shall not part thus.

ANTONIO.

I do hope we shall not part thus. O be of comfort,
70Make patience a noble fortitude:
71And think not how unkindly we are used.
72Man, like to cassia, is proved best, being bruised .

DUCHESS.

73Must I like to a slave-born Russian,
74Account it praise to suffer tyranny?
75And yet, O Heaven, thy heavy hand is in’t.
76I have seen my little boy oft scourge his top,
77And compared myself to’t: nought made me e’er go right,
78But Heaven’s scourge-stick.

ANTONIO.

But Heaven’s scourge-stick. Do not weep:
79Heaven fashioned us of nothing; and we strive
80To bring ourselves to nothing. Farewell Cariola,
81[To the Dutchess] If I do never see thee more, And thy sweet armful.
82Be a good mother to your little ones,
83And save them from the tiger: fare you well.

DUCHESS.

84Let me look upon you once more: for that speech
85Came from a dying father: your kiss is colder
86Than that I have seen an holy anchorite
87Give to a dead man’s skull.

ANTONIO.

88My heart is turned to a heavy lump of lead,
89With which I sound my danger: fare you well.

Exit [with elder son.]

DUCHESS.

90My laurel is all withered.

CARIOLA.

91Look, Madam, what a troop of armèd men
92Make toward us.

Enter Bosola with a guard [vizarded].

DUCHESS.

Make toward us. O, they are very welcome:
93When Fortune’s wheel is over-charged with princes,
94The weight makes it move swift. I would have my ruin
95Be sudden. I am your adventure, am I not?

BOSOLA.

96You are: you must see your husband no more,―

DUCHESS.

97What devil art thou, that counterfeits Heaven’s thunder?

BOSOLA.

98Is that terrible? I would have you tell me whether
99Is that note worse that frights the silly birds
100Out of the corn; or that which doth allure them
101To the nets? You have hearkened to the last too much.

DUCHESS.

102O misery! like to a rusty o’ercharged cannon,
103Shall I never fly in pieces? Come: to what prison?

BOSOLA.

104To none.

DUCHESS.

To none. Whither then?

BOSOLA.

To none. Whither then? To your palace.

DUCHESS.

105I have heard that Charon’s boat serves to convey
106All o’er the dismal lake, but brings none back again.

BOSOLA.

107Your brothers mean you safety and pity.

DUCHESS.

108Pity!
109With such a pity men preserve alive
110Pheasants and quails, when they are not fat enough
111To be eaten.

BOSOLA.

112These are your children?

DUCHESS.

These are your children? Yes.

BOSOLA.

These are your children? Yes. Can they prattle?

DUCHESS.

113No:
114But I intend, since they were born accursed;
115Curses shall be their first language.

BOSOLA.

Curses shall be their first language. Fie, madam!
116Forget this base, low fellow.

DUCHESS.

Forget this base, low fellow. Were I a man,
117I’ll’d beat that counterfeit face into thy other―

BOSOLA.

118One of no birth.

DUCHESS.

One of no birth. Say that he was born mean,
119Man is most happy, when’s own actions
120Be arguments and examples of his virtue.

BOSOLA.

121A barren, beggarly virtue.

DUCHESS.

122I prithee, who is greatest, can you tell?
123Sad tales befit my woe: I'll tell you one.
124A Salmon, as she swam unto the sea,
125Met with a Dog-fish; who encounters her
126With this rough language: ‘Why art thou so bold
127To mix thyself with our high state of floods
128Being no eminent courtier, but one
129That for the calmest and fresh time o’th’ year
130Dost live in shallow rivers, rank’st thyself
131With silly Smelts and Shrimps? And darest thou
132Pass by our Dog-ship without reverence?’
133‘O’, quoth the Salmon, ‘sister, be at peace:
134Thank Jupiter, we both have passed the Net,
135Our value never can be truly known,
136Till in the Fisher’s basket we be shown;
137I’th’ Market then my price may be the higher,
138Even when I am nearest to the Cook, and fire.
139So, to great men, the moral may be stretched.
140Men oft are valued high, when they’re most wretch’d .
141But come: whither you please. I am armed ’gainst misery:
142Bent to all sways of the oppressors’ will.
143There’s no deep valley, but near some great hill.

Ex[eunt.]
