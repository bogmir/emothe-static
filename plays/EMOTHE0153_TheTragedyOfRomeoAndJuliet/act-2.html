Act I

⸢Scene 1⸣

Enter Sampson and Gregory, with swords and bucklers, of the house of Capulet.

SAMPSON

Gregory, on my word we’ll not carry coals.

GREGORY

No, for then we should be colliers.

SAMPSON

I mean, an we be in choler, we’ll draw.

GREGORY

Ay, while you live, draw your neck out of collar.

SAMPSON

I strike quickly, being moved.

GREGORY

But thou art not quickly moved to strike.

SAMPSON

A dog of the house of Montague moves me.

GREGORY

To move is to stir, and to be valiant is to stand. Therefore if thou art moved thou runn’st away.

SAMPSON

A dog of that house shall move me to stand. I will take the wall of any man or maid of Montague’s.

GREGORY

That shows thee a weak slave, for the weakest goes to the wall.

SAMPSON

’Tis true, and therefore women, being the weaker vessels, are ever thrust to the wall. Therefore I will push Montague’s men from the wall and thrust his maids to the wall.

GREGORY

The quarrel is between our masters and us their men.

SAMPSON

’Tis all one. I will show myself a tyrant. When I have fought with the men, I will be civil with the maids; I will cut off their heads.

GREGORY

The heads of the maids?

SAMPSON

Ay, the heads of the maids, or their maidenheads. Take it in what sense thou wilt.

GREGORY

They must take it ⸢in⸣ sense that feel it.

SAMPSON

Me they shall feel while I am able to stand, and ’tis known I am a pretty piece of flesh.

GREGORY

’Tis well thou art not fish; if thou hadst, thou hadst been poor-john. Draw thy tool. Here comes of the house of Montagues.

MONTAGUE

54Speak, nephew, were you by when it began?

BENVOLIO

55Here were the servants of your adversary,
56And yours, close fighting ere I did approach.
57I drew to part them. In the instant came
58The fiery Tybalt with his sword prepared,
59Which, as he breathed defiance to my ears,
60He swung about his head and cut the winds,
61Who, nothing hurt withal, hissed him in scorn.
62While we were interchanging thrusts and blows
63Came more and more and fought on part and part,
64Till the Prince came, who parted either part.

LADY MONTAGUE

66Right glad I am he was not at this fray.

BENVOLIO

68Peered forth the golden window of the east,
69A troubled mind ⸢drove⸣ me to walk abroad,
70Where underneath the grove of sycamore
71That westward rooteth from this city side,
72So early walking did I see your son.
73Towards him I made, but he was ’ware of me
74And stole into the covert of the wood.
75I, measuring his affections by my own
76(Which then most sought where most might not be found,
77Being one too many by my weary self),
78Pursued my humor, not pursuing his,
79And gladly shunned who gladly fled from me.

MONTAGUE

80Many a morning hath he there been seen,
81With tears augmenting the fresh morning’s dew,
82Adding to clouds more clouds with his deep sighs.
83But all so soon as the all-cheering sun
84Should in the farthest east begin to draw
85The shady curtains from Aurora’s bed,
86Away from light steals home my heavy son
87And private in his chamber pens himself,
88Shuts up his windows, locks fair daylight out,
89And makes himself an artificial night.
90Black and portentous must this humor prove,
91Unless good counsel may the cause remove.

BENVOLIO

92My noble uncle, do you know the cause?

MONTAGUE

93I neither know it nor can learn of him.

BENVOLIO

94Have you importuned him by any means?

MONTAGUE

95Both by myself and many other friends.

97Is to himself—I will not say how true,
98But to himself so secret and so close,
99So far from sounding and discovery,
100As is the bud bit with an envious worm
101Ere he can spread his sweet leaves to the air
102Or dedicate his beauty to the same.
103Could we but learn from whence his sorrows grow,
104We would as willingly give cure as know.

BENVOLIO

106I’ll know his grievance or be much denied.

MONTAGUE

107I would thou wert so happy by thy stay
108To hear true shrift.—Come, madam, let’s away.

⸢Montague and Lady Montague⸣ exit.

BENVOLIO

109Good morrow, cousin.

ROMEO

Good morrow, cousin. Is the day so young?

BENVOLIO

110But new struck nine.

ROMEO

111Was that my father that went hence so fast?

BENVOLIO

112It was. What sadness lengthens Romeo’s hours?

ROMEO

113Not having that which, having, makes them short.

BENVOLIO

In love?

ROMEO

Out—

BENVOLIO

Of love?

ROMEO

BENVOLIO

115Alas that love, so gentle in his view,
116Should be so tyrannous and rough in proof!

ROMEO

118Should without eyes see pathways to his will!
119Where shall we dine?—O me! What fray was here?
120Yet tell me not, for I have heard it all.
121Here’s much to do with hate, but more with love.
122Why then, O brawling love, O loving hate,
123O anything of nothing first ⸢create!⸣
124O heavy lightness, serious vanity,
125Misshapen chaos of ⸢well-seeming⸣ forms,
126Feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire, sick health,

BENVOLIO

Dost thou not laugh? No, coz, I rather weep.

ROMEO

BENVOLIO

Good heart, at what? At thy good heart’s oppression.

ROMEO

131Why, such is love’s transgression.
132Griefs of mine own lie heavy in my breast,
133Which thou wilt propagate to have it pressed
134With more of thine. This love that thou hast shown
135Doth add more grief to too much of mine own.
136Love is a smoke made with the fume of sighs;
137Being purged, a fire sparkling in lovers’ eyes;
138Being vexed, a sea nourished with loving tears.

140A choking gall, and a preserving sweet.
141Farewell, my coz.

BENVOLIO

142An if you leave me so, you do me wrong.

ROMEO

143Tut, I have lost myself. I am not here.
144This is not Romeo. He’s some other where.

BENVOLIO

145Tell me in sadness, who is that you love?

ROMEO

146What, shall I groan and tell thee?

BENVOLIO

147Groan? Why, no. But sadly tell me who.

ROMEO

148A sick man in sadness makes his will—
149A word ill urged to one that is so ill.
150In sadness, cousin, I do love a woman.

BENVOLIO

151I aimed so near when I supposed you loved.

ROMEO

152A right good markman! And she’s fair I love.

BENVOLIO

153A right fair mark, fair coz, is soonest hit.

ROMEO

154Well in that hit you miss. She’ll not be hit
155With Cupid’s arrow. She hath Dian’s wit,
156And, in strong proof of chastity well armed,
157From love’s weak childish bow she lives uncharmed.
158She will not stay the siege of loving terms,
159Nor bide th’ encounter of assailing eyes,
160Nor ope her lap to saint-seducing gold.
161O, she is rich in beauty, only poor
162That, when she dies, with beauty dies her store.

BENVOLIO

163Then she hath sworn that she will still live chaste?

ROMEO

164She hath, and in that sparing ⸢makes⸣ huge waste;
165For beauty, starved with her severity,
166Cuts beauty off from all posterity.
167She is too fair, too wise, wisely too fair,
168To merit bliss by making me despair.
169She hath forsworn to love, and in that vow
170Do I live dead, that live to tell it now.

BENVOLIO

171Be ruled by me. Forget to think of her.

ROMEO

172O, teach me how I should forget to think!

BENVOLIO

173By giving liberty unto thine eyes.
174Examine other beauties.

ROMEO

Examine other beauties. ’Tis the way
175To call hers, exquisite, in question more.
176These happy masks that kiss fair ladies’ brows,
177Being black, puts us in mind they hide the fair.
178He that is strucken blind cannot forget
179The precious treasure of his eyesight lost.
180Show me a mistress that is passing fair;
181What doth her beauty serve but as a note
182Where I may read who passed that passing fair?
183Farewell. Thou canst not teach me to forget.

BENVOLIO

184I’ll pay that doctrine or else die in debt.

They exit.
